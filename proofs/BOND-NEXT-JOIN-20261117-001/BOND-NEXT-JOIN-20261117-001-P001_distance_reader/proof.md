# Reading the next joins from bond distances

## Standard statement

In every finite incidence system grown by the stated parity rule, the table giving the minimum cocircuit size minus one through each pair of bits determines all ordinary vertex stars on the given bits. Values above five may all be replaced by one symbol. The induced reading commutes with every renaming. In particular, it determines all legal next joins at every round. The finite incidence graph, open bits, and all terminology used in this statement are defined below.

## 1. What is supplied and what the reader must recover

The initial junction imposes even parity on three distinct fresh binary distinctions, called bits below. In a serial move choose one loose bit from each of one, two, or three distinct existing junctions. A loose bit occurs at just one junction. For two or more parents, every pair of parents must already have an ordinary common neighbor. For three parents, no ordinary neighbor may be common to all three. A neighbor is another junction sharing a bit. The new junction imposes even parity on all its chosen bits and three fresh ones. A consumed bit is now shared and cannot be used again. Each ordinary junction therefore has between three and six incident bits.

Call the set of bits at one junction its star. In the classical graph description the junctions are ordinary vertices, each shared bit is an edge between its two ordinary endpoints, and a loose bit can be drawn with a private leaf. For computing cuts only, wire all the private ends to one outside vertex. No growth rule ever treats that outside vertex as a junction.

The reader is given every current bit and the scalar bond distance for each distinct pair. For two bits this is the smallest size minus one of a finite K1–K3 projection on at least three bits containing them, with infinity when none exists. K1 means some joint row is excluded, K2 means that forgetting any member leaves all remaining combinations possible, and K3 means at least one joint row exists. The local parity law and its next-step policy are fixed. The reader is given neither the star list nor the old states, the loose bits, names with any special meaning, or the growth history. It must return stars on the actual input bits. Predicting the shape at a given size alone would not answer this question.

## 2. Which short projections are knots

The ordinary graph is simple and bipartite at every step. The seed has these properties. A new junction can share just one bit with any one parent. If there are several parents, each pair already has a common neighbor, so all parents lie in the same part of the current bipartition. Place the new junction in the opposite part. This also proves the absence of ordinary triangles on all legal serial histories.

For a set of ordinary junctions U, write ∂U for the bits with exactly one endpoint in U, counting a loose bit there once. Over the two-element field, summing the equations of U gives precisely the check on ∂U. All valid linear checks are sums of the junction equations, since their rows span the orthogonal complement of their common kernel. For a finite set F of observed bits, the annihilator of the projected states consists of exactly those valid checks supported on F, by extending a linear functional by zeros. Thus a constrained minimal projection is precisely the support of an inclusion-minimal nonzero check, and its projection consists of all even tuples on that support. The all-zero state supplies K3.

**Short sums.** Every nonempty U has at least three boundary bits, namely the three outputs of its last member. If U has at least three members, take the last three in any serial history that realizes it. They introduce nine distinct bits. Any of the nine that is used a second time inside U must go to a later one of these same three. At most two can do so, because the ordinary graph on three vertices is simple and has no triangle. At least seven of the nine belong to ∂U. The existence of a serial history proves the bound for a reader that receives only the table.

**Single junctions and neighboring pairs.** Deleting at most two ordinary vertices leaves every remaining ordinary vertex connected to the outside in the wired graph. Of its three outputs, at most two can go to the deleted vertices, since each later junction accepts at most one bit from it. Follow a remaining output. It either ends outside or reaches a strictly later vertex. Finiteness gives a path to the outside. In particular, whenever U is a singleton or an adjacent pair, both U and its complement are connected. For any cut contained in ∂U, its defining vertex indicator, extended by zero at the outside, cannot change off ∂U. It is consequently constant on each connected side. Its only choices are zero and the indicator of U. Therefore ∂U is an inclusion-minimal nonzero check and a K1–K3 knot. For two nonadjacent vertices the cut is the disjoint union of their two nonempty stars, so it is not minimal.

It follows that every bond of size at most six is either one star or the symmetric difference of two adjacent stars. If S and T are adjacent stars, their common bit occurs twice before cancellation, so the size of their joint cut is |S| + |T| − 2. The tests below treat that cut as a genuine knot, even though its members need not belong to the same junction.

## 3. The distance reader

For k = 3, 4, 5, 6 in order, carry out these steps.

1. Start a fresh graph on the input bits. Connect distinct bits a and b exactly when their bond distance is at most k − 1.
2. For every two distinct previously recovered stars S and T sharing a bit and satisfying |S| + |T| − 2 ≤ k, remove the links joining a member of S ∖ T to a member of T ∖ S. Retain pairs involving their common bit if the first step included them.
3. Read the maximal cliques of size at least three in what remains. These are all stars of size at most k. Carry their supports to the next threshold.

**Proof by induction.** Threshold membership means precisely that some bond of size at most k contains the two bits. Every real star of size at most k is such a bond. Any short two-star cut has both participating degrees smaller than k, since each degree is at least three and their sum minus two is at most k. Both stars are therefore available from the previous threshold. At the first threshold there is no such cut.

A cross-pair removed at step 2 cannot belong to any one ordinary star. Its two bits are incident to opposite vertices of the adjacent pair, excluding the shared bit. If the bits had a further ordinary endpoint in common, that endpoint would complete an ordinary triangle. Loose ends have no other ordinary endpoint. So the removal preserves every genuine same-star pair. Conversely, a pair contributed by a two-star cut either belongs to one of its two smaller stars or is precisely a removed cross-pair. By the short-sum classification there are no other cases. After step 2, two bits are linked exactly when they share an ordinary star of size at most k.

For completeness, take any two linked bits and their unique common ordinary endpoint v. In the graph using private leaves, any third bit linked to both must also meet v, since joining their other endpoints would form a triangle. Thus any clique of three or more lies in one qualifying star. Each such star is itself a maximal clique, since two distinct stars intersect in at most one bit. Step 3 recovers exactly the claimed supports. The induction proves the reader correct. At k = 6 every junction has been recovered. Only comparisons of bond distances with 2, 3, 4, and 5 were used.

## 4. Reading the next join, including all permitted choices

A bit occurring in one recovered star is loose. A bit occurring in two is shared. The overlaps recover ordinary neighborhood, hence every serial parent group and all its choices of a loose bit per parent. For the predecessor's complete-round policy, keep every eligible triple and every eligible pair belonging to none of those triples. At each junction compare the number of selected groups using it with its number of loose bits. Excess demand signals an unavailable round. A surplus gives that many singleton groups. If feasible, all injections of the available loose bits to their required uses, including the singleton uses, are the allowed allocations. Choices that differ only in names for new bits or identical singleton groups describe the same option. All inputs to that calculation have been recovered.

The theorem is uniform over every finite legal serial assembly, including all complete cubic rounds and their legal partial serial rearrangements. A feasible parallel step can be serialized with its allocated ports. Old common neighbors persist, and another new junction could create a common neighbor of an old eligible triple only by consuming from all three of those parents, duplicating that same triple. Distinct groups in the policy never do this. At a snapshot partway through a serialized round, the reader evaluates the policy afresh on the current presentation. After the first child of the seed has been added, two singleton additions remain in the earlier prescribed round. The fresh policy on that two-junction snapshot prescribes five singleton additions. Resuming a prior commitment would require that commitment as input.

Every operation of the reader commutes with any permutation of the old bits. If a bijection carries one pair table to another (even just the four threshold graphs), it carries the entire star family, loose bits, and possible next updates under the same bijection. A same-table/different-join pair within this class is therefore impossible. Under the stipulated all-even truth tables, imposing the equations on the recovered supports also gives back the allowed state relation. For each permitted update one may add its fresh parity equations, compute the next distance table and apply the same reader again.

The complete set of possible joins can contain several allocations on the old bits. Already after the seed gains three children, each outer junction has two distinct two-parent uses and one singleton use for its three loose bits. The 3! allocations at each of the three junctions give 216 choices on fixed old bits, all related by permutations of loose bits at their own junctions. The distance reader recovers the family without selecting one of those interchangeable choices.

## Scope

This result assumes the stated pure parity rule, three fresh bits, its parent conditions, and observation of all current bits including loose ones. It applies at each finite stage with no size limit. No infinite observation or uniform distribution on states is needed.

The cubic study's [attachment of the old eight](../../CUBIC-FROM-A-KNOT-20261117-001/CUBIC-FROM-A-KNOT-20261117-001-P001_local_growth/cube_interface.md) gives a direct test of the domain. All 28 old pairs belong to a three-bit knot in their preserved projection. They therefore form a clique of eight already at bond distance two in the full attached system. The first step of this reader would return a maximal clique of at least eight as a single junction. Its designated junctions have at most six bits. This disproves the reader's star characterization at the first threshold on the attachment. This control does not decide its final output after all four thresholds or whether some other reader recovers the mixed system. The [four-distinction collision for another law](../../../research/DISTANCE-INFO-20261117-001/distance_info.md) also bounds the general interpretation.
