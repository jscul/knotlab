# A loose child output distinguishes every input of the six-place site

Write \(H\) for the fourth three-input, three-output site. Its original inputs form \(I\), its two still loose outputs are \(a,b\), and its third output \(s\) goes to the spending child \(C\). That child takes two or three inputs and produces two outputs. Distinct ordinary sites meet at most once, and parent groups require distinct loose holders. As usual \(K=D+1\) is minimum bond cardinality through two distinct named bits. For cut counting only, wire every loose bit to the exterior and select the ordinary sites on the opposite shore. A bond is a support-minimal nonempty parity equation. A path or cycle below refers to this incidence representation.

## The strict reading persists

**Theorem.** Suppose one output \(f\) of \(C\) is still loose. Its other output can already have a child and any number of descendants. For every \(i\in I\),

\[
                 K(s,i)\le 6 < K(f,i).
\]

The sites and distinctions here describe the true source. An arbitrary table correspondence through \(D=5\) must preserve the displayed distinction between the two rows.

The star of \(H\) contains \(s,i\) and costs six. To bound \(K(f,i)\), write \(x\) for the old input holder on \(i\). There are three bit-disjoint ordinary paths between \(x\) and \(H\) at the birth of \(H\). Use \(i\) itself and the two paths to the other input holders through their respective earlier pair witnesses, followed by their inputs to \(H\). These witnesses are distinct since a common witness for the three parents would forbid \(H\). Every cut through \(i\) crosses each of these three paths. All these crossings already exist by the birth of \(H\) and are distinct from its outputs. [Input-path bound](../../VARIABLE-OUTPUT-READER-20261118-001J/VARIABLE-OUTPUT-READER-20261118-001J-P001_seven_to_six/structural.md#an-input-and-an-output-read-the-whole-arity)

Any ordinary shore with \(f\) crossing contains \(C\), since \(f\) is loose. If it contains \(H\), then \(a,b\) also cross. Take the latest selected site at or after \(C\). It exports at least two bits, distinct from \(a,b\) and the three earlier path crossings. The cut has size at least seven.

If the shore excludes \(H\), then \(s\) crosses. If even one site born after \(C\) is selected, the latest two selected sites at or after \(C\) export at least three bits. Together they produce at least four and can have at most one internal meeting. Their exports are disjoint from \(s\) and from all three earlier path crossings. This again costs at least seven. Thus any putative word of size at most six through \(f,i\) selects \(C\), omits \(H\), and selects nothing later than \(C\). Its same named cut was already present when \(C\) was born. At that point the terminal spending-child theorem gives the strict inequality to **every** true \(H\) input for either spending arity. Here is also a direct birth-time check. The six available crossings must be the three earlier path crossings, \(s\), and both outputs of \(C\). Every other input in \(Q\) of \(C\) therefore comes from inside. After erasing \(C\), the ordinary shore on the preceding sites has size \(3+|Q|\) and contains \(i\) and all of \(Q\) on its boundary, with \(H\) outside. If \(C\) is binary, before its birth the unspent \(H\) can be deferred until last. Since the shore omits \(H\), erasing it as well gives the same four-bit cut in the three-output-count prefix. It contains \(i\) and the single \(Q\)-bit as loose distinctions at distinct holders of opposite colors. All four-bit words in this prefix are ordinary stars, a property ensured by the legal triple \(I\) in the unary-, binary- and ternary-third cases. No such star contains both. If \(C\) is ternary, the five-bit cut after erasing \(C\) is a bond because a proper decomposition would require at least six bits. It contains the two \(Q\)-marks of the legal pre-spend triple \(\{s\}\cup Q\), and their third holder \(H\) is outside. The five-bond classification says its other three crossings are ordinary and none meets \(H\). But \(i\) meets \(H\) and is one of these crossings. This excludes the remaining case. ## Six loose bits determine all four high attachments

**Theorem.** Let a live one-spent source have six physically loose bits and at least one still loose output \(f\) at its spending child \(C\). Against every reachable rival, arbitrary whole-table isometry through \(D=5\) identifies every physical support and every named three-parent permission. There is no hypothesis on how many of the four high attachments apparently agree or where a proposed replacement is held.

The earlier live-source theorem settles the case with two six-place sites. With one such site it determines all small sites on their matched names and the two loose bits at \(H\). The only unspecified incidences are the four further high attachments, one per ordinary holder, in a set \(A'\) for the rival. This set overlaps the true \(A=I\cup\{s\}\). Any true attachment removed by a different choice becomes physically loose at its small holder. Since the width and all site arities agree, counting incidences gives the same number of loose bits in the rival. Its set of six loose bits is a bond, because the whole ordinary graph is connected. [Live-source low-site and overlap theorem](../../VARIABLE-OUTPUT-READER-20261118-001O/VARIABLE-OUTPUT-READER-20261118-001O-P001_one_spent_output/live_low_span.md#the-possible-four-attachments)

First \(f\) remains loose in the rival. If \(f\in A'\), then \(s\notin A'\), since the two named bits already share the same known site \(C\). By overlap some \(i\in I\) would belong to \(A'\). These two bits \(f,i\) would both meet the rival six-site, contradicting \(K(f,i)>6\) in the source.

Now all three inputs \(i\in I\) remain attached. Removing any one would make it loose in the rival alongside \(f\). The six-bit exterior bond would then put that pair through six, again contrary to the strict reading. Finally, removing \(s\) would leave \(s,f\) as loose siblings at \(C\). Their transposition would preserve every rival pair reading because every ordinary-site equation contains both or neither. In the source their readings to any \(i\in I\) are on opposite sides of six. Hence \(s\) remains attached as well. The low sites, the two high loose bits and these four high meetings comprise all incidence. Testing loose holders, pair witnesses and the absence of a common witness gives the entire transported menu.

In particular, a change at the same small holder of a consumed \(H\)-input and its spare cannot hide a live-mark change under these hypotheses. The earlier terminal example at width 31 exchanged such a sibling before any output of \(H\) was spent. It did preserve every exact pair reading and changed a physical support. Its entire continuation with one spent output has an empty menu.
