# Recovering high junctions from a sparse mixed source

**Theorem.** Suppose a finite reachable presentation \(H\) satisfies three conditions.

1. The seed is its only ternary junction.
2. Every four-place junction was added on a single parent.
3. Every bond of cardinality at most six has at most two junctions on its ordinary shore.

Take any other finite reachable presentation \(R\) under the full mixed rule. If a single renaming preserves the pair minima through six, it identifies every actual junction support. Thus it preserves the allowed states and all legal parent choices. This includes choices using high junctions themselves. In the observation, \(K(e,f)=D(e,f)+1\) is the minimum bond cardinality containing two different distinctions. Reading through six means knowing each exact value at most six and which pairs exceed six. The rival has no arity, fresh-count or shore restriction.

The hypotheses cover every all-three-fresh source. They also cover arbitrarily many two-fresh additions if each has three parents and any two such sites are at ordinary graph distance at least three. Here distance between sites is used solely to describe this sufficient condition on the classical incidence graph.

To verify the last assertion, consider a shore of three sites. Their total arity is at least \(3+4+4\), and there are at most two internal meetings, so its cut has size at least seven. In a larger shore take its four latest sites. If at most one made two outputs, they made at least eleven in total and at most four were used inside, by bipartiteness. If exactly two made two, they made ten, and using four internally would require a four-cycle. Its sites are at pairwise graph distance at most two. If three made two, the remaining site can meet at most one of them, and they are pairwise nonadjacent. If all four made two, none meet. In every case at least seven outputs leave the shore.

For a family with actual legal choices, take three unary children of the seed and make one binary join for each pair of children, using three fresh outputs per addition. A loose bit from each binary join is a legal triple: the three unary sites separately witness its three pairs, and none meets all three. Its three holders have arity five. One can cap this group with a three-parent, two-fresh join. On a remaining output of that site make a new unary, three-fresh junction, then repeat the three unary children and three pair joins on this new junction's outputs. The attachment uses only the incoming distinction of the new unary junction. Adjacent two-fresh caps in this sequence are at least four ordinary meetings apart. After any number of caps, leaving the next group uncapped gives a legal triple at three high holders. A three-fresh cap in a separate group can equally supply a six-place site.

## A pair that forces each source junction's equation

Put both presentations on the same named distinctions by the alleged renaming. The cut space \(C_H\) is the vector space of all even-parity equations of \(H\), and analogously for \(R\). A nonzero equation decomposes into disjoint bonds. The ordinary stars are bonds and form an independent basis of their cut space. A shared bit has two different ordinary endpoints; a loose bit can be given a private leaf when testing ordinary incidence. The exterior is used when computing cuts. [Graph and parity facts](../../VARIABLE-OUTPUT-READER-20261118-001D/VARIABLE-OUTPUT-READER-20261118-001D-P001_separator_inverse/separator.md)

All three- and four-bit equations agree, in both directions, by the short-equation theorem. Its hypothesis holds because the third assumption forbids a large four-bit shore. In particular both presentations have exactly the same single ternary star. In \(H\), a bond of size at most six is a star or a sum of adjacent stars. Indeed the only permitted shores have one or two sites. If two were nonadjacent their stars would be disjoint, and their sum would not be minimal. An adjacent pair of stars of arities \(d_1,d_2\) gives size \(d_1+d_2-2\). [Short-equation theorem](../../VARIABLE-OUTPUT-READER-20261118-001F/VARIABLE-OUTPUT-READER-20261118-001F-P001_finite_interval/structural.md#recovering-every-equation-through-size-four)

Fix a source star \(S\) at a site \(u\) of arity \(d=5\) or \(6\). Any two of its different outputs have \(K=d\). No smaller star can contain them. A two-site cut involving \(u\) costs at least \(d+1\), and a two-site bond avoiding \(u\) would have to use both child endpoints. Those children are distinct and of the same color, so are not adjacent. If an output is loose there is no child endpoint at all. We show that two outputs can be chosen so that no distinction outside \(S\) is within \(K\le d\) of both. The \(d\)-bit rival bond attaining the pair minimum must then be exactly \(S\). An output that is still loose can always be chosen, since no outside bit is that close to it.

At arity five, any outside bit within \(K\le5\) of an output meets its child. The only extra first-neighbor route in a two-site short bond would use the ternary seed and a four-place child of \(u\). Such a child is unary on \(u\); it does not meet the older seed. Thus any two of the outputs suffice, since a bit cannot simultaneously meet their same-color child endpoints.

At arity six \(u\) has three outputs. For an output ending at \(a\), let \(E_a\) list ordinary sites one of which must hold any close outside bit. The two-site size bound gives the following three possibilities.

- For a unary four-place child \(a\), take \(E_a\) to consist of \(a\) and its own unary four-place children. Indeed another site in a qualifying two-site bond with \(a\) has arity at most four. The only parent of \(a\) is \(u\), and \(a\) is not adjacent to the seed.
- For a five-place child \(b\) adjacent to the seed, take \(E_b=\{b,\text{seed}\}\). Only the seed can form a short two-site sum with \(b\).
- For any other child \(c\), take \(E_c=\{c\}\).

We need two of these sets that are disjoint and have no ordinary meeting between them. Two of the first type work. Their distinct children cannot coincide, and a unary child of one cannot meet the other, as each such site already has a different sole earlier parent. Two singletons of the last type also work, since all children of \(u\) have the same color.

A first-type \(a\) and a second-type \(b\) work as well. The site \(a\) avoids the seed. A unary child \(c\) of \(a\) cannot meet \(b\). If \(c\) came later, its only parent was \(a\). If \(b\) came later, it would have used both \(c\) and the seed as parents. Their first common neighbor had to exist when \(c\) was born, since a later first common neighbor would itself need an earlier witness for those parents. The sole earlier neighbor \(a\) of \(c\) does not meet the seed. This excludes the meeting even if \(b\) has three parents. The remaining pairs of sites across \(E_a,E_b\) are on the same color or were just excluded.

Finally at most two children of \(u\) can meet the seed. Before the first child that used both, the seed and \(u\) already had an earlier common neighbor consuming a different seed bit. The seed has three outputs in total. Thus if two children are of the second type, the third either gives the preceding first-type pair or is a singleton not meeting the seed, which works with either second-type child. Three second-type children are impossible. In all remaining cases the three children contain a pair of one of the types already treated. This finishes the forcing of all high source stars and proves \(C_H\subseteq C_R\).

## An extra equation would need too many close pairs

If the containment were strict, choose a shortest \(W\in C_R\setminus C_H\). Some generating rival star is absent from \(C_H\), so \(|W|\le6\). Equality of all shorter equations gives \(|W|=5\) or \(6\). Each rival equation of either size is a bond: a nonminimal one would require two disjoint ternary bonds, but there is only one. All pairs in \(W\) therefore have \(K\le |W|\) in \(H\).

For every source star \(S\), translation by \(S\) remains in \(C_R\setminus C_H\); minimal size gives \(|W\cap S|\le\lfloor |S|/2\rfloor\). If a six-place \(S\) met \(W\) in three bits, then \(W\mathbin{\triangle}S\) would also be a rival bond of size \(|W|\). A fixed bit of \(W\setminus S\) would be within \(K\le6\) of every member of \(S\), using the first bond on half the star and the second bond on the other half. The output pair proved above excludes such an outside bit. Thus every ordinary site holds at most two bits of \(W\), and the seed at most one.

If \(|W|=5\), discard its possible seed bit. At least four bits remain. Every pair among them must meet at an ordinary site. The only way a five-bit or smaller two-star bond could give a pair with disjoint endpoints would use a bit at the ternary seed. In a simple triangle-free graph at least three pairwise incident edges share an endpoint: for two edges meeting there, an edge meeting both elsewhere would close a triangle. A private leaf has degree one. This would put at least four bits at one ordinary site.

If \(|W|=6\), discard its possible seed bit to obtain a set \(Q\) of at least five bits. Any two of them meet or possess endpoints at two adjacent four-place sites. These are the only remaining cross-pairs of a short two-star sum. Every bit of \(Q\) has a four-place endpoint: otherwise its at most two ordinary endpoints could together account for only two other members of \(Q\). The ordinary graph on the four-place sites is a forest, because every such site was born unary and the latest site of a cycle would have two earlier neighbors.

For \(e\in Q\), take its set of four-place endpoints in that forest, including their connecting edge if there are two. Expand this small subtree along the incident forest edges by half an edge and call the closed result \(T_e\). Two of the resulting subtrees meet exactly when their endpoint sets meet or contain adjacent vertices. If \(T_e,T_f\) are disjoint, \(e,f\) must instead share an endpoint outside the forest. Neither bit can have another endpoint outside the forest, and this site already holds two bits of \(W\). Therefore every other \(T_g\) must meet both \(T_e\) and \(T_f\). These subtrees lie in one tree component. Their connecting path contains a common required ordinary vertex: in the unique interval between the original endpoint sets, whose distance is at least two, take the first vertex after leaving the first set. The half-edge expansions still require any connected subtree meeting both to contain that vertex. An ordinary vertex belongs to \(T_g\) only when it is a four-place endpoint of \(g\). At least three other bits would have this same endpoint.

If all \(T_e\) meet pairwise, they share one point. For example root the tree and choose the deepest of their respective nearest points to the root. On the path to any pairwise intersection, the nearer such point precedes the deeper one, which therefore lies in both subtrees. An ordinary point can belong to at most two of the subtrees, by the incidence bound on \(W\). An interior point of an edge can belong only to bits incident at one of its two endpoints, at most four bits in all. This again rules out five members of \(Q\). We conclude that \(C_R=C_H\).

## Recovering the actual junctions

Each rival star is now a source bond, hence either a source star or a sum of two adjacent ones with at most six bits left. Such a sum involving the seed contains two of the seed's bits and cannot itself be a different rival star, since the seed is a common actual site. The only other possibility is a sum of two adjacent four-place source stars. Both underlying stars would have to be missing as actual rival stars: the sum shares three bits with each of them. In the basis of actual source stars, an actual rival star therefore has either zero missing-site coordinates or exactly two. Their span cannot contain the unit vector of any missing source site. Equality of the two cut spaces precludes a missing site. This proves equality of the full ordinary incidence and of all next choices under the given renaming.

There is a direct reader. At each size from three through six, link the pairs with minimum at most that size. From these links remove the cross-pairs coming from two previously identified intersecting stars whose sum has size at most the current threshold. Such a cross-pair has a nonshared bit from each star. Its bits cannot meet one another at a third site, by ordinary bipartiteness. The remaining pairs are exactly the meetings at a single site of at most the current arity, by the short-shore condition. Their maximal cliques of size at least three identify those stars. The preceding proof shows that the same output is correct for the unrestricted rival. The within-law reader supplied the algorithm for the fixed-three-fresh case. [Within-law inverse](../../BOND-NEXT-JOIN-20261117-001/BOND-NEXT-JOIN-20261117-001-P001_distance_reader/proof.md)

One binary, two-fresh exception can already defeat the output-pair argument. Take a six-place site \(u\) with three loose outputs, as supplied by a three-parent, three-fresh cap on the first open group above. On two outputs of \(u\) make separate unary, three-fresh children \(p,q\). Join \(p,q\) at a binary, two-fresh site \(w\). Finally join \(u,w\) at a binary, three-fresh site \(z\), using the third output of \(u\). The required witnesses are \(u\), then \(p\) or \(q\). The outside bit \(wz\) is within \(K\le6\) of all three outputs of \(u\). The adjacent star sums at \(p,w\) and \(q,w\) give size six, and the star at \(z\) gives size five. The cut around each adjacent pair is a bond: its two selected sites are connected, and after deleting them every remaining ordinary component reaches the exterior through an output of its latest site, since the two adjacent deleted sites have opposite colors and cannot both receive that site's outputs. This example identifies the failed proof step for that exception; it supplies no opposing full-table pair.

## A binary exception whose two parents started at the same high site

**Corollary.** All junctions and legal choices are still determined through \(K=6\) if the sole two-fresh addition \(w\) takes two parents \(p,q\) that were born unary on the same site \(u\) of arity different from four. All other additions make three fresh distinctions. The seed is also permitted as \(u\). This covers the preceding construction and every continuation of it using three fresh outputs per addition. The rival is unrestricted.

No cut on at least three sites costs six or less: three sites have total arity at least eleven, with at most two internal meetings, and the four latest in a larger shore make at least eleven outputs with at most four internal meetings. The four-place sites still induce a forest. Before \(w\) is born, \(p,q\) are roots of different components of unary four-place sites, since their sole older neighbor \(u\) is outside that forest. A later unary site can attach to at most one existing component. The exceptional site joins the two components once, and subsequent unary four-place sites cannot create a cycle. The proofs excluding any extra short rival word and identifying actual incidence use only this forest, the short-cut bound, a single seed star and the existence of the forcing pairs.

It remains to check the pairs. For a high site other than \(u\), the old output argument applies. The exceptional \(w\) is not its child, and cannot meet a unary output child of that site. An older such child meeting \(w\) would have to be \(p\) or \(q\), both born on \(u\); a younger one has no earlier neighbor besides its own parent. The five-place argument also applies at \(u\), because \(w\) is not its child.

For a six-place \(u\), let \(a\) be the youngest of its three input parents. Its two pairs with the other parents had distinct witnesses at its birth. A later first witness is impossible, and a single witness for both pairs would have forbidden the join at \(u\). Thus \(a\) had at least two earlier parents and has arity at least five: it precedes the sole two-fresh site. A bit outside \(u\)'s star within \(K\le6\) of \(au\) must meet \(a\), or the seed if \(a\) is five-place and adjacent to the seed. A close outside bit for \(up\) must meet \(p\) or one of its four-place children. The sites \(a,p\) have the same color. None of \(p\)'s four-place children can meet the older \(a\): a unary child takes only \(p\), and \(w\) takes exactly \(p,q\). If the seed does meet \(a\), it has the color of these children. It neither equals any of them nor meets \(p\), whose only earlier neighbor was \(u\). Thus no outside bit is close to both \(au,up\). Their own minimum is six; they meet at \(u\), and their other endpoints are distinct and nonadjacent. This input/output pair forces the whole star, and the same holds with \(q\) in place of \(p\). All the needed equations now follow. In the explicit construction each of the three input parents is five-place and avoids the seed. Each yields the same two mixed pairs and one with the output to \(z\), whose only potential outside endpoint is \(z\). The three pairs between input bits are also forcing, giving twelve in total.

For comparison let the common site of \(p,q\) instead be a four-place unary \(t\) on the seed. Grow \(w\) on \(p,q\) with two outputs, another site \(b\) on \(p,q\) with three, and \(c\) on \(t,w\) with three. The four-place graph contains the cycle \(t,p,w,q,t\). The six bits \(tc,wc,pb,qb,tp,wq\) all have mutual \(K\le6\), either by a common holder or by an adjacent four-place pair. No ordinary site holds more than two of them. They do not form an equation: \(p,b\) have loose bits outside this set, so both would be on the exterior side of any proposed cut, although \(pb\) is selected. This pattern defeats the forest and incidence-count argument on its own. It does not give two presentations with opposing verdicts.

## An arbitrary high last junction on a two-fresh, low-arity prefix

**Separate theorem.** Start with the ternary seed and any finite prefix of unary or binary additions, each making two fresh distinctions. Make one further addition whose arity is five or six, with either allowed fresh count. Under every full-table isometry to any reachable mixed rival, the complete menu of three-parent choices agrees, including choices that use a fresh distinction at the last junction. Entries through \(K=6\) suffice. This conclusion concerns the permissions, even when the two last additions are given different named input groups.

Write \(F\) for the two or three fresh bits of the high last junction. Let \(L\) be the equation space of the low-arity prefix, understood on the enlarged name set with zero coefficients on \(F\). The full source has no four-bit bond with at least three sites on its ordinary shore. Such a shore would be parent-closed and could be constructed by restriction in the same chronological order. Its first two joins after the seed must be unary, immediately producing at least five loose bits. All prefix additions and any three-fresh final addition make at least as many distinctions as they take. Thus its final frontier could fall to four only if it contained the last addition on three parents with two fresh outputs. In that case the entire restricted history is all-two. With \(u\) unary additions and just one three-parent addition, the frontier would be \(3+u-1=4\), so \(u=2\). The prior exact closure excludes every all-two history on three or more sites with that frontier and at most three unary and two three-parent additions. Its states record all current loose counts, active meetings, and the sets of still available sites jointly witnessed by retired sites, so its closure applies at arbitrary depth. The short-equation theorem now puts the entire prefix space \(L\) in the rival cut space. No equation of size at most four contains a member of \(F\) on either side: in the source its only holder is high, and none of the possible ternary stars, four-place stars, or adjacent ternary sums contains it. For source site count \(j\), \(L\) has rank \(j-1\). The full width is \(3+2(j-1)+s\), with \(s=0\) or \(1\) according as the last site takes two or three fresh bits. Every rival has at most \(j\) sites, their stars being independent. At least one rival equation contains each fresh bit, since each has an ordinary holder. The rival therefore has exactly \(j\) sites and its cut space has just one coset outside \(L\). Since \(L\) vanishes on \(F\), *every* member of that coset contains *all* of \(F\).

Consequently the bits of \(F\) have exactly the same membership in every ordinary rival star. Such distinct bits can share only one ordinary holder: sharing two would give two meetings between the same sites. Thus all are physically loose at one site. It is not the seed, since a bit there would have a ternary equation. For \(|F|=2\) the common site count and width force every rival addition to have two fresh outputs. For \(|F|=3\) the three still-loose bits already exhaust the maximum possible number of fresh outputs. In either case that site has no ordinary children and can be moved to the end of a realizing order. Pairs that had used it as a witness already had an earlier witness. A previously lawful three-parent join at that site cannot have acquired another common witness in the meantime, since creating such a witness would already have been forbidden. [Deferring a site with all outputs loose](../../VARIABLE-OUTPUT-READER-20261118-001E/VARIABLE-OUTPUT-READER-20261118-001E-P001_marked_symmetry/terminal_cap.md)

Deleting that final rival junction gives a reachable prefix on exactly the old names. Its equation space is precisely \(L\): the other \(j-1\) independent stars all vanish on \(F\) and span the whole subspace with that property. The two old prefixes therefore have identical pair tables calculated on the old names. Since all source prefix stars have arity at most four, the one-sided low-arity theorem identifies the ordinary prefix sites with no relabeling of those names. We have reduced an arbitrary alleged isometry to two last junctions on the identical named prefix, with the same fresh set and fresh count \(h\). Their fresh row agrees through \(K=h+3\le6\). The common-prefix theorem now gives all three-parent permissions. The rank argument identified which bits are fresh under the alleged map, so no cut mark or earlier snapshot is an input to the reader.
