# Rank, three unused columns, and two families at all widths

Write \(D\) for bond distance and \(K=D+1\) for the minimum size of an inclusion-minimal nonempty parity equation containing the two entries. Let \(n\) be the number of ordinary sites. Each site contributes one independent equation. Its fresh bits have not occurred earlier, so its row is independent of the earlier rows. Every parity equation belongs to the span of the ordinary rows.

## The rank bound from distance four

A source under study has width \(w=2n+5\). A matching reachable rival with \(r\) sites and \(b\) additions producing three bits has \(w=2r+1+b\). The seed is its only three-place site, because the pairs at \(K=3\) identify all three-place ordinary sites in this class. The first two additions are unary and therefore have three outputs. Thus \(b\ge2\) and is even. If \(b=2\), the all-size minimum-width theorem applied with the *rival* as its source would identify every physical support in the four-addition source, already at distance four. The site counts would agree, contradicting \(r=n+1\). Thus \(b\ge4\) and \(r\le n\) in every rival. Forcing \(n\) independent equations or recovering a rank-\((n-1)\) subspace missing a used coordinate supplies a matching lower bound. ## A table certificate for recovering the final three outputs

Suppose that entries through distance five force a rank-\((n-1)\) subspace \(U\) of equations in every rival. Exactly three coordinates \(F\) are absent from \(U\), and every pair containing one of them reads above distance four. These are conditions testable on the table, for example by the short-equation procedure in the [31-distinction example](inverse_fixtures.md). Then the same observations determine every three-parent verdict at any finite size in this four-addition class.

Every coordinate appears in an actual site, so every coordinate in \(F\) occurs in an equation outside \(U\). The rank bound leaves only one nonzero coset of \(U\). Each word in that coset contains all of \(F\), since every word in \(U\) vanishes on all three. Any site holding one therefore holds all three. Two ordinary sites cannot meet on more than one bit. Thus there is exactly one such site and the three bits are loose there. An ordinary site is a bond, so its arity must be six. A smaller site would put one of its pairs at distance at most four. All its fresh outputs are the three loose bits, hence its other three bits were inputs. It has no ordinary children.

This site may be deferred to the end. Removing it cannot lose an essential pair witness for a later step. Any two parents to which it supplies a witness already had an earlier common witness when it was born. Removing a witness cannot create a new common witness. On reinsertion its inputs are still loose, and no later site can have become a common neighbor of all its parents: to meet all three already-existing parents, that site would have had to join them in the presence of the deferred site. This was forbidden. Thus erasure leaves lawful prefixes in every presentation. Their entire equation spaces equal \(U\), by rank and restriction to the old coordinates. The source prefix has exactly three three-output additions and a legal triple where the deferred site can grow. The three-branch theorem for a live prefix identifies all its actual supports against any rival sharing its full parity space. The common-prefix theorem now gives every subsequent three-parent verdict by a single matched fresh row through \(K=6\). The certificate supplied both the old physical prefix and its fresh group without labels or history. For the specific width-31 example, enumerating its two possible sites lowers the cutoff to four. ## Up to two shrinking joins with every site of arity at most five

At every finite size in this subfamily, distance four forces the full state relation and every three-parent verdict against every reachable mixed rival. A shrinking join takes three parents and introduces two bits. At most two such joins are allowed here. The third and fourth three-output births can each be unary or binary. Arbitrarily many two-parent, two-output steps are allowed.

A shore is the selected set of ordinary sites in a cut with every loose bit wired to one exterior. A shore of three or more sites starts with a boundary of at least seven on its first three sites. Their degree sum is at least eleven, and they have at most two internal meetings. A later site with \(p\) parents, \(h\) outputs and \(j\) selected parents changes the boundary by \(h-p+2(p-j)\). Only a shrinking site can save one. Thus every large boundary is at least five. All through-four equations are the seed and the four-place stars because a sum of two adjacent stars has size at least five. These equations are forced in every rival by the short-equation theorem. If every large boundary has size at least six, the short-shore transfer theorem likewise forces all five-place stars.

Consider a shore of size at least three whose boundary has exactly five bits. Its initial three sites have degrees 3, 4, 4, and both shrinking sites occur later with all parents inside. Every other later site has zero increment, so it has two parents inside and two outputs. No binary three-output site lies in this shore. There are at least five selected sites. The last three export at least four bits between them. If an input enters the shore, all four outgoing crossings must come from those last three, and the receiving site must be among the first three, since all later parents are inside. A directed path from the selected seed to the outside parent of the entering bit must leave the shore strictly before that receiver, hence from one of the first two sites. Neither is among the last three. This contradiction makes every such shore parent-closed.

Consequently every input of a shrinking site is internal to all these shores. Choose an input whose other holder differs from the seed. That holder is four-place or an earlier shrinking site, since it lies inside every such shore. In chronological order the protected-bit theorem forces both shrinking equations. Every output of a binary three-output site is also protected because the site lies outside every such shore and no input can enter one. Force the binary three-output sites in reverse order of birth. The other end of a protected output is loose, at a forced four-place or shrinking site, or at a later binary three-output site. These are exactly the protected-bit theorem's hypotheses. Thus every ordinary source equation is forced, and the rank bound gives equality of the complete equation spaces. Equal allowed states preserve every marked three-parent verdict. There is also a class allowing six-place sites. Suppose there are no shrinking joins, and every six-place site has a loose bit or meets three ordinary sites of arity at least five. Every shore on at least three sites has boundary at least seven. The short-shore theorem forces every site through size five. Its identifying-pair argument under either of the stated hypotheses forces each six-place equation through distance five. The rank bound again gives full state and three-parent recovery against arbitrary rivals. More generally one can replace those two conditions on each six-place site by an attaining size-six pair whose common closed neighborhood through \(K=6\) contains only its six bits.
