# A renaming can fail when every two junctions meet at most once

In complete-round cheapest-count parity growth there is a reached stage at which a permutation preserves every pair reading and fixes every distinction of every live junction, yet no extension preserves the next table. Here are two histories of different sizes. From six starting junctions the two relevant rounds are rounds two and three. From seven starting junctions they are rounds one and two. All starting supports project to their full parity knot, and every two junctions share at most one distinction at every stage.

## Why the complete table agrees

Use the classical graph representation with one vertex for each junction, and a single auxiliary outside vertex as the second holder of every loose distinction. The meet-once condition applies to the ordinary junctions of the arrangement. A bond is a cut whose two parts are connected. Its support is a minimal nonempty parity equation. Write K for the smallest size of a bond containing the indicated distinctions, and D = K − 1 for a pair. The absence of any such bond has its own reading.

Retain A and B, and remove C and D with their five incident distinctions to form the remainder Q. In the examples Q is connected, including the outside vertex. Let λ be the least number of Q distinctions separating A from B. Suppose λ ≥ 1. All of the following bounds are attained.

- K(AC,AD) = K(BC,BD) = min(4, 2 + λ). If A,B lie on the same side, crossing both named distinctions puts C,D together on the other side and crosses all four distinctions to the poles. The part consisting of C and D attains four. If A,B are separated, C,D must both be on the far side from the named pole. The two named distinctions and an A–B cut in Q must cross. A minimum A–B cut in Q has two connected parts. Placing C,D on that far side attains 2 + λ.
- K(AC,BD) = K(BC,AD) = 4. If A,B are together, all four cross. If A,B are apart, C,D are apart as well, so CD crosses in addition to the two named distinctions and at least one Q distinction. The same part {C,D} attains four.
- The pairs from a single three-place junction C or D have K = 3. Its own support achieves three. A cut crossing two such distinctions crosses either a third in the four-junction part or a distinction of Q. For instance, crossing AC and BC puts A and B together. D on their side forces CD across, and D on C's side forces AD and BD across. For AC and CD, A and D are together. Placing B with them forces BC across, and otherwise BD crosses. The remaining cases follow by exchanging the two poles or the two middle junctions.
- For any distinction z in Q, K(AC,z) = K(BC,z). If A,B are together then AC and BC cross together. If A,B are apart, put C and D each on the opposite side from its former side. The restrictions of both parts to Q are connected: each has exactly one pole through which its Q vertices can reach the four-junction part. C and D after the exchange are each adjacent to the pole on their own side. The number of crossings into C from the two poles is one on either assignment, and likewise at D. CD and every Q distinction keep their crossing status. This is a size-preserving correspondence in both directions, including when there is no common bond.

Thus if λ ≥ 2, all pair readings are preserved by exchanging AC and BC. At the marked round none of the five distinctions belongs to a live junction, and every other distinction is fixed.

In both histories at the marked stage there are two distinction-disjoint ways from A to the outside and two from B to the outside in Q. On A's side they go by different original branches and different pair junctions. On B's side they go by its two branches. These give two distinction-disjoint ways from A to B, so λ ≥ 2. In the successor the whole A side has just the single fresh distinction of the triple reaching the outside. This distinction separates A from B in Q, and the rest stays connected, so λ = 1. In particular {AC,AD,37}, or {AC,AD,23} in the seven-start history, is now a bond. The image pair BC,AD still needs four distinctions in a bond. These three old distinctions were all shared at the start. Every choice of fresh names leaves this contradiction in place. An enumeration of every bond independently measures all 666 and 1,128 pair readings for the six-start history and 253 and 435 for the seven-start history. ## All sizes with at most two live junctions at a reached stage

Assume the ordinary starts are connected and the simultaneous constraints project to exactly the displayed parity at each starting junction. A successful round uses every loose distinction exactly once. If a new junction introduces no fresh distinction, it took all the old loose distinctions by itself and the stage closes. In an open stage every new junction therefore has a fresh distinction, and only the new junctions are live. The open stage again meets the starting gates. Old solutions extend by choosing fresh distinctions for each new row independently. Each new row holds no proper nonempty equation: the only old equation supported entirely on loose distinctions in a connected arrangement is the sum of all rows. Indeed a sum can avoid all shared distinctions only by choosing both or neither holder at each ordinary meeting. For a new row a constraint on a proper subset could only come from an old equation on the distinctions it took, or by combining such an equation with that row. Its taken set is a proper subset of all loose distinctions in a nonterminal round. The projection is consequently its full parity relation. Addition keeps ordinary pairs meeting at most once, since each new group takes at most one distinction per parent and simultaneous new junctions share none. These facts justify taking the immediately preceding arrangement as an admissible start in an exhaustive search, at any positive age.

Here is the preservation fact used for the small-live bound. In the graph reading, replace a fixed distinction e between two junctions or a junction and the outside by a connected two-terminal network having minimum terminal cut one. Keep the identity on the distinctions of the replacement, including the old name e if it survives in the network. Then every old pair-table isometry fixing e extends provided all its other moved distinctions lie outside that replacement. For a bond containing an outside-network distinction, if the two terminals are together all network vertices must lie with them: any opposite network component would be disconnected from the opposite outside vertices. If the terminals are apart, any bond using both parts consists of a connected separating cut in the network and the corresponding connected cut on the old side. In the original graph the latter cut uses e. The least cost to cross the replacement is one, so the outside-pair minima keep their values. For an outside distinction x and an inside distinction y the new minimum is K(x,e) − 1 plus the minimum size of a connected separating network cut containing y. The formula includes the case of no such bond on either side. Pairs wholly in the replacement are fixed. This also proves preservation through any round consisting solely of one-parent additions: each replaces its own fixed loose distinction by a two-terminal network with one incoming distinction and parallel fresh distinctions to the outside. Distinct replacements can be made successively.

With one live junction no two-parent group can form. With two live junctions P,Q, the only possible multi-parent group is their pair. If it cannot form, the preceding preservation fact applies. If it does form, P and Q had shared a parent. In the round that produced P,Q each had lookahead demand one. Therefore each has one fresh distinction if it took at least two parents, and two if it took one. There are three cases.

- With one loose distinction each, their join takes both, is implied, and needs no fresh distinction. This simply replaces the outside vertex by the new junction. All old pair readings persist.
- With loose counts one and two, their join J has one fresh distinction, and the spare loose distinction r gives a one-parent junction U with two fresh. In the next graph take J as the old outside vertex for comparison. The whole change replaces r by a network consisting in series of r, the two parallel fresh distinctions of U, and the single fresh distinction of J. It has terminal cut one. Every pointwise-live permutation fixes r, so it extends.
- With two each, P,Q would both be one-parent additions at the same H. H would have held exactly the two former loose distinctions and been the sole live junction in its predecessor. If H has any shared distinction, the sum of all equations gives a proper two-distinction equation on H, in violation of its full parity projection. If the predecessor consists only of H, two distinctions fail the starting arity bound of three. Thus this third case does not occur for admissible reached stages. Even if the starting gate is relaxed to arbitrary parity rows, it cannot break a matching. All current distinctions on the two one-parent additions are fixed pointwise. They form the whole growing part, which meets the closed remainder only at H. A bond cannot cross both parts of a graph attached at a single junction, since the part of a cut away from that junction would then be disconnected. The next round acts wholly in the growing part, so a permutation of the remainder extends by fixing every distinction in the growing part, including the fresh ones.

Consequently **at least three live junctions are necessary for a failure** at any size. This statement concerns the full pointwise reading of the growing end in the question.

## A single starting junction is safe at every arity and age

From a single junction of m ≥ 3 distinctions the complete cheapest-count law fills the subset pattern on m names, level by level, and then closes. This description of the law was proved earlier in [FRESH-COUNT, Theorem 4](../../../research/FRESH-COUNT-20261118-001/fresh_count.md). To see it directly, index the current junctions at level r by the r-element subsets. Two such sites can join only if they share r−1 names. Any collection of at least three distinct r-subsets that pairwise share r−1 names either has a common (r−1)-subset or lies inside one (r+1)-subset. For the latter implication, fix two sets differing by one name each. A third that omits a name of their intersection must contain both differing names and be contained in their union. A further set containing the intersection and a name outside that union would have intersection of size only r−2 with this third. This proves the dichotomy. For r = 1 every pair is required. For r ≥ 2 the maximal groups lacking a common parent are exactly the complete collections of r-subsets of each (r+1)-subset. Each site therefore needs m−r loose distinctions. At a nonfinal birth these demands satisfy the law's arity floor. At the final site the one group takes all loose distinctions and the fresh count is zero. This proves the description at every age, including the end.

We show that the full pair table already determines the parity equation space at each positive open stage. In the classical graph representation use the m-dimensional cube of all subsets, where each adjacent pair differs by one name. At age r, the levels above r form a connected set. Contract that set to the outside vertex. The surviving distinctions are exactly the internal meetings and the loose distinctions of our stage. Each bond of the contraction lifts to a bond of the full cube with exactly the same size, because the contracted set was connected and stays on one side.

In the m-cube the least nonempty cut size is m, and for m ≥ 3 only the cut at a single vertex or its complement has size m. For completeness, split the cube into two (m−1)-cubes along one coordinate. If both halves are split by a cut, at least 2(m−1) distinctions cross inside the halves. If the halves are individually intact and are opposite, 2 to the power m−1 distinctions cross. If just one half is split, at least m−1 internal distinctions cross, plus at least one more for each vertex placed opposite the intact half. Induction beginning with the one-dimensional case proves the minimum. For m ≥ 3 the first two bounds are strictly greater than m, and the third permits equality only when a single vertex is opposite.

Consequently K(x,y) = m exactly when x,y share one of the m-place junctions, allowing also the outside when it represents just one remaining vertex. Every ordinary junction really gives such a bond: deleting its vertex from the cube leaves a connected graph, and contraction preserves that connectivity. If the outside represents several vertices, give every loose distinction a separate exterior endpoint for the purpose of recognizing this shared-junction relation. If it represents just one, retain that vertex. In either case the resulting ordinary incidence diagram is simple and has no triangle. Any set of at least three distinct connections that meet in pairs must therefore have a common junction: after two agree at a junction, a third missing it would close a triangle. Its maximal such sets are precisely the m-place junctions. At the last open stage they include the outside, whose parity equation is already the sum of the ordinary ones.

Thus every full-table permutation carries the equation space. When it fixes the growing end pointwise it fixes every loose distinction. Extending it by the identity on fresh distinctions preserves both the old equation space and each added row, hence the successor equation space and all its bond readings. **One starting junction of any allowed arity can never supply a counterexample, at any round.**

## How a new three-distinction bond can expose two old shared distinctions

Consider an open nonterminal round and two distinctions x,y that were already shared. The allowed states of the old distinctions are exactly the projection of the new states: the fresh distinctions of each new junction can satisfy its one equation independently. In particular, a two-distinction bond consisting only of x and y cannot first appear in this round. If their bond distance first falls from at least three to two, a witnessing bond of size three must be {x,y,z} with z fresh. A third old distinction gives the same equation before the round, with every proper subequation old as well.

Take the part of this bond away from the outside. Exactly one new junction lies in that part and it has exactly one fresh distinction z. Every new junction has fresh distinctions, each of which crosses to the outside if that junction lies in the part. Every old loose distinction on this part is taken by this one new junction, and all its parents lie on this part. Otherwise a previously loose distinction would cross as a fourth member of the bond. Thus the two old shared distinctions separate the old ordinary junctions in a cut with exactly those two crossings. A whole collection of loose distinctions on one side has just become a single fresh distinction. The six- and seven-start examples realize this event inside a simple meeting arrangement. This necessary condition concerns a decrease to two on an already-shared pair. Other distances and discrepancies first involving a fresh distinction require their own tests.

## Six is the least starting junction count for this five-distinction construction

Here the assumption is the specific interior just proved: C and D are exhausted, have exactly the displayed three distinctions each, and λ in the remainder falls from at least two to one during a positive-age step. There is no size bound on any other junction, and the number of earlier rounds is unrestricted.

A triangle of ordinary meetings can first appear only in the first round. After that, every possible parent is a newest live junction, and no two newest junctions meet one another. A new junction and two of its parents can then never form a triangle. All three distinctions of each middle junction C,D belong to ordinary triangles. Neither C nor D can have been born in a later round. A junction born in round one in an open history has a fresh distinction. It either still holds that distinction loose, or shares it with a junction born in round two, where it cannot belong to a triangle. Hence C and D are both present at the start.

A fall of λ to one requires that no ordinary A–B path in the remainder existed at the preceding stage. To prove this, consider the distinguishing cut of size one in the next remainder. A closed next round just replaces the outside by the one implied junction, so consider an open next round. If the sole crossing were an old distinction, all new junctions would be on the outside side: each has a fresh distinction. The other side would then already give the same cut of size one before the round, including any old loose distinction it contained. The sole crossing must therefore be fresh. An ordinary A–B path from an earlier stage would persist, and would have to cross on an old distinction. This is impossible.

Suppose the start had at most five junctions. If both poles A and B belonged to it and were live, their common neighbor C makes them candidates. Every candidate pair in a completed round belongs to a group, leaving a permanent ordinary A–B route in the remainder through its new junction. If either pole were exhausted at the start, it would require two distinct neighbors in the remainder for λ to reach two. An ordinary meeting of A with B already gives a permanent route. Besides the other pole and the two middle junctions there is at most one available neighbor.

If both poles were absent at the start, C and D would meet only each other there. A connected start would then consist just of C and D, which have no common neighbor to produce a child touching both. If exactly one pole, B, were absent, it would have to be born in the first round to meet C and D. Each has just one loose distinction to spend on B. If A were live, its initial triangle with C and D makes its pairs with both candidates, so these groups must all be the unique group containing C and D. B would therefore meet A. If A were exhausted, it would need two ordinary neighbors in the remainder. At most two further starting junctions are available, so every possible additional parent of B is a neighbor of A and gives a permanent route. Without such a parent, B is born from C and D alone. They have no other child, so B's lookahead demand is zero and it receives just one fresh distinction. Its degree in the remainder stays one. This exhausts starts of at most five junctions for this construction.

The six-start history attains the bound. Other configurations can only be excluded by their own structural arguments or the recorded complete census.

Ask whether the two can flip together while all other distinctions stay fixed. In a parity system this is equivalent to the two occurring together in every equation that contains either one. Flipping equal columns in the system of equations changes every row an even number of times. Here the answer is yes for 0,1 and no for 2,1. This reading uses the allowed states of the current arrangement. Their two-distinction marginal alone cannot supply it. A test restricted to the pair-distance table already failed.

If the reading must remain the minimum size of a bond through the marked distinctions, **three** are enough, and the failure of the full pair table makes three the least arity in that hierarchy. Distinction 15 belongs to the pointwise-fixed growing end. A current bond {0,1,15,16} attains size four, as does the analogous bond with {2,3} substituted. There is no bond through {2,1,15}. These queries classify every specified renaming in the example. The twelve fixed growing-end distinctions are 9 through 20. Reading a remaining distinction against them leaves the classes {0,1,2,3}, {4}, {5}, {6}, {7,8}. The last pair has identical holders and can always be exchanged. Every permutation of the first four preserves the old table, so there are 48 pointwise-live matchings in all. Within the four, exactly the eight permutations preserving the partition {{0,1},{2,3}} extend, including the exchanges within each pair and exchange of the two complete pairs. The latter preserves the span of the equations by adding the central equation to the respective holder equations. The other permutations take a same-pair reading of two in the successor to a cross-pair reading of three. Consequently 16 of the 48 extend, counting the optional exchange of 7 and 8. Either Boolean test above on {0,1} and its proposed image keeps precisely those sixteen.

There is no sufficient relabeling-invariant unary reading of the current *allowed states and pointwise growing end*. The equation-preserving swaps within the pairs and of the complete pairs fix every other distinction and are transitive on the four. All four therefore have the same value under any such unary reading. This proves minimal arity two for a new state-derived relation and minimal arity three within the original minimum-common-bond convention. For a supplied matching one yes/no comparison suffices. If instead the task is to reconstruct the unnamed partition of the four from scratch, there are three possible partitions. Testing which of three other distinctions goes with a given one takes at most two yes/no questions, and a single yes/no answer cannot distinguish the three possibilities. No general sufficiency of either reading is asserted for arbitrary histories.

## Minimum reached size by exhaustive calculation

The two examples above prove failure without any census. The numerical assertion that no smaller reached failure exists uses two exhaustive computations and the preceding size-independent lemma. Either one or two live junctions are analytically sufficient for extension. With k ≥ 3 new live junctions, all distinctions of the predecessor that were loose appear in fixed live supports. Thus only distinctions already shared between predecessor junctions can move. Given the predecessor's ordinary connected meeting graph and its set of live junctions, the complete law prescribes all multi-parent groups. Their incident demands plus any numbers of one-parent additions determine exactly the predecessor loose counts. Enumerating those possibilities with n + k bounded therefore covers every admissible positive-age stage in range. If only the current number W of distinctions is bounded, 3N ≤ 2W−k follows by summing the arities of its N junctions. At W ≤ 22 one needs only N ≤ 13. Distinct assignments on an ordinary graph representative can describe isomorphic arrangements.

The 11-junction sweep and the 22-distinction sweep finished with zero failed or undecided source tests. In the first, 153 initially oversized successor checks were completed by computing every needed row. Each positive subgroup comparison either proves that all generating permutations preserve the equations or checks that each actually extends to the next table, allowing every fresh renaming. A coarsened table can certify success only if its entire generally larger permutation group already preserves the equations. The generator and all relevant fresh assignments have no numerical isometry cap. The independent direct prefix agrees on all 640 assignments through eight junctions with k ≥ 3.
