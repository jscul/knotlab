# Which states a forbidding join can lose

**Standard statement.** Consider finite Boolean constraint systems with one or two occurrences per variable and initially at least three distinct variables per constraint. Under either of the two specified reductions in the number of private new variables, every stage of an even-parity or unsigned not-all-equal system has a solution. A stage can exclude old solutions and can lose a prescribed relation on one of its old supports. Independently choosing local readings after settling the stage gives the classical Bell bound for any one common nonnegative weighting of its solutions.

**Nearest known result.** The common-distribution Bell bound and extension of an event from an exact projection are classical and were proved for the predecessor's readings. Ordinary growth also extended *every* old state. The issue here is what happens to those states, the projections on individual supports and the Bell argument when the last private distinction can be withheld. The two-holder colouring argument for not-all-equal growth is adapted below to pinned words and to the two rival histories. ## 1. Exact one-round projection

Write \(W\) for the old allowed set. A funded ordinary proposal takes one loose old distinction from each of \(p\ge1\) distinct parents. The proposals spend different loose distinctions. Determine the ordinary count \(q\) from that same old presentation and its whole allowed set. Both rivals keep the ordinary zero at an already-implied birth. With \(h=\max(0,3-p)\), the first uses \(f=\min(q,\max(h,q-1))\) private fresh distinctions and the second uses \(f=\min(q,h)\). Fix the funded proposals before installing their constraints. Let \(Z\) denote the births actually receiving zero fresh distinctions, including redundant births.

**Projection lemma.** The exact set of retained old states is

\[
W'_{\rm old}=\{w\in W:\ w|_{I_b}\text{ satisfies the birth law for every }b\in Z\},
\]

where \(I_b\) is birth \(b\)'s set of old inputs. For parity, every surviving old state has \(2^{\sum_{b:f_b>0}(f_b-1)}\) completions. For not-all-equal, its number of completions is

\[
\prod_{b:f_b>0}\left(2^{f_b}-\mathbf 1\{w|_{I_b}\text{ is constant}\}\right).
\]

For parity with positive \(f_b\), one can choose every new bit except the last freely. For not-all-equal all fresh choices work when two old inputs already differ. When all old inputs agree, one constant fresh word fails. Different births have private fresh distinctions, so their counts multiply and their existential tests compose. Thus reducing a positive fresh count can change the state weighting and future funding while retaining every old state. A round loses old states exactly when it contains at least one zero-fresh birth whose rule was not implied by the preceding stage. Simultaneous losses need not be disjoint.

**Quantitative parity statement.** If the old homogeneous parity matrix is \(M\) and the zero-fresh constraint rows are \(J\), the exact fraction of old states retained is \(2^{-r}\) where \(r=\operatorname{rank}[M;J]-\operatorname{rank}M\). This follows by taking the dimension of the two solution spaces on the same old distinctions. On a connected holder presentation let \(z=|Z|\) and suppose there is at least one funded birth. Then \(r=z-1\) if *every* birth receives zero, and \(r=z\) otherwise. Indeed a sum of old rows supported only on loose distinctions must choose either both or neither of the holders of every shared distinction. In the connected case this leaves only the empty sum and the sum of all old rows, whose support is the complete loose set. The nonempty zero-fresh input sets are disjoint. A nonempty sum of their rows can equal that complete set exactly when they comprise all births, in which case the unique dependence uses every one. The general rank formula also covers disconnected presentations.

For either rival, a new restriction can only come from a birth with at least three parents. For \(p\le2\), a positive ordinary count already obeys \(q\ge3-p\), and the positive floor survives either reduction. The original low-arity zero exception is always an implication. Even one positive-weight ancestor with no lift makes literal weighting L undefined even when there remain many allowed states. Conditioning the starting weighting once on having a lift gives the separate comparison in the design.

## 2. Finite and infinite survival

**Parity.** The all-zero state satisfies every even-parity constraint, including all retained old ones. The argument applies at each funded round and to its union through any number of rounds. Old stars and descendant stars may nevertheless lose their full even-parity projections.

**Unsigned not-all-equal.** We need a little more than the existence of a state. Call some distinctions pinned when we prescribe their values. If every constraint that still needs satisfaction has at least two unpinned distinctions, every such pinning extends, provided each constraint originally has at least three distinct places, each distinction has at most two holders, and each constraint outside the test has either already been satisfied or is among those just listed.

Here is a proof using just the two-holder property. Ignore constraints whose pinned bits already include both values. At each remaining constraint select a pair of its unpinned distinctions and provisionally require them to have opposite values. Regard each selected pair as a link between its two distinctions in an auxiliary graph. Every distinction occurs in at most two selected pairs. The components are isolated distinctions, paths and cycles, including possible parallel pairs. Alternating values satisfies the selected pairs on a path or an even cycle.

If an odd cycle contains a constraint with a third unpinned distinction, replace one end of that constraint's selected pair by that third distinction. The third one had at most one selected occurrence since it was unused at this constraint. It therefore belonged to a different path or was isolated. This replacement opens the odd cycle and joins it to that path, creating no new cycle. Repeat until every remaining odd cycle uses only constraints with two unpinned places. Each of these constraints has a pinned distinction because its original arity was at least three. At one constraint on the cycle drop the selected pair and alternate along the remaining even-length path. Its two end values agree. Choose them opposite to one pinned value there, satisfying the dropped constraint too. All other constraints still see their selected opposite pair. Fill unused unpinned distinctions arbitrarily. This proves the pinning assertion, including the unpinned case.

Every rival stage on an admissible not-all-equal start satisfies those hypotheses, by induction. A birth with at least one fresh distinction keeps the minimum arity by the formula for \(f\). For an ordinary implication on the \(p\) proposed loose inputs, prescribe zero on all of them. Each old holder loses at most one unpinned place, since there is one distinct loose input per parent. The pinning argument produces a complete old state with all these inputs zero. Consequently **no nonempty such input set implies not-all-equal**, and the inherited low-arity zero exception cannot arise on this branch. A zero-fresh rival birth has at least three old inputs. The whole round retains the two-holder property because only loose distinctions acquire a second occurrence and every new distinction has its own holder. The unpinned argument supplies a global state after installation. It also follows that *every* zero-fresh not-all-equal birth really forbids old states. Complementing the witness with all inputs zero gives a second excluded old state.

The hypotheses matter. Three unsigned two-place not-all-equal constraints requiring three pairwise unequal bits have no solution. At arity three, if one permits three holders per distinction, the seven triples \(\{i,i+1,i+3\}\) on indices modulo seven are also unsatisfiable. Each pair of indices occurs once. A satisfying colouring would put exactly one equal-valued pair in each triple, hence seven over the whole system. Dividing seven indices into two classes leaves at least \(\binom32+\binom42=9\) equal pairs. These are boundary controls outside the funded start class.

**Infinite histories.** A stall creates no further constraints, while a closed or stalled finite allowed set can persist. On any countably infinite sequence of completed finite rounds, each stage has finitely many states. Join each later state to its unique preceding restriction and add one root at the beginning. There are nodes at every level. Inductively a node admitting arbitrarily deep extensions has a child with the same property, since it has only finitely many children. A resulting chain assigns every distinction of the union consistently. If a prescribed finite word occurs at every later stage, apply the same argument to the states realizing that word. This gives existence and, where applicable, projected support in the limit. It assigns no weighting or label of finite-change nativeness at infinity.

## 3. When a displayed knot remains lawful

**Not-all-equal.** Fix one displayed support \(H\) and any nonconstant word on it. Drop its already-satisfied constraint and pin the whole word. If each other holder retains at least two places outside \(H\), the pinning proof extends the word. In particular if two holders of the initial presentation share at most one distinction, every displayed support keeps its full not-all-equal law forever. Births only meet a given earlier holder once, different births in the same round have disjoint supports, and subsequent births keep that property. Every **newborn** therefore retains its full projected not-all-equal law at all future stages even if the initial presentation has multiple sharing. These finite words also extend through any infinite history by §2. Nonemptiness of the common relation alone would give no such projection guarantee.

Where two old holders share several distinctions, there is an exact word test. Drop \(H\)'s satisfied rule and fix its word. For each remaining holder \(j\) with \(r_j\) unfixed places, choose a common subset \(F\) of all unfixed distinctions to carry one. The word extends exactly when the following inequalities have a simultaneous solution:

\[
\mathbf 1\{j\text{ has no fixed one}\}
\;\le\;|F\cap j|\;\le\;
r_j-\mathbf 1\{j\text{ has no fixed zero}\}.
\]

They state precisely the missing-one and missing-zero conditions on each holder. The two-remaining-places condition avoids a separate feasibility test for each word in the named class.

**Parity.** On any displayed support \(H\), the allowable words satisfy precisely the old row combinations whose support lies within \(H\). Indeed an assignment on \(H\) extends iff the resulting linear system for the other distinctions is consistent. A failed elimination gives a sum of equations cancelling every outside distinction and disagreeing with the proposed word. Thus a displayed parity rule retains its full law iff the space of row combinations supported on \(H\) is exactly the one-dimensional span of its own parity row. Any extra nonzero proper-support requirement witnesses failure of K2.

There is also a direct structural check. Remove holder \(H\) and take the connected components of other holders through shared distinctions. For each component with no loose distinction of its own, require even parity of the fixed \(H\) bits that it meets. Together with \(H\)'s displayed parity these are all the requirements. In a component, any vanishing sum on the remaining internal distinctions must include all holders or none. The all-holder sum survives cancellation only if there is no free loose distinction there. This is the same elimination criterion and allows repeated meetings. Under parity even a source with only single meetings can lose a displayed knot, as the next example shows.

## 4. The smallest rounds that distinguish the laws

Use three old holders \(A,B,C\), each pair sharing its own distinction. Give them separate loose bits \(a,b,c\). Case 0 has no extra loose bits. Case 1 adds one more at \(A\). Case 2 adds one at \(A\) and one at \(B\). Allocate \(a,b,c\) to the common proposed birth. Each pair of holders is jointly met by the third, and no holder meets all three. Hence there is one ternary group, with one separate unary birth for each extra loose bit. These unary siblings through different parents share no parent. Their only proposed next groups involving the ternary child are its individual pairs with them. Its demand \(D\) is consequently 0, 1 and 2 in the three cases. The initial supports have their full prescribed laws, as can also be checked by the criteria in §3.

| Old presentation | Law and ordinary ternary count \(q\) | One fewer, old states retained | Only the floor, old states retained |
|---|---|---:|---:|
| 0 extra, 6 distinctions | Parity, 0 | 8/8 | 8/8 |
| 0 extra, 6 distinctions | Not-all-equal, 1 | 18/26 | 18/26 |
| 1 extra, 7 distinctions | Parity, 1 | 8/16 | 8/16 |
| 1 extra, 7 distinctions | Not-all-equal, 1 | 44/62 | 44/62 |
| 2 extra, 8 distinctions | Parity, 2 | 32/32 | 16/32 |
| 2 extra, 8 distinctions | Not-all-equal, 2 | 146/146 | 106/146 |

The no-extra parity birth is ordinary in disguise because adding the three old equations already gives its rule. In Case 1 adding the old equations and the new zero-fresh parity equation forces \(A\)'s extra loose bit to zero. The old support at \(A\) and the unary newborn containing that bit both lose K2 despite the wholly single-meeting source. In Case 2 the one-fewer rule retains one private bit at the ternary birth and every old state extends. The arity-floor rule withholds that bit and forbids old states. Having no future spare, having fewer completions and actually forbidding a previous state are different tests.

These first-round sizes are sharp across lawful starts. A new exclusion needs at least three parents. With just three old holders, pairwise candidacy among all three requires the three distinct pair meetings, since the third holder must witness each pair. At least three loose distinctions are needed too, giving six total. This is attained for not-all-equal. With exactly six, parity's only possible ternary birth exhausts the loose distinctions and is already implied, so seven are necessary and attained. With four or more old holders, minimum arity three and at least three loose distinctions give at least eight distinctions, since \(2n=\sum_H |H|+\#\text{loose}\). Finally, for the *two rivals' surviving old sets to differ*, an additional arity-floor restriction must be absent under the one-fewer rule. This needs a birth with \(p\ge3\) and \(q\ge2\), hence demand at least two. Its two distinct sibling births consume at least two additional loose inputs. Five loose inputs plus the three pair meetings give eight with three old holders. Four or more holders require at least nine by the same count. Case 2 attains the eight-distinction minimum under each law.

## 5. A lawful multiple-meeting start can lose an old not-all-equal knot

This post-freeze sharpness example is separate from the sampled starts. Take five unsigned supports

\[
H=(h,a,b,c,d,e,f),\quad U=(a,g,u),\quad V=(b,i,v),\quad W=(c,d,g),\quad Z=(e,f,i).
\]

For either value of \(g\) or \(i\), the other branches have available completions. More explicitly, an arbitrary nonconstant word of \(H\) extends by choosing \(g,i\) to satisfy \(W,Z\), then taking \(u,v\) to satisfy \(U,V\). An arbitrary allowed word at \(U\) extends by choosing \(c,d\) opposite, so \(H,W\) are satisfied, and then completing \(Z,V\). The same argument treats \(V\). For any allowed word at \(W\), choose \(a\) opposite \(g\) and \(h\) opposite \(c\), satisfying \(U,H\), and complete the other branch. Interchanging the two branches treats \(Z\). Thus every source projection is fully lawful.

The only loose distinctions are \(h,u,v\). The three pair witnesses for parents \(H,U,V\) are \(W,Z,H\), respectively for \((H,U),(H,V),(U,V)\). No single holder meets all three. The unique group has \(p=3,D=0,q=1\) and both rivals use zero fresh. The old word \((h,a,b,c,d,e,f)=(0,1,1,0,0,0,0)\) forces \(g=i=1\) and \(u=v=0\), so the new constraint forbids it. Its complement goes too. Exactly 480 of 640 old states survive and \(H\) now allows 124 of its original 126 words. The resulting relation still passes K1–K3. It has lost its original not-all-equal law.

Add a further loose distinction \(z\) to \(H\), assigning \(h\) to the ternary group and \(z\) to its unary sibling. Now the ternary birth has \(D=1,q=1\), still zero fresh under both rivals. The unary birth receives two fresh distinctions. The same two old words are lost for either value of \(z\). Thus forgetting \(z\) on \(H\) still leaves a forbidden word, and its actual eight-place projection fails K2. The checker gives 1,288 old states, 966 retained old states and 2,898 complete rival states. The old eight-place support retains 250 of 254 nonconstant words. All four other original supports and both new ones have their complete ternary not-all-equal laws. Ordinary growth on the same funded proposal retains all 1,288 old states and has 6,762 complete states. This example distinguishes the original law, the knot test on the actual projection, and global survival.

## 6. External settings after deletion

Fix any nonempty surviving stage, one accepted presentation and two local readings. Each complete state \(w\) specifies its four prospective signs \(A_0(w),A_1(w),B_0(w),B_1(w)\), including when a sign asks about both marks at that observer. Choose external settings \(x,y\) independently of the preparation. Under a common nonnegative normalized weighting, the four-answer distribution on those quadruples gives each setting table as its corresponding marginal. For a single state,

\[
A_0(B_0+B_1)+A_1(B_0-B_1)=\pm2.
\]

Either \(B_0=B_1\) or \(B_0=-B_1\). Averaging gives the bound 2. Any odd-minus orientation of the four products has the same pointwise bound because their unsigned product is +1. The full register and hence the selected answer at either side have marginals independent of the remote setting. Any one setting-independent deletion of old states just changes the common weighting. This applies to U, T, literal L where it exists, and L conditioned on one common survival event. A nonexistent observer or an empty support supplies no score. A fixed common probability law on an infinite surviving structure would satisfy the same finite-read proof.

For any finite collection of such contexts, every surviving complete state satisfies their exact projected supports together. Every individually supported local event extends to a complete state by the definition of projection. Individually choosing several events can still give an incompatible selection. For example, under either law three outer ternary knots on \((i,p_i,q_i)\), for \(i=a,b,c\), can each show \((1,1,0)\) while a central knot on \((a,b,c)\) forbids all three outer events together. Even any two of those events extend. By contrast the GHZ four parity requirements and the six Mermin–Peres requirements put every sign in two contexts but require an odd total number of negative products. They have zero common assignments, so cannot be exact projections of a globally surviving stage. Individually possible contexts in a collectively empty conjunction also have no common experiment on survivors. Mere agreement of distributions on overlaps does not imply the existence of a common weighting, as the predecessor's positive-mass counterexample shows.

**Least added access to settings.** Begin with two equal fair outputs \(R,R\). In just context 11 let Bob flip his output with independent probability \(t\), which requires access to both settings. All trials are kept. With uniform independent settings, each pair still occurs with probability \(1/4\), Alice and Bob each expose a fair bit in every cell, and the correlators are \((1,1,1,1-2t)\). This gives \(S=2+2t\), attaining the maximum change from altering a sign on mass \(t\) in one setting cell. At \(t=0\) the original setting-blind rule remains. A common deletion independent of settings leaves its score at 2 regardless of how it biases \(R\). With \(P(R=1)=p\), the added joint flip changes Bob's probability of one at fixed \(y=1\) by \(t(1-2p)\) as Alice's setting changes from 0 to 1. If Bob also receives the original mask alongside his changed output, their difference reveals the flip even when the selected bit stays fair. Zero altered setting rules cannot exceed the bound. This minimality is in number of altered context rules relative to the stated equal-output control.

At \(t=1\) the value 4 exceeds the quantum bound. A wholly quantum-compatible comparison with all trials and hidden remote settings in both *entire output pairs* uses a different baseline. For independent fair bits \(r,s,m\), Alice's pair is \((r,s)\). Bob's pair is \((r,1-s)\) for \(m=0\) and \((s,r)\) otherwise. Only at 00 use the former pair regardless of \(m\). Both pair distributions are uniform everywhere. Selecting the locally indexed bit gives \((1,1/2,1/2,-1/2)\), hence \(S=5/2\). On \(\lvert\Phi^+\rangle\), take \(A_0=B_0=Z\), \(A_1=(Z+\sqrt3X)/2\) and \(B_1=(Z-\sqrt3X)/2\). The two matching Pauli correlations are 1, the mixed ones vanish and single outcomes are fair, giving the same full tables. The mathematical resource in the comparison is access to the externally chosen pair 00. Neither rival growth formula uses either setting.
