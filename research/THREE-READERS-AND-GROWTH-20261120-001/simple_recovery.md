# Recovery when two holders meet at most once

Under the ordinary finite not-all-equal law, suppose each distinction has one or two holders, each holder has at least three distinctions, and the projection on each holder is its full not-all-equal relation. Keep the named distinctions fixed and require each rule to be essential. Throughout this note *single meetings* means that any two different holders share at most one distinction. Several loose distinctions at one holder are allowed.

**Claim.** If a connected presentation has single meetings, every other lawful irredundant presentation of the same states has exactly its holders, except for the closed example with four mutually meeting holders on six distinctions. The competing presentation may have repeated meetings. Both alternatives in the exception make no birth. Ordinary growth preserves single meetings, hence each reached stage in this class can also be read from its own states, subject to the same closed exception. The growth law is supplied.

## 1. Which two distinctions occur together

For two names x and y, seek an assignment of all other names for which precisely 01, 10 and 11 are allowed on (x,y). When a word fails at 00 and both single changes repair it, every violated holder must contain both names. Thus every observed pair has a common holder in every presentation.

In a presentation with single meetings, this test finds every pair held together. At their holder H, fix all places other than x and y to zero. Remove H temporarily. Give each loose distinction in the remaining structure a distinct auxiliary endpoint for the colouring argument. A neighbour of H retains at least two distinctions after its single shared place is removed. An ordinary holder that did not meet H retains at least three. Give the remaining distinctions two values so every holder sees both wherever possible. The elementary alternating-traversal argument supplies this assignment in any connected component except a closed odd sequence all of whose holders have two places. A component with odd-degree holders can be made even by adding a temporary holder with one distinction to each odd holder. Start a traversal at the temporary holder and alternate values. At a genuine old holder of odd degree at least three, an opposite-valued pair survives deletion of the temporary distinction. If all degrees were even, start at a holder of degree at least four if one exists, which also leaves it an opposite-valued pair. An even two-place sequence alternates. [Earlier proof and colouring source](../READS-HOLDERS-SOURCES-20261120-001B/recovery.md)

In an exceptional odd component, every holder has lost a place from H. It has at least three different holders, since there were single meetings. At most two of them held x or y. Choose one of the others and alternate so that it sees two ones. Its place in H is pinned zero, so it also satisfies its own rule. Each other holder of this component sees both values away from H. Now 01, 10 and 11 all satisfy H with the identical exterior, while 00 violates it. This proves the pair test.

## 2. When one common holder suffices

Suppose one physical holder H occurs in both presentations. For any shared x of H in the original, let G be its other holder. Every y in G except x lies outside H and passed the pair test with x. In the competitor all those y must lie in the single remaining holder of x. That holder contains G. A larger holder cannot have the full local law, because that law would allow G to be constant inside a mixed word. Thus G itself is shared by the two presentations. Connectedness propagates the conclusion to every original holder. Any additional rule would be implied, so the competitor has exactly the same holders.

## 3. Both presentations have single meetings

Describe a name by its one or two holders. Any collection of such descriptions in which every pair meets either has one common holder or uses the three different pairs among precisely three holders. To see this, a singleton forces a common holder. Otherwise, two different pairs meeting at a holder require a pair avoiding it to use their two other holders. Every subsequent pair must then be one of these three or miss a previous pair. Single meetings exclude repeated pairs.

An original holder of arity at least four therefore forces a common holder in the competitor. If all original holders have arity three and a name is loose, it has exactly two other names paired with it. A shared name in any single-meeting presentation has at least four. Both single-meeting presentations give exactly the same pair test, so the name must be loose in the competitor and its two partners recover its holder.

It remains to consider all-shared original ternaries. No four names pass the pair test with each other, so the competitor is also ternary. Every name has four partners, so it is shared in the competitor too. If there is no common holder, each alternative ternary consists of the three original meeting names of three mutually meeting holders. Each original meeting must participate in two different such triples because its name has two alternative holders. Therefore the two original holders at that meeting have both other neighbours in common. The same holds on each ensuing meeting. This forces exactly four mutually meeting holders, with no further place on any of them. The alternative holdings are the four triples of meetings that avoid each of the original holders. The six-name census checks equality of the 18 states and exhausts every lawful irredundant presentation of them. [Earlier named witness](../READS-HOLDERS-SOURCES-20261120-001B/census.md)

## 4. A competitor has a repeated meeting

Induct on the number of names. Distinct names e and f shared by the same two holders of the competitor can be transposed without changing its states. The pair test of section 1 is computed from those states, so transposing only e and f preserves the original named pairs.

In the single-meeting original, if e and f share a holder, both must be loose there. Indeed, if e also had another holder, that holder would have at least two other distinctions. Neither lies at the common holder, and at most one can meet the other holder of f, which contradicts the transposition. If e and f have disjoint holders, each other distinction at each of their holders must also touch one of the opposite name's holders. A loose name would provide only one opposite holder, which cannot supply the two required distinct meetings. Thus the four holders are genuine and every one meets both opposite holders. All four have degree exactly three and all places are already shared. This is precisely the six-name exception covered above.

In the loose-sibling case, fix e to zero and f to one. This discharges their single original holder H and the two competitor holders of their repeated meeting. Every remaining rule has exactly its original places. Each remaining rule retains its full projection and essentiality, because e and f occur in no remaining rule. Discard the names now free in this restricted relation. They agree on both readings: every name in a remaining physical holder has a legal state in which it is that holder's single one, and flipping it violates the rule.

The remaining connected components also agree as sets of names. Each physical component contributes a separate factor to the allowed states. Conversely, no product factorization can cut through a physical holder, since its full not-all-equal projection restricts the whole holder and is free on every proper subset. Each remaining original component contains a neighbour of H and hence a distinction formerly shared with H that has become loose. By induction on fewer names, that component has exactly the same remaining holders in the competitor. Its loose distinction rules out the closed six-name exception. Any such component consequently supplies a common holder in the original problem, and section 2 finishes the argument. If no original holder remains, the original had only H. Its full elementary relation admits no proper candidate support and only H can define it irredundantly. This completes the induction.

Each new holder in ordinary growth shares exactly one distinction with each of its old parents. New holders in the same parent step share none, and old meetings retain their original distinctions. Thus every later stage of a single-meeting start has single meetings. This argument is a statement about the ordinary models and named full states. Inspecting only pair disagreement frequencies gives a different input from the fixed-exterior pair tests above.
