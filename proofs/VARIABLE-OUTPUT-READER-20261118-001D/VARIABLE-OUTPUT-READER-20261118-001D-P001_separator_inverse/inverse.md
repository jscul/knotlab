# What the allowed-state relation determines at every finite width

**Theorem.** In the serial two-or-three-fresh parity family, every coordinate bijection carrying one full allowed-state relation onto another carries the actual legal three-input choices onto each other. The same conclusion holds for two components retained by genuine single-distinction cuts, when the marked triples on both sides survive the cuts and the bijection carries one retained allowed-state relation onto the other. The bijection need not preserve boundary flags.

The scalar table records a minimum for each pair. A bijection preserving the allowed-state relation preserves all these minima. The theorem leaves open a scalar isometry that changes the allowed-state relation. The proof compares the two presentations directly, so no reachability assumption on intermediate rearrangements is needed. ## Two elementary source restrictions

Write \(G\) for the ordinary meeting graph. Wire every loose distinction to one additional vertex \(o\), called the exterior, to obtain the multigraph \(H\). Each shared distinction is an ordinary edge. A binary assignment is allowed precisely when its selected edges have even incidence at every vertex. Evenness at \(o\) follows by summing the ordinary equations. The minimal nonempty supports of allowed assignments are therefore the simple cycles, including a two-edge parallel cycle. Equality of the relations on the same edge names gives equality of all these cycle sets.

We use the following consequence of the earlier alternating-count proof. A lawful finite construction cannot have two final junctions \(S,T\) such that all outputs of the preceding prefix are eventually consumed in the ordinary graph, all earlier sites including the seed have two or three distinct ordinary children, the seed has three, and the last prefix site sends its two outputs to \(S,T\). To see the scope of that proof, neither the alternating count nor its reverse layer induction uses the input counts of the two sinks. If both sinks were to have three identical parents, the later sink would already be forbidden. They can therefore have at most two common parents, as the initial layer of that induction requires. All other steps apply verbatim. [Alternating count and layer argument](../../VARIABLE-OUTPUT-READER-20261118-001C/VARIABLE-OUTPUT-READER-20261118-001C-P001_triple_inverse/complementary.md#an-elementary-count-forces-the-reverse-tests)

**Removing two ordinary vertices.** After deleting any two ordinary sites \(u,v\) of \(H\), every remaining site is connected to \(o\). Otherwise take a component \(C\) away from \(o\). It has no loose distinctions. Its latest-created site has at least two outputs and can send them only to \(u,v\), at most one to each. Thus both sites follow every site of \(C\). Every ordinary prefix is connected, so \(C\) is exactly the prefix before whichever site appears first, say \(u\). Every loose distinction of this prefix goes to \(u\) or \(v\). Append \(u\) on its actual old inputs, then append a junction on just the nonempty set of \(v\)'s inputs from \(C\). This second step is lawful. An earlier witness of a pair of these parents belongs to \(C\) or is \(u\), since no other ordinary site outside \(C\) touches it. If there are three such parents, they are all of \(v\)'s original parents, so no common earlier witness exists. Taking two fresh outputs at each new site gives exactly the excluded two-sink construction. This proves the assertion. Deleting just one ordinary site also leaves every component attached to \(o\), directly because the latest site of a detached component would need two distinct later neighbors. Deleting \(o\) leaves the connected graph \(G\). Thus \(H\) has no cut vertex whenever the claim about a triple is nonvacuous.

**Rerooting and common continuations.** For a graph isomorphism moving the exterior from \(s\) to \(t\), every parallel class must be incident to both, since ordinary meetings are simple. The latest site holds two or three parallel outputs to the exterior. Consequently \(t\) is latest with exterior \(s\), and \(s\) is latest with exterior \(t\). Except in the one-site case, deleting both and retaining every old edge to either as loose gives the identical physical prefix. The alternatives consume complementary groups. The common-prefix theorem gives an empty triple menu in both. Moreover, following any such distinct state-equal one-step alternatives by the same literal sequence preserves triple-menu equality. No old distinction is loose in both successors, so every subsequent connection to the old part goes through the disputed junction. A legal triple and its three distinct pair witnesses form a six-cycle. It cannot cross this one-vertex connection. The residual old holders still give no triple with one another or the disputed junction, and every possible triple on the new side has exactly the same witnesses in both histories. ## The part supporting the three marked inputs

Transport the bit names along the proposed bijection. Suppose one presentation has a legal triple, with three distinct ordinary parents. Its three pair witnesses are distinct because no witness is common to all three. The six sites lie on a simple ordinary cycle, hence in one maximal 2-connected ordinary block \(B\). A component outside this block can meet it at only one site, since two attachments would enlarge the block.

At each \(v\in B\), collect in \(A_v\) its actual loose edges and every ordinary branch attached there, including all the loose edges of the branch. Each nonempty \(A_v\) has terminals \(o,v\). Every outside branch has a route to \(o\) after deletion of \(v\). Replace each nonempty group by one indexed edge \(ov\), keeping the ordinary edges of \(B\). The resulting simple graph \(T\), the classical torso of this part, is 3-connected. Deleting \(o\) and any one ordinary vertex leaves \(B\) connected. After deleting two ordinary vertices, project paths to the exterior from \(H\) onto \(T\). The preceding removal result supplies those paths. The six-cycle ensures there are enough vertices for 3-connectivity.

We show that the same literal edge groups also yield the same labeled torso in any alternative reachable graph \(H'\) with the same cycles. We use the graphic rank \(r(X)\), the maximum number of edges in a cycle-free subset of \(X\). It equals the number of incident vertices minus the number of edge-containing components. Thus the cycle sets determine it. An edge set is connected as a cycle matroid when its edges are linked by chains of cycles with successive cycles sharing an edge.

In a 2-connected graph any two edges lie on a common simple cycle. Subdivide the two edges. Deletion of any single vertex still leaves the graph connected. Seek two internally disjoint paths between the new vertices. Split each other vertex into an entrance and exit with capacity one between them, and allow capacity two along each original edge direction. Send one unit along a path. If there is no augmenting path, the set reachable from the start in the residual graph has a cut of capacity one because every crossing forward arc is full and each reverse-crossing arc carries zero. Such a cut would remove just one interior vertex. Hence a second unit can be sent, and the resulting two integral paths give the cycle.

Fix \(A=A_v\) and let \(D=E(H)\setminus A\). Its edges are connected as a cycle matroid. Indeed \(T-ov\) is 2-connected. On expanding its other indexed edges, each edge of an expanded group lies on a terminal-to-terminal path. Take a cycle in \(H\) through that edge and an ordinary edge of \(B\). Its portion inside the group connects the terminals. Any such path substitutes for its indexed edge in a torso cycle. This links every expanded edge to the connected family of torso cycles. Also \(A\) and \(D\) have connected edge support and exactly two common vertices, giving

\[
 r(A)+r(D)-r(E(H))=1 .
\]

The same ranks and connected cycle matroid on \(D\) hold in \(H'\). In particular \(D\) has connected edge support. If \(A\) has \(c\) edge-containing components there and \(b\) vertices common to \(A,D\), the graphic rank formula gives \(b-c=1\). Each component needs two distinct attachments, since \(H'\) has no cut vertex. Thus \(b\ge2c\), forcing \(c=1\) and \(b=2\). This argument applies even when the matroid on \(A\) alone is disconnected.

In either graph the intersections of \(A\) with cycles meeting both \(A,D\) are exactly its simple paths between the two terminals. Each such path can close through the connected complement. In particular a named distinction \(e\in A\) is a direct edge between its terminals exactly when some crossing cycle meets \(A\) in \(\{e\}\). Replacing every group by one indexed edge in \(H'\) now gives a connected graph with the very same labeled torso cycles. A crossing cycle traverses each group it meets along one terminal path, and any torso cycle expands to a simple cycle by choosing these paths. The different groups have disjoint interiors.

For completeness, the labeled cycles identify every vertex star of \(T\). A bond is a minimal nonempty edge cut, equivalently a minimal deletion lowering graphic rank. The star of each vertex in \(T\) is a bond whose deletion leaves a connected cycle matroid, since deleting that vertex leaves a 2-connected graph. In any connected graph representation, a bond with this property must be a star. Otherwise each of the two connected sides of the bond would have a surviving edge, with no cycle meeting both sides after deletion. The two torso graphs have the same rank and therefore the same number of vertices. Their matched stars identify all endpoints. This gives the required torso identification directly from cycles and ranks.

## Locating the exterior

Any group \(A_v\) of at least two edges forces the alternative exterior onto one of its two torso terminals. If it contains an interior vertex, removing both terminals separates that vertex from the rest of the torso. The two-ordinary-vertex removal result excludes two ordinary terminals. If it has no interior, its parallel edges cannot both be ordinary. There is a group of at least two edges because the latest junction has at least two loose outputs, either in the block or in a branch attached to it. If two different groups have this size, their indexed edges meet at just \(o\). The alternative exterior must then be \(o\).

Whenever the torso exterior stays \(o\), the singleton crossing-cycle test identifies each marked edge as a direct loose edge at the same ordinary parent. No ordinary vertex outside the torso can witness a pair of different parents in \(B\), since each external group meets only one ordinary torso vertex. The actual pair witnesses and the absence of a common witness therefore transfer.

The remaining possibility is one large group \(A_s\) and an alternative exterior at \(s\). Set \(C=B-\{s\}\). This is connected. In the first presentation, every other group has at most one edge, a direct loose distinction, and the only ordinary exit from \(C\) is \(s\). In the alternative, the ordinary exit is the vertex formerly called \(o\). Loose distinctions at sites of \(C\) are their former ordinary edges to \(s\), again at most one per site. In either ordering the latest site of \(C\) has at least two fresh outputs. One must go to the exit site. Thus every site in \(C\) precedes the exit, and connected prefixes force \(C\) to be the entire prefix before the exit.

The two prefixes have identical incidence because every inside edge is unchanged and every edge from \(C\) to either torso terminal is loose at its inside site. The two exit junctions take complementary groups of these actual distinctions. Both input groups are legal on this prefix, and the number and names of fresh outputs may be chosen alike when applying the common-prefix theorem. The old residual holders cannot give a triple with each other or with the exit junction. Later branches attached at the exit can supply no witness of a pair in \(B\). This contradicts the original legal triple supported in \(B\). We have proved preservation in one direction, and exchanging the two presentations gives the converse.

## Components retained after genuine single-distinction cuts

Cutting an ordinary bridge leaves its distinction as one loose boundary bit on each retained side. Consider a connected piece left after removing the bridges and retaining the boundary bits. Every discarded ordinary component touches the piece through a single bridge. Any original path to the exterior from a retained site, avoiding two specified retained ordinary sites, gives a path in the local wired graph. At its first exit from the piece the boundary bit leads directly to the local exterior. Therefore the ordinary two-vertex removal property descends to this piece.

The local birth order has a connected prefix at each step. Indeed, a bridge must have entered its child by a singleton addition. Any input of a multiple-parent addition belongs to a four-cycle through another parent and their earlier common neighbor. The original root determines a tree of pieces joined by bridges. A piece away from the root begins at the child of its sole incoming bridge. No vertex beyond that bridge could have existed earlier, by prefix connectedness. Its first local junction retains the incoming distinction and its two or three fresh outputs, giving local arity three or four. Every other local addition keeps all its original parents. Two distinct sites in a retained piece cannot have an ordinary pair witness in a discarded branch, since that would provide a route around the bridge. Thus local input tests hold on the local prefixes. All sites still make at least two fresh distinctions, including any output later used by a discarded branch.

The torso, direct-edge and exterior arguments consequently apply to surviving marked candidates in these pieces. A marked boundary would already have been rejected. For a survivor every marked distinction that is loose locally is also loose in the full source. Only the changed-exterior argument needs care about an initial local junction of arity four.

In that case \(C=B-\{s\}\) is the entire *local* prefix in each presentation, by the same latest-site argument. The two local exit choices are again legal complementary groups on \(C\). Its last site has at least two loose outputs just before the cap, and each group uses at most one per site. Hence it belongs to both groups. All their parents lie on its one bipartition side, opposite the cap. After the first cap the remaining distinctions in \(C\) are exactly the second group's at most three bits. A legal triple supported in \(B\) on the true side cannot include the cap together with an old parent. It must therefore use all three remaining bits in \(C\).

Since the true triple survived the cuts, none of these three is a boundary. There is consequently **no discarded branch attached anywhere in \(C\)** in the true source because each such branch would leave a boundary bit among its remaining distinctions. In the true full graph \(C\) is an ordinary component behind its cap, with at most one loose distinction per site, and the latest-site argument now makes it an actual prefix of the original three-distinction seed history. Both local cap input groups are legal choices on this identical physical prefix. The common-prefix theorem again gives an empty old triple menu, a contradiction. This handles the local arity-four possibility without generalizing the seed of the earlier theorem.

Thus equal retained allowed-state relations preserve the verdict on surviving marks. The unresolved scalar case requires an isometry of retained scalar minima which changes the labeled cycle family. A counterexample for the full unpruned scalar question must also supply an isometry before the cuts.

Two concrete checks distinguish the scopes. Starting from the state-equal width-eighteen pair, attach three two-output children to the three fresh outputs of the disputed junction, then one two-output junction on each pair of these children. At width thirty the two homogeneous check spaces still agree, and each presentation has the same eight actual triples among the six latest outputs. For an arity-four local seed, add a three-output singleton child to the original root and repeat the same six additions at that child. Detaching its incoming bridge produces a four-place local seed and preserves all eight true triples. Attach one more singleton gate through a marked bit of one of them. Removing this new branch gives the same retained check space, while the marked bit is now a bridge in the full source and fails the required boundary test.
