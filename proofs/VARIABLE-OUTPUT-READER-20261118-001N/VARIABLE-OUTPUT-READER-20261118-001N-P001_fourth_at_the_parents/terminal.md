# A terminal fourth three-output junction

Write \(K=D+1\) for the smallest cardinality of a bond containing two named distinctions. A table through \(K=k\) gives each exact entry at most \(k\) and identifies the entries larger than \(k\). A bond is an inclusion-minimal nonempty parity equation. The incidence graph wires all loose distinctions to one exterior vertex when taking cuts. Parent counts and witnesses always refer to ordinary sites.

## A unary terminal junction against every reachable rival

**Theorem.** Suppose the fourth three-output addition takes one old bit \(e\) and all three of its outputs \(F=\{f_1,f_2,f_3\}\) are still loose. The whole table through \(K=6\) determines every marked three-parent permission against every reachable mixed rival under the given name correspondence. If any triple is legal, it also determines all ordinary junction supports. The competing history can have any number of three-output additions, in any order. The last addition in the displayed source history need not be the unary site.

The unary site \(v\) has only one ordinary meeting, at \(e\). Deleting \(e\) from ordinary incidence leaves the named part \(F\) and a nonempty part on the old side. In every reachable presentation all ordinary stars have size at most six. The clipped separator theorem therefore identifies exactly the same part \(F\) on deleting \(e\) in the rival. This uses ordinary incidence, where distinct loose bits have no common exterior neighbor. In particular \(e\) must join the two ordinary parts in the rival, since deleting a loose bit would not disconnect ordinary incidence. [Clipped separator theorem](../../VARIABLE-OUTPUT-READER-20261118-001G/VARIABLE-OUTPUT-READER-20261118-001G-P001_two_three_fresh/separator_horizon.md)

On the side \(F\), every ordinary star is contained in \(F\cup\{e\}\). Two distinct such sites would use at least \(3+3-1=5\) bits, because each has degree at least three and two ordinary sites meet at most once. There is exactly one site. Its support is \(F\cup\{e\}\), and the three bits of \(F\) are loose there. This site is not a seed, whose degree is three. Every nonseed takes at least one earlier parent. Its single ordinary attachment must therefore be its input, and all three bits in \(F\) are its outputs. This argument fixes the direction of the attachment without assuming the rival's chronology.

In either history delete this site and its three fresh bits, leaving \(e\) loose at its old holder. Every other birth can be replayed in its original order. A site with only one ordinary neighbor cannot supply a common witness for two distinct older holders, none of its fresh bits is used elsewhere, and no other input uses \(e\). Thus the remaining history is reachable, and the deleted site may be appended last. In the source the remaining history belongs to the three-output-count family with third addition unary, binary or ternary.

Let \(L\) denote the old equation space, \(C\) an old bond and \(W=F\cup\{e\}\). All old bonds remain bonds since a new equation outside \(L\) contains all of \(F\). The complete list of new bonds is

\[
 W,\qquad F\cup(C\setminus\{e\})\quad(C\text{ an old bond containing }e).
\]

Indeed, a new word is \(W\mathbin\triangle X\) with \(X\in L\). If \(e\notin X\), it contains \(W\). Otherwise decompose \(X\) into disjoint old bonds. The piece containing \(e\) already gives the asserted smaller or equal word. Conversely, when \(X=C\) is a bond containing \(e\), a smaller old word would be a proper old subword of \(C\). A smaller new word, after adding \(W\), would likewise yield a proper old subword containing \(e\). The word \(W\) is minimal since there is no old nonempty equation on \(\{e\}\).

Every new bond containing two old bits has an old bond containing the pair and two bits smaller. Hence the restrictions of the two successor tables to the old names give exactly their respective predecessor tables, including after clipping at six. For distinct \(f,g\in F\) and \(x\) old, \(x\ne e\), the other readings are

\[
 K(f,g)=K(f,e)=4,\qquad K(f,x)=K_{\rm old}(e,x)+2 .
\]

The three preceding arbitrary-rival theorems apply to the source prefix and the rival just obtained. They give the same legal prefix triples, and the same physical stars if some prefix triple is legal. An old marked triple survives the unary addition exactly when it was legal and avoids \(e\). A triple with a fresh bit cannot be legal. Two fresh bits have the same holder, and a single fresh holder has only one ordinary neighbor while a legal triple needs two distinct witnesses there. Thus the permissions transfer under the original identification of all names. If the successor has a legal triple, the prefix does too, and the one deleted star has already been identified physically. [Third unary](../../VARIABLE-OUTPUT-READER-20261118-001M/VARIABLE-OUTPUT-READER-20261118-001M-P001_unary_third/proof.md) · [Third binary](../../VARIABLE-OUTPUT-READER-20261118-001L/VARIABLE-OUTPUT-READER-20261118-001L-P001_binary_third/proof.md) · [Third ternary](../../VARIABLE-OUTPUT-READER-20261118-001K/VARIABLE-OUTPUT-READER-20261118-001K-P001_third_three_fresh/proof.md)

At the smaller cutoff \(K=5\), the same argument applies when both presentations have maximum arity five. The source prefix is then in the unary-third or binary-third family, both of which use only \(K\le5\). Against unrestricted arity six there is still a local conclusion at \(K=5\). Each fresh bit has closed through-four neighborhood exactly \(W\), so a rival bond attaining a fresh pair forces \(W\) as a four-bit equation. If \(W\) enclosed at least three ordinary sites, its latest three sites would export at least four bits. Equality exhausts \(W\), and each of those three sites has two outputs and degree at most five. Thus each \(f\) then has an ordinary holder of degree at most five. Its star cannot have degree three, since \(f\) has no reading three. A four-star there must equal \(W\). A five-star there would be contained in \(W\) together with the old bits reading three to \(e\). Those extra bits exist only if \(e\) meets the unique ternary star \(T\). Then \(W\cup T=F\cup T\), and a rival five-site can use at most one bit of the physical \(T\), leaving at most \(3+1=4\) places. Finally a two-site shore of cost four would require two ternary sites meeting, excluded by the unique physical ternary star. This forces \(W\) as a physical rival star at \(K=5\). The proof locating the three individual loose outputs in an unrestricted rival uses the partition at \(K=6\).

Now suppose only that the supplied correspondence maps the three loose outputs \(F\) of the fourth source addition onto the three loose outputs of some rival site. The old prefixes and the two parent counts may differ. A site with all its outputs loose can be deferred to the end even when it has two or three inputs. Any pair using it as a witness already had an earlier witness, and a later common witness for all three of its own parents would itself have been forbidden. This permits deletion and replay in both presentations. ### What erasure can change

For a terminal parent set \(P\) with \(p=|P|\), old bonds remain bonds. A new word through a fresh bit is \(F\cup J\), where \(P\mathbin\triangle J\) is an old word. Each input has reading \(p+3\) to each fresh bit. Any two of three legal old inputs have old minimum at least five. These are chronological and small-cut results for all reachable presentations. For \(p=3\) and \(|J|\le3\), either \(J=P\) or \(J\cap P=\varnothing\). One common member would give an old word of size at most four containing two of the three eligible bits. Two would give a nonempty word of size at most two. In the disjoint case \(P\cup J\) is an old bond of size at most six. Indeed a proper decomposition at this size must be two disjoint ternary bonds, and each ternary bond contains at most one of the three eligible inputs. Thus the old-old readings through five are unchanged by birth. Through six only pairs inside \(P\) can shorten. A fresh pair reads either five or six. It reads five exactly when an old five-bit bond contains all of \(P\), in which case its other two bits each read five to every fresh output. Such a bond selects all three distinct old input holders on its ordinary shore.

For \(p=2\) and \(|J|\le2\), again \(J=P\) or \(J\) is disjoint from \(P\). An overlap of one otherwise gives an old word of size at most two. If \(|J|=1\) and it is disjoint, the old word is a ternary star through the two loose inputs at distinct holders, which is impossible. A fresh pair therefore reads five, and the only old-old pair that can shorten through five is the pair \(P\). A fresh bit reads five to an old bit outside \(P\) only if an old four-bit bond contains \(P\) and that bit. A unary cap has fresh-pair minimum four, so the table distinguishes this case on either side.

* When \(p=2\), the closed neighborhood of a fresh bit through five in the source is exactly \(F\cup P\), by the absence of four-bit bonds through \(P\). Both its bits are loose at different source holders. Also \(F\cup P\) is a rival equation, since it is the sole possible witness for a fresh pair of value five.
* When \(p=3\) and the rival has two parents, only the pair \(Q\) can differ. Erasing the rival cap can only increase that reading. Each \(q\in Q\) reads five to a fresh output and therefore belongs to an old source five-bit bond containing all of \(P\). Its shore has at least three sites.

Adding the forced equations shows that any new candidate covering a true partner replaces at most one true bit. ## Locating the terminal outputs without a matching promise

**Theorem.** At arbitrary width, the preceding three-parent fourth-addition conclusion holds against every reachable rival under every \(K\le6\) table isometry. This cutoff is sharp. At width 23 a terminal fourth three-parent, three-output birth has a correspondence that preserves all readings through \(K=5\) and changes a marked verdict. The binary fourth-addition conclusion likewise holds against every reachable rival through \(K=5\) when the third three-output addition was unary or binary. In these binary cases the entire state space transfers for a unary-third prefix and for a binary-third prefix with at most three shrinking joins. At four shrinks every matching rival has the same empty menu. Shrink counts here are taken after deleting the terminal site. All statements allow later two-output additions provided the three outputs of the indicated fourth site are still loose. [Width-23 sharpness example](census.md)

Use \(n\) for the number of sites after deferring and deleting the source terminal site. Any rival on the full \(2n+7\) names has at most \(n+1\) sites. Indeed the table identifies a unique ternary site, so the first two additions are unary with three outputs. In the rival width formula \(2r+1+m\) the number \(m\) of nonseed three-output additions is even. If \(m=2\), the minimum-width inverse applied to that rival as source would identify both parity spaces through \(K=5\), although the two ranks would be \(n+2\) and \(n+1\). Hence \(m\ge4\) and \(r\le n+1\). [Minimum-width inverse in the reverse direction](../../VARIABLE-OUTPUT-READER-20261118-001J/VARIABLE-OUTPUT-READER-20261118-001J-P001_seven_to_six/family.md)

First suppose the source prefix is unary-third, or binary-third with at most three shrinking joins. Its old words through four remain exactly the whole successor's words through four, because every newly introduced word has size at least five. The old seed and all old four-place stars transfer by the four-word theorem. Apply the protected-row argument above **in the successor**. For a binary terminal source, only the two loose inputs \(P\) acquire fresh partners through five, and only the old pair \(P\) can shorten. None of the protected rows chosen above uses a member of \(P\). In the binary-third case with two shrinks one can choose among the three protected outputs of \(B\), at most one of which belongs to \(P\). With three shrinks the full old frontier is already a large five-boundary, so no protected bit is old loose. All additional cross-pair exclusions at the pair of five-stars involve a shared old input. For a ternary terminal source, every old-old entry through five is unchanged, and any old bit newly within five of a fresh mark belongs to an old large five-boundary through \(P\). Every protected row is again exactly its old row. Thus the same arguments force every old five-star as a word supported on the old names. For a binary terminal source \(F\cup P\) is also forced as the unique through-five witness on a fresh pair, as proved above. All \(n+1\) source site equations are present, giving equal spaces by the rival rank bound. This proves the binary assertion.

For a ternary terminal source with one of the same prefixes, the rival has at least \(n\) independent words supported entirely on the old names. We next obtain this same bound for the remaining ternary-third prefix. Here the old choice \(P\) is legal, hence there are zero or one old shrinking sites. Write \(H\) for the old six-place site, with inputs \(I\). Every source low site transfers as above through five. The old shrinking site's input rows are protected, since every old shore of at least three sites costs at least six and thus no old bit has a fresh partner through five. These old low words span \(n-1\) dimensions. At each input-output pair on \(H\) the source old reading is six, and no word of this low span of size at most six attains it. **Some \(i\in I\) has \(K(i,f)>6\) for every fresh \(f\).** If zero old sites shrank, every old shore with at least three sites costs at least seven. No old six-bit cut can then contain \(P\), and no shared input of \(H\) is in \(P\). With one old shrink, assume all three inputs of \(H\) have fresh partners through six. For each \(i\in I\) the cap word calculation gives an old bond \(C_i\) of size at most six containing \(P\cup\{i\}\). Its ordinary shore \(U_i\) contains all three distinct loose holders of \(P\). If \(H\) were selected later, reaching six would require its increment to vanish. By cut submodularity the union \(U\) of these pinned minimum shores is again a minimum shore. It contains all three old holders of \(I\), excludes \(H\), and contains all three holders of \(P\). The six distinct bits \(I\sqcup P\) cross, exhausting its boundary. In the actual successor, \(I\) joins \(U\) to \(H\), and \(P\) joins \(U\) to the new terminal site. Deleting these two ordinary sites would disconnect the nonempty \(U\) from the exterior. The two-ordinary-site deletion theorem forbids this in every reachable source. This proves the asserted strict row. That theorem completes the ternary assertion for every marked triple.

### A binary fourth addition on the four-loose prefix

The remaining binary-third prefix has four shrinking joins and exactly four loose bits \(E\). They occur with multiplicities \(1,1,2\) on a chronological ordinary path \(A,M,Z\), with \(Z\) latest. The only permitted binary parent pair \(P\) takes the single loose bit at \(A\) and one of the two at \(Z\). The whole old frontier \(E\) is a four-bit bond. It is the only old four-bit boundary containing \(P\). Indeed all large four-shores are the consecutive prefixes starting just after the fourth shrink and ending at the full old source. Any proper one excludes the latest site \(Z\), hence its boundary excludes \(Z\)'s globally loose input to the cap. A physical four-star cannot hold two loose bits at distinct holders, and a pair of sites cannot bound four bits with a unique ternary star. [Four-loose geometry and rank](../../VARIABLE-OUTPUT-READER-20261118-001L/VARIABLE-OUTPUT-READER-20261118-001L-P001_binary_third/four_loose.md)

Therefore a fresh bit's through-five partners are the other fresh bits and precisely \(E\), witnessed by the terminal star and its sum with \(E\). The cap word calculation shows that all old readings through five are unchanged since the input pair was already at four. We show that \(n\) independent words supported on old labels still transfer to any successor rival. In the four-loose proof let \(U\) be the positive five-loose prefix immediately before the fourth shrink \(c\), and \(k\) the number of two-output binaries following \(c\). The proof forces every site in \(U\) by a pair of bits with both holders in \(U\). These internal forcing bits keep their full through-five neighborhoods here, because they are not among the four loose bits \(E\). It also forces \(c\) by pairing a shared input \(i\) of \(c\) with an output, using the already forced five-word \(\partial U\). The row at \(i\) has no new fresh neighbors. Therefore every rival five-word through this pair is supported on old names, and all the old pair exclusions, comparisons to \(\partial U\) and its sum with the candidate remain valid. When the forced proof uses the fact that a member of the resulting four-word reads at most four to an output, this also holds in the full successor, since there is no new word through four. If \(k=0\), these are \(n\) independent old-supported words. If \(k\ge1\), the old proof gets \(k+1\) independent words on the two spares of \(U\) and the bits born at and after \(c\), disjoint in span from the site words of \(U\). Its forcing pairs and controlling rows all use cutoff four. The new bits have no partners at this cutoff, so the same \(k+1\) old-supported dimensions are present. This again gives \(n\). The rank argument above forces \(F\) to be precisely the three loose outputs of one rival nonseed site.

On erasing that rival site, its old table through five agrees with the old source table. A unary rival would have fresh-pair value four. A ternary rival preserves all its old entries through five. A binary rival uses a pair \(Q\subset E\), since its inputs read five to a fresh bit. Every pair in \(E\) already read at most four before this birth on either side, so erasing a binary rival also preserves its old table through five. The old four-shrink theorem gives the rival precursor an empty menu, ruling out a ternary rival cap. We are left with a binary \(Q\). Its bits were physically loose at different rival holders, with mutual reading at most four. Consequently the rival precursor has a four-cut on a shore of at least three sites. A single star cannot contain both bits, the unique ternary star rules out a two-site four-cut and a three-bit bond is a single ternary star.

The rival precursor has rank \(n\) and exactly three nonseed three-output additions. Reordering the large four-shore first gives a prefix with four loose bits. Such a prefix has already used all three three-output additions, since the minimum-width family with two cannot reach four. Subsequent additions use at least two inputs and have two outputs. The old final frontier has at most four loose bits. Among the three possible old families, the ternary-third and unary-third families always have at least five. The binary-third family gives exactly the four-shrink case and the path \(A,M,Z\) above. After the binary three-output addition, the only old holders with loose bits are the adjacent \(M,Z\). The only other such holder is the new site. Adjacent ordinary sites have opposite colors, so no triple on three distinct available holders is legal. This applies in both presentations. ### A binary fourth addition after the earlier six-place site

Let the deleted fourth site now be binary and the third three-output addition \(H\) ternary. If the prefix has no shrinking join, the table through \(K=6\) determines all successor stars against any reachable rival. The same holds with one old shrink if the old prefix has any legal triple and neither of the two terminal input bits belongs to the seed. We give the reduction at all zero- and one-shrink prefixes.

Every old shore with at least three sites costs at least six, so no old word of size at most five contains both loose terminal inputs \(P\). Their holders are distinct with the same color, which also excludes a singleton or two-site shore at that size. Through five a fresh row thus sees exactly \(F\cup P\), and only the old pair \(P\) can shorten. The high-prefix forcing pairs at its low shrinking sites are shared inputs. Their two short rows are unchanged and miss \(F\), so all \(n-1\) low-site equations still transfer. At cutoff six a fresh partner outside \(P\) can only be on the seed \(T\), and only if \(P\) itself meets \(T\). To see this, write the new word \(F\cup J\) with \(|J|\le3\). Disjoint \(J\) would put \(P\cup J\) in an old bond of size at most five. Unless \(J=P\), a nonempty overlap therefore uses one input and gives an old word of size at most three. This must be the unique ternary star, containing the other input and all of \(J\setminus P\).

Choose an old input of \(H\) outside \(T\), possible since two sites meet at most once. This shared bit is outside \(P\) and has no fresh partner through six. It still reads six to each old output of \(H\), since only the pair \(P\) can have become shorter than six. The previous old-supported rank argument now applies. The common low span cannot realize this pair with six bits, so the rival has \(n\) independent equations avoiding \(F\) and rank \(n+1\). Its fresh block is physically a single terminal output triple. Erasing that site, the tables of the two prefixes agree through five with the sole possible exception of the pair \(P\). Indeed, a two-parent rival would have to take \(P\) itself by the fresh row, and a three-parent rival leaves all its old-old readings through five unchanged.

Every ordinary rival site of size at most five in this prefix is physically a low source site. For five places one may apply the reverse low-star argument with its one potentially changed pair as follows. A physical five-site has at least four bits outside the physical ternary \(T\). Among these, two bits close through five in the source must meet at a common source site of degree at most five. A short sum of two adjacent sites would have to use two seed bits and three bits of a four-place site, so two nonseed bits in it meet at that four-site. The possible exception \(P\), if both its members are in these four or more nonseed bits, would require a third nonseed bit to meet both loose source holders of \(P\). Those holders are distinct and have the same color, so this is impossible. All the nonseed bits of this rival star therefore meet pairwise at ordinary source sites of size at most five. They share one such holder by simplicity and bipartiteness. Its forced low-site equation and the rival star overlap in at least four places, hence have difference of size at most two and must agree. The common through-four theorem gives the lower arities. All these arguments apply even if the value of the exceptional pair has increased on erasure. [Reverse low-star argument](../../VARIABLE-OUTPUT-READER-20261118-001J/VARIABLE-OUTPUT-READER-20261118-001J-P001_seven_to_six/inverse.md#five-bit-equations-at-the-first-shrink)

The rival prefix has rank \(n\) and three three-output additions. It must have a six-place star because its physical low stars are among just \(n-1\) source low stars. The initial two unary additions exhaust the other three-output allocations. The old low stars now agree physically by counting, and the rival has the same number of old shrinking sites, zero or one. Its old shores with at least three sites thus also cost at least six. A three-input terminal rival would have a fresh-pair value of six, whereas the binary source has five. Both terminal sites are binary and take the same named \(P\).

With zero old shrinks every site in both successors has at least as many outputs as inputs and a unique ternary seed. The nonshrinking within-class theorem determines all current stars. With one old shrink, the two old six-bit frontiers both contain \(P\). When \(P\cap T=\varnothing\), neither birth can shorten an old pair through six except \(P\) itself. Indeed the only possible short word carrying a fresh bit then is the actual five-star. In both prefixes the pair \(P\) costs at least six by the short-shore bound and at most six by the whole frontier. Restoring that single old reading gives equal old tables through six. If the old source has a legal triple, the old ternary-third theorem identifies all its physical stars, and adding the same named star gives the same whole successor.

Thus the terminal binary case outside these uniform arguments requires an earlier ternary three-output addition and at least one old shrink. With one, either the old menu is empty or one of the terminal inputs meets the seed. With two, the old menu is always empty. There are no larger shrink counts in this prefix branch. These conditions specify the residual in this argument. Empty predecessor menus alone do not determine the successor menu of a binary cap. ## What can change at the holder or witness of a legal triple

In this section the two additions are made on the same named physical prefix, on permitted inputs \(P,Q\). They have the same three fresh names. A matched fresh row through \(K=6\) suffices, including the entry to one other fresh name. The common-prefix theorem already gives equal parent counts \(p\) and equal menus. In particular an attempted old-only triple destroyed by consumption or by a new common witness cannot yield a matched reading. We identify when the terminal site itself can change at an actual witness or holder of a live triple. [Common-prefix theorem for all mark placements](../../VARIABLE-OUTPUT-READER-20261118-001H/VARIABLE-OUTPUT-READER-20261118-001H-P001_high_junctions/mechanisms.md#equal-parent-counts)

**A terminal site witnessing a pair of a live old-only triple has its entire named star fixed.** Let the surviving marks be \(l_a,l_b,l_c\). At each of these three holders, an old input consumed by only one of the two additions would remain loose on the other side. It could replace that holder's mark in the legal triple there. Its reading to a fresh bit would exceed \(p+3\), whereas an actual input reads exactly \(p+3\). Thus the two input sets agree at all three holders. If the terminal site witnesses the marked pair at \(a,b\), its input names there coincide. It misses \(c\), since otherwise it would meet the whole triple. In the binary case its two inputs are already fixed. [Strict reading for any surviving old mark](../../VARIABLE-OUTPUT-READER-20261118-001F/VARIABLE-OUTPUT-READER-20261118-001F-P001_finite_interval/structural.md#separate-derivation-for-a-terminal-junction)

In the ternary case suppose the third input changes to \(q\in Q\setminus P\). Its reading six on the \(P\) side requires an old cut \(C\ni q\) with \(|C|\le2|C\cap P|\), by writing the realizing word as the new star plus an old cut. If it used one input the cut would have size at most two, and if it used two it would contain two members of a legal triple in a cut through four. It must therefore contain \(P\cup\{q\}\) and have size at most six. Its selected shore contains \(a,b\), hence also their loose marks \(l_a,l_b\). These six distinct bits are all globally loose. By connectivity of ordinary sites, an old cut with only loose bits must be the full old loose frontier. It would have to contain \(l_c\) as well. This excludes the changed third input.

**A terminal site holding a live fresh mark can change only by exchanging two loose siblings on a six-bit prefix frontier.** Write the other two marks \(l_a,l_b\). The legal fresh triple requires two distinct witnesses \(x,z\) among the terminal parents for its meetings with \(a,b\), and an old common witness \(r\) of \(a,b\) outside the entire parent set. The common-prefix argument excludes unary parents and proves that a changed holder set cannot support this live fresh triple. Hence the old parent holders coincide. If the parent bits differ take \(q\in Q\setminus P\) at the same holder \(w\) as \(e\in P\setminus Q\). The sites \(a,b\) have the opposite color from the parent holders. The same cut bound now requires a cut \(C\) containing all of \(P\) and \(q\), of size at most \(2p\). For \(p=2\) a cut using just one input would have size at most two, and for \(p=3\) the previous argument excludes one or two. All selected parent holders are inside. The two paths \(x,a,\mathrm{exterior}\) and \(z,b,\mathrm{exterior}\), using the two loose marks, have disjoint bits outside \(P\cup\{q\}\). Each contributes another crossing. Thus \(p=3\), and all six crossings have been accounted for. The cut is a bond because a decomposition into two ternaries could not contain all three eligible inputs.

If exactly one of \(a,b\) is inside, only one ordinary crossing is available, from the other holder to its witnessing parent. The path \(a,r,b\) cannot use this same crossing since \(r\) is not that parent. If both are outside, the two ordinary crossings are exactly \(xa,zb\). Their inside endpoints connect inside the bonded shore. The path \(a,r,b\) lies outside because \(r\) is not a parent. There is therefore an ordinary cycle through those two crossings. Ordinary cycles are binary sums of four-cycles, and a four-cycle using either crossing would have to use both. Their endpoints are pairwise distinct, and the two inside endpoints have the same color, so no such four-cycle exists. Both \(a,b\) must be inside. All six crossings are then loose, giving exactly \(P\sqcup\{q,l_a,l_b\}\) as the whole old frontier. The five old holders have multiplicities \(2,1,1,1,1\). The latest old site produces at least two still-loose outputs, so it is \(w\). The alternatives exchange precisely its two siblings \(e,q\). ### Exact pair readings in the sibling exchange

More generally, let the old frontier have six loose bits, let a lawful three-parent, three-output terminal cap consume \(P\), and suppose an unconsumed bit \(q\) shares its old holder \(w\) with \(e\in P\). The sibling exchange preserves the **entire exact table** if and only if the fresh-pair reading is six. When a fresh bit is in a legal triple, this value is guaranteed. In either case exchanging \(e,q\) is a physical relabeling between the two choices. It suffices to show exactly when their rows agree in one successor. The equation spaces themselves differ, since their new generators differ on precisely the two old bits \(e,q\) and there is no two-bit old word. [Fresh-pair value in a live triple](../../VARIABLE-OUTPUT-READER-20261118-001H/VARIABLE-OUTPUT-READER-20261118-001H-P001_high_junctions/mechanisms.md#exchanging-one-marked-terminal-output-with-an-older-loose-distinction)

A fresh minimum below six would be five. Its old five-bit bond contains \(P\) and therefore contains \(q\) too, since both loose siblings cross exactly when \(w\) is inside. This gives \(K(f,q)\le5\), whereas \(K(f,e)=6\). Conversely assume the fresh minimum six. Each fresh bit reads six to either sibling, using the terminal star and the exterior's six-bit bond. It remains to consider an old bit \(k\) different from both siblings. The old swap fixed all other names, so the old readings \(K_{\rm old}(e,k)\) and \(K_{\rm old}(q,k)\) agree. Old bonds persist, giving an upper bound on both new readings.

Write \(v,o\) for the new terminal site and exterior. Each meets three old loose bits, and three parallel fresh bits join \(v,o\). Take a new bond \(D\) through \(k\) and one sibling. If \(v,o\) are on the same side of the cut, merging them gives an old bond through \(k\) and both siblings. If they are on different sides, \(D\) contains all three fresh bits and has size at least six. When \(k\) was old loose, the entire old six-bit frontier already supplies the required old bound.

Suppose \(k\) is an old ordinary meeting and \(v,o\) lie on opposite sides. Let \(m\) count crossings between old ordinary sites, and let \(s\) count crossings among the six old loose bits. The two siblings attach to opposite terminals, so \(s\ge1\) and \(|D|=m+s+3\). On deleting the two terminals, every ordinary component on either side has a loose bit to that side's terminal, by connectivity before deletion. Contract those components in the old ordinary graph. The resulting connected multigraph has \(m\) links, including \(k\). From the component of \(w\), take a shortest path to the nearer endpoint of \(k\), retaining that path's components as \(Y\). The other endpoint of \(k\) is outside \(Y\). In the old wired graph \(\partial Y\) is a bond, since \(Y\) is connected and every remaining component has a loose bit to the old exterior. It contains \(k,e,q\).

If the path has length zero, the sole selected component uses at most three old loose bits to its own terminal and at most \(s\) to the other one. Its old boundary is at most \(m+3+s=|D|\). Otherwise its old ordinary boundary has size at most \(m-1\), using an internal path link. At least one old loose bit lies outside \(Y\), at the omitted endpoint's component. Thus its old boundary costs at most \(m-1+5\le |D|\). In every case an old bond through both siblings and \(k\) is at most as large as \(D\). This proves equality of the two exact rows at every old name and the exchange criterion.

The criterion and witness conclusions assume a common named physical prefix.
