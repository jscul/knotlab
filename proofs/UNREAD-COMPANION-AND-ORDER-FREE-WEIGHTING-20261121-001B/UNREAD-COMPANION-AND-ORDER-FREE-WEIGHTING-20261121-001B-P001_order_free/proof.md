# Every order of fair feasible reveals

For a nonempty finite set of binary words, revealing the distinctions in any order, tossing a fair coin whenever both values still have a completion, gives the uniform law **exactly when the set is an affine binary space**. At an ordinary growth step one needs an additional condition. Every old word must survive and have the same number of children for two successive uniform stage laws to agree with equal division of a uniform old law. Ordinary parity has both properties. A physical not-all-equal birth can change a marked probability arbitrarily many holder meetings away while preserving every old word.

## The relation theorem

Number the \(n\) distinctions and let \(S\subseteq\{0,1\}^n\). For nonempty \(S\) write \(U_S(w)=1/|S|\). Fix a permutation of the distinctions. At a realized prefix the picker sees only which values of the next distinction occur in at least one compatible full word. It chooses uniformly among those possible values. If there is no word to launch, there is no probability distribution. The empty word on zero distinctions is one word and has probability one.

1. **One order.** Each word receives \(2^{-b(w)}\), where \(b(w)\) is the number of positions at which both next values were possible along that word. Thus this order is uniform precisely when all its leaves have the same \(b\). Equivalently, at every branching prefix for that order the two child sets have equal cardinality. Necessity follows by conditioning the desired uniform law on that prefix. Sufficiency follows by induction down its successive splits.
2. **Every order.** This is equivalent to requiring that at *every* feasible partial assignment and each remaining coordinate, the two child counts are either \(0,c\), \(c,0\), or \(c,c\), with \(c>0\). Any partial assignment and proposed next name can be visited by a fixed order. The remaining suffix can be arbitrary. The criterion suffices even if the selection of the next coordinate uses the revealed history and fresh independent coins. With the uniform law as the target, the algorithm uses its exact conditional law at every choice. Equivalently, follow any fixed full word: along every ordering of its coordinates the conditional probabilities telescope to \(1/|S|\). The order-selection coins are independent of the unknown word conditional on what has already been revealed.
3. **Affine sufficiency.** If \(S=v+V\), any nonempty partial slice is a translate of the vectors in \(V\) vanishing on the already specified coordinates. A remaining coordinate is constant on that slice or is a nonzero binary linear functional there. In the latter case adding any direction on which the functional is one bijects the two classes. Thus every split satisfies the criterion.
4. **Necessity.** Induct on \(n\). The zero-coordinate nonempty case is immediate. Choose one coordinate \(i\). Each nonempty \(i\)-slice, with \(i\) removed, also passes every order: reveal \(i\) first and then use the proposed order on that slice. By induction these slices are affine. If only one exists, insert its fixed coordinate. If both exist, they have equal sizes because the first coin is fair. Write them \(a_0+V_0\) and \(a_1+V_1\).

   Suppose the direction spaces differ. Then their annihilators \(V_0^\perp,V_1^\perp\) differ. Choose a vector in their symmetric difference with smallest nonempty support \(C\), and switch slice names if necessary so its parity equation is present only in slice zero. No nonzero vector supported on a proper subset of \(C\) lies in either annihilator. Such a vector would lie in both by minimality, and adding it would produce a smaller member of the symmetric difference. Here the binary alphabet matters: a nonzero coefficient is one, so the sum deletes its entire proper support. Consequently every proper subset of \(C\) is freely and uniformly projected in both slices. On \(C\), slice zero has exactly the one parity equation and slice one projects onto every word. These projection facts follow because the annihilator of a coordinate projection consists precisely of the original parity equations supported on those coordinates.

   Fix any \(j\in C\). Reveal \(C\setminus\{j\}\) first, at any values. The two \(i\)-slices still each have conditional mass one half. Within one of them \(j\) is determined, and within the other it is fair. Thus the uniform target's conditional split at \(j\) is \(1/4,3/4\) in some order. Both values exist. The requested next fair coin fails. This contradiction forces \(V_0=V_1=V\). The full relation consists of the translate of \(V\) with \(i=0\) together with the translate reached by adding \((a_1-a_0,1)\), writing \(i\) last. It is affine.

In fact, even demanding only that all fixed orders produce *the same* full-word distribution forces uniformity here. Induct on \(i\)-slices in the preceding proof. Conditional on \(i\), the common law is uniform on each affine slice by induction; the mass of each nonempty slice is one half when both exist. Even if their sizes were unknown, conditioning on \(C\setminus\{j\}\) gives the same likelihood on both slices. The \(1/4,3/4\) contradiction again forces parallel directions. The slices then have the same size. This is stronger than invariance of the *mixture* over orders. The ternary not-all-equal example below has a uniform mixture despite failing every individual order.

Self-contained tests of affineness include closure under \(a\oplus b\oplus c\) for all \(a,b,c\in S\), or equality with a complete consistent family of parity equations. To see the closure test, translate by any one member; the translate contains zero, is closed under binary addition, and is therefore a vector subspace. In the literature, Simonis and Ashikhmin give the related binary almost-affine-code theorem in §5, Proposition 3, crediting an earlier equivalent result of Beimel and Chor. Their definition uses powers of two for the sizes of *all coordinate projections*. The argument above works directly with the feasible-coin protocol, gives an unequal split certificate, and identifies the separate conditions required at a funded growth step. [Simonis and Ashikhmin, *Almost Affine Codes*](https://doi.org/10.1023/A:1008244215660)

## From one allowed relation to a local law

For any fixed structure the verdict is exactly about its *complete* relation \(S\). A family of affine local laws stays affine or empty under conjunction on identified binary names, fixed substitutions, pins, coordinate bit complements and existential forgetting. These operations respectively combine equations, substitute coordinates, set constants, translate, or take a linear projection. Thus each nonempty instance in this language passes. Conversely, if each primitive nonempty law may be used **by itself** as an admissible presentation, universal passage implies that each such relation is affine by the relation theorem. Unsatisfiable instances cannot be normalized. If a family excludes isolated realizations, the valid test is whether the *admitted visible relations* are affine. A nonlinear predicate can then be hidden. For example, adjoining a zero first input to the graph of AND leaves two affine words, even though the unconstrained graph has four nonaffine words. On those four words the two operands can be revealed first, and four of six orders are uniform; taking the output first asks for a \(3:1\) split. The same phenomenon occurs if a not-all-equal predicate on four names is conjoined with odd parity on those very names: the complete relation is just odd parity. A restricted composite cannot certify the isolated predicate.

A single holder of canonical arity has a sharper result. If its entire relation passes K1–K3, each deletion projects onto all \(2^{n-1}\) words, while K1 excludes the full cube. A single uniformly successful fixed-order picker requires power-of-two cardinality, hence exactly \(2^{n-1}\). Each one-coordinate deletion is then a bijection. At each word, flipping any one bit exchanges membership and nonmembership. The bit-flip walks connect every word to zero, so membership depends precisely on the parity of the full word, in one of its two signs. Conversely either full-support parity equation passes the canonical and picker tests. The zero-coordinate ambient set has no proper nonempty relation. The Boolean argument at one or two names concerns the bare deletion clauses only. The program’s full checker requires at least two loop variables and one contact. Systems of several parity equations, fixed names or the full cube can pass the picker while failing to be one canonical holder. The conclusion for an arbitrary language refers to nonempty, independently realizable holders, and to its allowed operations.

## Extending old states and keeping histories

Let \(p:N\to O\) be the declared restriction from finite new words to old ones, and put \(c(o)=|p^{-1}(o)|\). For nonempty \(N\), uniform new states induce the old law \(c(o)/|N|\). Given an old probability \(\mu\), equal division gives each new word \(w\) mass
\[
L_\mu(w)=\mu(pw)/c(pw).
\]
An old state with no child has refusal mass \(\mu(o)\). Total lifted mass is \(s=\sum_{c(o)>0}\mu(o)\). Its old marginal is exactly \(\mu\) on the surviving old words and zero off them. Thus arbitrary priors are preserved *without loss* iff their support survives; all priors survive iff \(p\) is onto. The unconditioned lift is uniform on \(N\) iff \(\mu(o)=c(o)/|N|\) on all \(O\), including zero-mass dead words. With explicit conditioning on acceptance and \(s>0\), replace \(\mu\) by the prior conditional on \(c>0\); the rejected mass remains recorded separately. For \(\mu=U_O\) and \(O,N\ne\varnothing\), unconditional equality of the two uniform procedures requires **every** \(c(o)\) to be the same positive integer. With dead words and equal positive counts only on the image, conditional agreement loses the excluded prior. An empty \(N\) has no normalized state. With empty \(O\), an ordinary restriction \(N\to O\) can exist only for empty \(N\). A known empty relation differs from the unique empty-coordinate word.

When \(N\) is affine and \(p\) is coordinate restriction, every nonempty fibre is a translate of one kernel, so all fibres over its image have the same size. Surjectivity onto the specified old relation still needs justification. For example, adjoining the equation “old bit equals zero” to an old unrestricted bit leaves an affine two-word enlarged set if a fresh bit is free, but kills half of the uniform old law. Conversely a nonaffine graph of AND over two old bits has one child per old word, so the two uniform stage schemes agree on that step. Its full three-bit reveal test fails. An arbitrary old prior, a nonlinear transition, or old-dependent choices of growth structure also need their own test.

**Criterion for all three throughout one history.** Start uniformly and use coordinate restrictions between nonempty nested stages. The three procedures agree at every stage and in every reveal order if and only if every stage relation is affine and every restriction covers the whole preceding relation. Necessity follows from the relation theorem and survival of the positive uniform mass of each old word. Sufficiency uses the equal kernel fibres of each affine new relation. For a language of structures, quantify this statement over its admissible histories. When every primitive may appear alone, the structure-level converse forces every primitive to be affine. Its growth policy must still supply the proper extension property. This characterization compares the three specified rules; the logical constraints alone do not require any of them to define physical chances.

For a specified finite history of structural allocations with nested state coordinates, each final word has unique ancestors \(o_0,\ldots,o_t\). Stepwise local division gives weight \(\mu(o_0)\prod_{j<t}c_j(o_j)^{-1}\) to the path. Direct division of each old state's mass among its *final* words gives \(\mu(o_0)/d_t(o_0)\), where \(d_t\) is its count of distinct final descendants. With positive starting mass and no unrecorded refusals these two agree iff the product of child counts equals \(d_t(o_0)\) along every final path with that ancestor. Uniform final weights give the old marginal \(d_t(o)/|O_t|\). Equal numbers of final descendants across starting words are enough for that marginal, yet can leave the intermediate law or individual paths different. Requiring uniformity at *each* stage with no dead old words requires constant positive immediate fibre size at *each* transition. If there are intermediate dead ends, the formula still applies to completed paths; the missing mass is the sum of the refused partial-path weights. A structural allocation chosen with a history-dependent probability multiplies the same formula by its actual conditional probability at each choice. When distinct histories lose their labels and merge at an endpoint, add all their path weights. Counting distinct endpoints uniformly or counting successful histories uniformly can each give a third result. Ordinary growth keeps the old constraints and allocates private fresh distinctions to each same-round birth. For a parity birth with \(f\ge1\) fresh bits every old word has \(2^{f-1}\) extensions. A permitted zero-fresh birth is already implied and has one. Multiplication across the distinct fresh sets gives a positive constant for each old word, independently of future rounds. Each finite reached parity relation is affine and its new words cover all the old ones. With a uniform start, all three weightings therefore coincide along every completed ordinary parity history with that fixed presentation and allocation. A stall adds no completed transition. At closure the last allowed set still exists. The prior proof establishes surjectivity under both ordinary laws. [Ordinary extension and allocation proof](../../ENTANGLEMENT-READ-20261120-001A/ENTANGLEMENT-READ-20261120-001A-P001_common_state/proof.md)

A not-all-equal birth with \(f>0\) fresh distinctions has \(2^f-1\) completions when its nonempty old inputs all agree, and \(2^f\) when some differ. A unary input always belongs to the first case. Products across simultaneous independent sets of fresh bits determine the exact fibre size; a whole presentation might make that product constant. U on each new stage weights old events according to those possibly varying counts. L keeps the old law and in general gives unequal weights to new words. A fair feasible reveal can favor different words at different orders. In one isolated ternary not-all-equal knot, after revealing first bit zero, the next bit zero leaves one word and one leaves two. The uniform target needs \(1/3\), \(2/3\) there. The fixed-order fair picker gives each of the two words whose first two bits agree weight \(1/4\) and the other four \(1/8\). All six orders fail and their equal mixture gives \(1/6\) per word by permutation and complement symmetry. For a partially fixed knot with every known input equal and \(r>0\) fresh bits left, uniform conditional sampling chooses the value opposite to that common bit next with probability \(2^{r-1}/(2^r-1)\). These are exact weighting requirements. Evaluating mere feasibility can itself require looking ahead in the present relation. Relative completion counts can be precomputed or replaced by rejection; proposing full independent unbiased words and rejecting violations also yields \(U_S\) on acceptance, with probability \(|S|/2^n\) per proposal. Local conditional rejection implements equal division at an ordinary birth without future-stage information. None of these facts is a complexity lower bound or a native chance rule.

## A physical change arbitrarily many meetings away

In the repaired family let \(m=2k\ge2\). The old holders \(0,\ldots,m+1\) form a chain with a pair of distinct shared names at each successive meeting. Add holder \(W\) with a separate pair of names to each end, making a cycle of doubled meetings. Each end holds exactly one additional, loose name, \(p\) or \(q\). Only the ends are live. They are both neighbors of the fully spent \(W\), which supplies exactly their binary proposal. No other holder meets both for \(m\ge2\). Both ends pay their unique loose names. There is one funded binary birth, with no demand from other new siblings. Parity already implies \(p=q\) because adding all old parity equations leaves exactly these two names. The ordinary exception for an implied last birth gives zero fresh names. Not-all-equal admits the equal-input case and requires one fresh name. The new child gives one or two completions according as \(p=q\) or \(p\ne q\). The [ordinary operator](../../../research/ORDINARY-DYNAMICS-20261120-001/end_operator.md) and the [independent physical runs](../../../research/UNREAD-COMPANION-AND-ORDER-FREE-WEIGHTING-20261121-001B/growth_weights.py) implement this rule.

All old local holders and the new not-all-equal child are lawful at every size in this argument. The two-name parity closure has a separate arity verdict. For NAE fix any allowed word on an old holder and set all unassigned doubled links to a mixed pair, for example 01. All remaining old holders contain at least one such mixed link. Assign the free loose inputs as needed and choose a legal fresh bit at the new child. To extend any child word, fix \(p,q\) from it and set all old doubled links to mixed. Each local declared relation is therefore realized in full. For parity, deleting any one holder leaves its other holders connected, both before and after the binary birth. With an old word specified on the deleted holder, the residual system has a solution: use a spanning tree to meet its equations from the ends inward. At the start there is at least one remaining loose bit to set the final parity; after closure the deleted holder's own even equation supplies the required total. Consequently each even holder word extends. The two-place closing parity equation obeys the three Boolean deletion clauses, but the program’s checker rejects it as a new canonical knot because its arity is below three. The ordinary engine expressly permits this implied closing row. Every other parity holder here and the three-place NAE child have enough openings for the canonical check. A fixed far event below is a joint read on the two named distinctions of the central link, not a singleton. Every singleton bit remains fair by complement under NAE.

Here is an exact count for every \(k\). Order doubled-link words \(00,01,10,11\). Let \(T_{uv}=1\) when the concatenation of \(u,v\) is not constant, otherwise zero. Let \(L_s(u,v)\) use the same test on \(u,v,s\). Put \(M=\operatorname{diag}(1,0,0,1)\); it marks an equal central pair. For fixed old loose values \(s,t\), the total old count is \(\operatorname{tr}(T^{2k}L_t T L_s)\), and the event count replaces \(T^{2k}\) by \(T^k M T^k\). This is direct summation around the doubled cycle. Let \(Z,E\) be the respective counts summed over \(s,t\), and \(D,F\) their sums with signs \((-1)^{s+t}\). The original uniform-stage probability is \(E/Z\), the new uniform-stage probability is \((3E-F)/(3Z-D)\), and the lift preserves \(E/Z\).

For explicit verification, separate the even combinations \(e=(00+11)/\sqrt2,\ u=(01+10)/\sqrt2\) and the odd ones \(o=(00-11)/\sqrt2,\ v=(01-10)/\sqrt2\). These are simply normalized changes of basis for four ordinary counting columns. On \(e,u\), \(T_0=\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}1&2\\2&2\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)\) and \(L_0+L_1=\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}3&4\\4&4\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)\); on \(o,v\) both are diagonal with entries \(-1,0\). The difference \(L_0-L_1\) sends \(e\) to \(-o\) and \(o\) to \(-e\). With \(H=T_0^k=\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\b&a+b/2\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)\), the even block of \((L_0+L_1)T(L_0+L_1)\) is \(C=\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}89&100\\100&112\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)\). Thus, writing \(M_0=\operatorname{diag}(1,0)\),
\[
 Z=\operatorname{tr}(H^2 C)-1,\quad E=\operatorname{tr}(H M_0 H C)-1,\quad
 D=1-(H^2)_{11},\quad F=1-(H M_0 H)_{11}.
\]
Here subscripts 11 denote the top left entry. Since \(a^2+ab/2-b^2=\det H=(-2)^k\), multiplication gives
\[
 ED-ZF=8\{2(-2)^k(7a^2+16ab+7b^2)-(2a+3b)(7a+9b)\}.
\]
The positive denominator of the change is \(Z(3Z-D)\). For odd \(k\) the numerator is strictly negative. For even \(k\ge2\), \(2(-2)^k\ge8\), and the coefficients of \(8(7a^2+16ab+7b^2)\) strictly exceed those of \((2a+3b)(7a+9b)\). Both \(a,b\) are positive. The probability changes at every \(k\). The shortest chain of holder meetings from the new birth to a holder of the marked central link has length \(k+1\): the birth first meets an end and the central link is \(k\) steps from its nearest end. \(W\) connects the same two ends, adding no shorter route. No unbounded-growth premise is involved; increasing separation changes the finite source. In the original candidate the two ends instead directly share just one extra distinction, without \(W\). No old holder meets both ends for the enrolled lengths. The paid unary births give a constant number of completions per old state. Their ordinary action and central marginal provide the matched negative control.
