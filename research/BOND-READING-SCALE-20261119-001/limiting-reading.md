# The limiting reading and an upper bound in both units of size

Write \(J\) for the number of ordinary junctions and \(n\) for the number of literal distinctions at observation. The exterior is an extra vertex in the classical graph description. All results use the finite homogeneous parity law, an observed stage of positive age, and one specified renaming fixing each name on every live support. An individual stage can need fewer marks than any worst-case bound. Write \(R_{\rm all}(n)\), or \(R_{\rm all}(J)\) when the unit is junctions, for the least current order sufficient on all stages of **at most** the indicated size to carry all future bond readings of all orders with a coherent extension. A fixed target such as future pairs defines its own, possibly smaller, function.

## The full reading characterizes transport

Let \(C\) be the binary span of the ordinary junction equations. A bond is a support-minimal nonzero member. The minimum marked size \(K(S)\), infinity included, names a bond exactly on the test \(K(S)=|S|\) for nonempty \(S\). All such supports generate \(C\): in any nonempty member, choose a bond it contains and add it in binary arithmetic. This strictly reduces the support and ends at zero. Thus the yes/no answers to the equality tests already determine the entire equation space, and thereby the allowed states. This reconstructs the span. A particular list of physical junctions giving that span is a different object.

There is a proper, invariant family. Use only the nonempty subsets of **shared** distinctions in those equality tests and include the supplied live junction equations. Every exhausted junction equation is supported on shared distinctions. Its decomposition into bonds above stays wholly on shared distinctions. Together these exhausted and live equations generate \(C\). Equivalently, omit every set containing any loose distinction. Even if only one fixed name \(e\) and one known live row \(L\) containing it are singled out, equality tests solely on sets avoiding \(e\) give all the bonds of \(C\) with zero at \(e\); these span that entire restricted space. Adding \(L\) recovers \(C\). Neither construction requires a remembered birth or a choice between tied names.

The growing end and a matched span carry every later round. The complete-round law determines the parent groups and new supports from the end, the old rows are carried by the matching, and the new rows extend the two spans correspondingly. Repeating gives one consistent naming for all future stages. Conversely an unmatched old span can be detected by a later higher-order reading whenever there is a later stage. In an open round each new equation has its own fresh distinction. A combination supported entirely on old names therefore uses no such equation. In a closing round the sole relation without a fresh name is the equation on all old loose distinctions, already the sum of the old rows. Consequently the space of equations supported entirely on the original names at any later stage is exactly \(C\). Each original bond stays a bond on that support. Given different old spans, some bond in one is no equation in the other and its equality test differs at every subsequent stage. Minimum sizes for other old marked sets can change. This also explains why a later mismatch on a large set need never appear on a pair. ## Focusing one bond and counting junctions

If a bond \(B\) is a physical junction support, choose one name from each distinct neighbor on its support and call this set \(T\). Any cut containing \(T\) crosses every parallel copy there, hence contains \(B\), so the only bond through \(T\) is \(B\). For an arbitrary table the union of its smallest bonds through \(T\) is exactly the set of \(e\) with \(K(T\cup\{e\})=K(T)\). If that union has exactly \(K(T)\) names it is the unique minimum. This argument transfers the literal image of \(B\) to any matching table at order \(|T|+1\). One can instead use the single equality at \(B\) at order \(|B|\). Both follow solely from the readings. An exhausted ordinary junction has only ordinary neighbors, hence at most \(J-1\). Thus reading through \(J\) always suffices for all equations and later orders, including arbitrary multiplicities.

More precisely, if a fixed map preserving order \(r\) changes the span, at least two exhausted stars fail to lie in the rival span. All the live rows and the exterior row are fixed, and the sum of all rows including the exterior is zero, which rules out one failure in isolation. Each such star has at least \(r\) distinct neighbors and at least \(r+1\) literal names. In particular \(J\ge r+1\). Every ordinary junction has degree at least three and the open exterior has degree at least two. Summing the two bad star sizes and all remaining degrees gives
\[
               2n\ge 2(r+1)+3(J-2)+2\ge 5r+1.
\]

## A stronger bound using the least missing equation

Suppose two cut spaces on the same names have identical minimum-bond readings through \(r\) and different spans. Let \(b\) be the least weight in their symmetric difference. Then
\[
                          r<\lfloor b/2\rfloor+3 .
\]
The argument works for two graphic presentations as well as for one graph under a permutation. It does not require any prescribed generating rows to be recognized.

First, in a connected weighted graph, every cut of size at most \(b\) is in the binary span of minimum terminal-pair cuts of size at most \(b\). Here is a proof of the cut-tree fact used in this step. Begin with one block containing all vertices. Keep the blocks on a tree, with every existing tree link recording a minimum cut separating an original vertex pair on its two sides. Within a nonsingleton block \(W\), choose a pair \(s,t\) of minimum local connectivity among the pairs in that block. A minimum \(s,t\) cut can leave each neighbor component \(P\) of the tree minus \(W\) intact. Indeed \(P\) is a minimum-cut shore for a pair \(p\in P,\ q\notin P\). Put the new cut shore \(S\) on the side containing \(p\), complementing it if needed. Submodularity gives \(d(S\cup P)+d(S\cap P)\le d(S)+d(P)\). The intersection still separates \(p,q\), so moving all of \(P\) into \(S\) cannot increase cost. The components \(P\) are disjoint. Split \(W\) on the resulting cut and reattach each neighbor component on its original side. Its old partition of the graph is preserved. Continue to singleton blocks. The final endpoints of any new link descend from its original two subblocks of \(W\); their local connectivity is at least the chosen minimum in \(W\) and at most the weight of this very cut. Thus every final tree link supplies a minimum cut of its own endpoints. For any cut \(\delta(S_0)\), add the fundamental tree cuts at the links with endpoints on opposite sides of \(S_0\). A tree path crosses their partition boundaries oddly exactly when its two ends have different membership, giving exactly the original cut. Each fundamental cut used has size at most \(d(S_0)\). This proves the fact directly.

Choose the source side with a size-\(b\) equation missing on the other side. From this basis fact there must be a minimum \(s,t\) cut \(B\) of size \(b\) missing there. Take \(b\) distinction-disjoint \(s,t\) paths from the ordinary unit-capacity maximum-flow theorem. They cross \(B\) at exactly one name apiece. Put \(k=\lfloor b/2\rfloor+1\) of those names in \(T\). Any bond containing \(T\) that leaves \(s,t\) on the same side must cross those \(k\) paths at least twice each. Thus \(K(T)=b\), and every size-\(b\) witness is a minimum \(s,t\) cut.

If \(r\ge k+2\), choose a rival minimizing bond \(B'\) through \(T\). For every pair \(e,f\in B'\), the matching value \(K(T\cup\{e,f\})=b\) provides a source minimum \(s,t\) cut containing all those names. Crossing requirements in the family of minimum \(s,t\) cuts have the following property: pairwise compatibility implies simultaneous compatibility. To see this, orient each minimum cut by its \(s\)-shore. These shores are closed under intersection and union by cut submodularity. There are no edges between the two differences of any two minimizing shores, since that would give a strict improvement. Each crossable name therefore has a fixed direction whenever it crosses. Its witnesses are the interval between the least and greatest minimum shores that cross it. Pairwise intersecting such intervals have a common shore by taking the union of all lower endpoints. This remains valid when some common marks \(T\) are already specified. Hence a single true source minimum contains all of \(B'\). Both have size \(b\), so \(B'\) is also a source bond. The word \(B\mathbin{\triangle}B'\) is now in the source and missing in the rival, of weight at most \(2(b-k)<b\). This contradicts the choice of \(b\). [Earlier interval proof](../../proofs/GROWN-END-READING-20261119-001A/GROWN-END-READING-20261119-001A-P001_transport/proof.md#all-shortest-bonds-at-any-one-distinction-can-be-read)

For the marked single-stage transport problem, both bad exhausted stars have at least \(b\ge 2r-4\) names. The same degree sum now gives \(2n\ge4r+3J-12\ge7r-9\). In particular a uniform sufficient order, with the value at least two understood for the pair reading, is
\[
             \min\!\left(J,\ 1+\left\lfloor(2n-1)/5\right\rfloor,\
                                  1+\left\lfloor(2n+9)/7\right\rfloor\right).
\]
A small maximum size of exhausted junctions, or a small maximum number of distinct neighbors, can give a much lower stage-specific bound. The inequalities are sufficient bounds, without an optimality claim about their constants.

## Scope of the size law

The explicit families in this study give \(R_{\rm all}(J)=\Theta(J)\) in the unrestricted law. For literal distinctions they give \(\Omega(\sqrt n)\le R_{\rm all}(n)\le O(n)\), with the uniform ceilings above. The lower bound is on a cumulative size function: choose the largest integer parameter whose proven size limit fits the budget. The one-round failure on an old pair from the preceding study already gives the square-root lower bound when the future target is pairs alone, even under a single ordinary meeting per pair of junctions. The quadratic cost is necessary for the specified two-hub shrinking construction used in that proof. It is an open question whether such a cost holds throughout the unrestricted law.
