# One binary two-output addition and the inverse on cycles

**Theorem.** Start with the ternary seed. Allow one addition on two parents with two fresh distinctions, and let every other addition make three fresh distinctions. The exceptional addition may occur anywhere, with any legal parents. For every finite source so obtained, a single renaming that preserves pair minima through six to any reachable mixed rival identifies all actual junction supports, the allowed states, and every legal next group. The statement includes the case without the exceptional addition.

Here \(K(e,f)=D(e,f)+1\) is the minimum cardinality of a bond containing different distinctions \(e,f\). Entry values through six mean that the observer knows each value at most six and which exceed six. The rival may use two or three fresh distinctions at every addition. The classical incidence graph is used in the proof. Its ordinary vertices are junctions, a shared bit joins its two holders, and a loose bit has one ordinary holder. Cuts wire loose ends to the exterior. To test whether bits meet at a holder, give the loose ends separate leaves. The ordinary graph is simple and bipartite. Its stars are bonds and an independent basis of the parity equation space. A nonzero equation splits into disjoint bonds by successively removing a contained minimal equation. [Incidence and cut-space facts](../../VARIABLE-OUTPUT-READER-20261118-001D/VARIABLE-OUTPUT-READER-20261118-001D-P001_separator_inverse/separator.md)

We prove a structural form as well. Suppose a source has the seed as its only ternary site and satisfies the following three conditions.

1. Each bond of size at most six has at most two sites on its ordinary shore.
2. For each star \(S\) of size \(d=5,6\), two of its bits have \(K=d\), and no bit outside \(S\) has \(K\le d\) to both of them.
3. In the graph \(G_4\) induced by the four-place sites, each edge belongs to at most one simple cycle. Such a graph is called a cactus in classical graph theory. Different components are allowed.

Then the same conclusion against unrestricted rivals holds. We first verify the local conditions in the one-exception theorem, then prove the reverse inclusion for the structural form. A family with arbitrarily many binary two-output additions satisfies the conditions at the end.

## Source bonds and a pair identifying each high star

In the one-exception source every nonseed site has degree at least four. Joining a selected site of parent count \(p\) and output count \(h\) to the previously selected sites in birth order adds \(p+h-2j\) boundary bits, where \(j\le p\) is its number of earlier selected neighbors. The increment is nonnegative since \(h\ge p\). For any three selected sites the boundary is at least \(3+4+4-2\cdot2=7\), by bipartiteness. Consequently every shore containing at least three sites has boundary at least seven. This argument also applies to any number of binary two-output additions when all other sites make three outputs.

A bond through size six is therefore a single star or the sum of two adjacent stars of degrees \(s,t\), of size \(s+t-2\). Two distinct bits of a fixed high star at \(u\) have \(K=\deg(u)\). Indeed, a two-site cut involving \(u\) costs strictly more, and a two-site cut avoiding \(u\) would need to use two distinct same-color neighbors of \(u\). If a bit is loose it has no other ordinary endpoint. In particular a loose bit of \(u\) has no outside bit within this threshold and can be paired with any other bit of its star.

For a used bit \(uv\) and threshold \(d=\deg(u)\), every close outside bit meets \(v\), or meets a neighbor \(s\ne u\) of \(v\) satisfying \(\deg(v)+\deg(s)-2\le d\). Call these possible ordinary holders the set for the arm \(uv\). Two arms certify the star if their sets are disjoint and have no ordinary meeting between them. In this case the rival bond attaining their common minimum \(d\) must be the whole star, since none of its other bits could be outside it.

For \(d=5\), the site is born binary with three outputs. Choose two outputs whose children are different from the sole exceptional site, if all are used. Each chosen child's set is just itself. The only way to enlarge it would be a four-place child adjacent to the seed. A nonexceptional four-place child is unary on \(u\) and cannot meet the older seed. The two singleton sites have the same color. This proves condition 2 at five.

For \(d=6\), a high endpoint \(a\) always has set \(\{a\}\), enlarged to \(\{a,o\}\) precisely if \(a\) is five-place and meets the seed \(o\). In particular two high endpoints of \(u\) certify its star if either endpoint avoids the seed or both are six-place. For a regular unary four-place output child \(p\), the set consists of \(p\) and its four-place children. Its only earlier neighbor is \(u\).

The seed's first ordinary neighbor was born unary, with three fresh bits, so it is four-place. In the whole source at most two high sites can meet the seed. Consequently three distinct high endpoints of arms of \(u\) always contain a certifying pair. This particular test uses only condition 1 and the uniqueness of the ternary seed, since that uniqueness already forces the first unary addition to make three outputs. It works for any combination of three incident high neighbors, including input parents.

If the exception precedes \(u\), or is absent, every four-place output child of \(u\) is unary, as are its own four-place children. Two unary output children certify the star. Their low children are distinct, each has only its own earlier parent, and all of these grandchildren have the same color. Three high output children also suffice by the seed bound. With one unary child \(p\) and two high children, the high pair works unless both meet the seed and at least one, say \(b\), is five-place. This \(b\) was born on \(u,o\). No four-place unary child of \(p\) can meet \(b\) in either birth order. The seed does not meet \(p\), and has the same color as the children of \(p\). Thus \(up,ub\) certifies the star in this last case. [All-three output argument](../../VARIABLE-OUTPUT-READER-20261118-001H/VARIABLE-OUTPUT-READER-20261118-001H-P001_high_junctions/high_inverse.md#a-pair-that-forces-each-source-junctions-equation)

Now let the exception \(w\) follow \(u\), and suppose again that all outputs of \(u\) are used. Let \(a\) be the youngest of its three input parents. For each of the other two parents there was a witness already adjacent to \(a\) at its birth. Indeed, any common neighbor born after both members of a pair would already have needed an older witness for that pair. These two witnesses are different, since a witness common to all three parents would have forbidden the join at \(u\). Hence \(a\) had at least two inputs, and is high since \(w\) comes later. If at least two output children of \(u\) are high, they and \(a\) give three high endpoints, which suffice.

For a regular unary output child \(p\), the pair \(au,up\) certifies the star unless \(w\) was born precisely on \(a,p\). The sites \(a,p\) have the same color. A later regular unary child of \(p\) cannot meet the older \(a\), and an exceptional child of \(p\) can meet \(a\) only by taking them both as parents. If the seed is in the set for \(au\), it neither meets \(p\) nor equals or meets any four-place child of \(p\). With at most one high output child there are at least two regular unary output children if \(w\) is not a child of \(u\). At most one of them can be the second parent of \(w\) together with \(a\). If \(w\) is a child of \(u\), at least one other output child is regular unary, and \(w\) cannot have parents \(a,p\) as well as \(u\). This proves condition 2 for every six-place site.

Finally \(G_4\) in this one-exception source is a forest or has a single cycle of length four. Before \(w\) is born each component is a tree of unary four-place sites, directed away from its oldest vertex. A cycle first arises only if the two parents of \(w\) are already in the same four-place tree. Let \(b\) be the younger parent and \(a\) the older. At birth the sole old neighbor \(t\) of \(b\) already witnessed the pair \(a,b\). It is four-place, since otherwise \(b\) would be the oldest vertex of this tree and could not have \(a\) in it. The unique tree path is \(b,t,a\). Adding \(w\) closes exactly this cycle of length four. Later unary four-place vertices cannot create another cycle. This verifies condition 3.

## What an extra equation would force

We now assume the three structural conditions and compare with an unrestricted rival. Write \(C_H,C_R\) for their binary equation spaces after transporting the names. Condition 1 excludes a four-bit bond on a large source shore, so the short-equation theorem gives equality of the three- and four-bit equations in both directions. The seed is the common unique ternary site, since ternary equations in any reachable presentation are ternary stars. Condition 2 forces each remaining source star in the rival through the minimum of the specified pair. Thus \(C_H\subseteq C_R\). [Three- and four-bit inverse](../../VARIABLE-OUTPUT-READER-20261118-001F/VARIABLE-OUTPUT-READER-20261118-001F-P001_finite_interval/structural.md#recovering-every-equation-through-size-four)

If the inclusion is strict, take a minimum-weight \(W\in C_R\setminus C_H\). Some rival generator of weight at most six lies outside, so \(|W|=5\) or \(6\). The unique ternary bond excludes the two disjoint ternary bonds required by a nonminimal rival word of either weight. Each such word is therefore a bond. For each source star \(S\), translation by \(S\) stays outside \(C_H\), implying \(|W\cap S|\le\lfloor |S|/2\rfloor\). If a six-place star had overlap three, \(W\) and \(W\mathbin\triangle S\) would be rival bonds of the same size. A bit in \(W\setminus S\) would be within \(K\le6\) of all six members of \(S\), contradicting condition 2. Thus each site contains at most two selected bits and the seed at most one. These restrictions apply to every equal-weight translate. If two four-place sites each contain two selected bits, their meeting, if any, must be selected. Otherwise translating by the first star gives the second three selected bits at the same weight.

Discard a possible seed bit, leaving \(Q\). If \(|W|=5\), the at least four remaining bits are pairwise incident to a common ordinary site for each pair. A cross-pair in a short two-site sum of size at most five would need a bit incident to the seed, since the only such degree pair is \((3,4)\). In a triangle-free simple graph three or more pairwise incident edges have a common vertex, as an edge meeting the other ends of two intersecting edges would close a triangle. This contradicts the limit of two per site.

For \(|W|=6\), there are five or six bits in \(Q\). The only offsite cross-pair avoiding a seed bit comes from two adjacent four-place stars. Hence any two of these bits meet or have four-place endpoints that meet each other. Every bit of \(Q\) has a four-place endpoint, since otherwise its at most two ordinary endpoints could each meet at most one other selected bit. For \(e\in Q\), write \(V_e\) for its four-place endpoints, one vertex or the ends of one edge. If two such sets do not meet and have no joining edge, the two bits must share their sole non-four-place endpoint. In particular each bit can have at most one such partner, by the limit of two per site.

Let \(J\) be the subgraph of \(G_4\) induced by all these endpoints. If it had more than one component, every bit could have at most one partner outside its own component. Two occupied components are therefore impossible with at least five bits. Moreover \(J\) contains a cycle. Otherwise, in the tree \(J\) thicken each \(V_e\) by half an incident edge in every direction, retaining the joining edge if \(V_e\) has two vertices. Two resulting closed subtrees intersect exactly for meeting or adjacent endpoint sets. If two are disjoint, each of the other at least three must meet both, since both disjoint subtrees have already used their one possible disjoint partner. Every subtree connecting them contains a fixed ordinary vertex between the two endpoint sets, which are at distance at least two. Only bits actually having that vertex as an endpoint contain it after thickening. If all subtrees meet pairwise, root the tree and take the deepest of their nearest points to the root. The root path to a pairwise intersection with the subtree giving this point shows that every subtree contains it. A common ordinary vertex supports at most two bits, and an interior point of an edge supports at most four from its two ends. Both alternatives are impossible. [Forest argument](../../VARIABLE-OUTPUT-READER-20261118-001H/VARIABLE-OUTPUT-READER-20261118-001H-P001_high_junctions/high_inverse.md#an-extra-equation-would-need-too-many-close-pairs)

## Confinement to one cycle

Fix a simple cycle \(C\) of \(J\). All its vertices are incident to \(Q\). Every component of \(G_4\setminus V(C)\) in this connected part attaches to at most one vertex of \(C\), since two attachment vertices would give another cycle sharing edges with \(C\). The cycle has even length at least four and no chord.

Suppose a bit \(e\in Q\) does not meet \(C\). Its four-place endpoints lie in one branch attached at \(v\in C\). For any bit \(f\in Q\) meeting \(C\) away from \(v\) and not meeting \(v\), its four-place endpoints are neither equal nor adjacent to those of \(e\). Therefore it must share the unique non-four-place endpoint of \(e\). There can be at most one such \(f\), and it meets only one cycle vertex. All other bits meeting \(C\) meet \(v\). They cover at most \(v\) and its two cycle neighbors. Since the entire cycle is occupied, it must have length four, both cycle edges incident to \(v\) must be selected, and the one exceptional \(f\) is attached at the opposite site. Every further bit avoiding \(C\) must be in a branch at \(v\) to be close to both selected cycle edges, which have only \(v\) in common and each have only four-place endpoints. Like \(e\) it would have to share the non-four-place endpoint of \(f\), which already holds two selected bits. Thus the two cycle edges, \(e\), and \(f\) would exhaust \(Q\), contradicting \(|Q|\ge5\). Every bit of \(Q\) consequently meets \(C\).

Translate \(W\) by stars at vertices of \(C\), considering only final words of the same size six. Every resulting nonseed bit still meets \(C\), since all changes occur on stars of \(C\). Its four-place endpoints are at distance at most one from \(C\) in \(G_4\). In a cactus, paths from any other cycle towards \(C\) must leave that cycle at its one attachment towards \(C\). This follows by removing the vertices of the other cycle when the cycles are disjoint. When they intersect, a second way out would close a cycle sharing edges with the first. Since ordinary cycles have length at least four, the other cycle has a vertex at distance at least two from \(C\). The tree argument just proved again requires a fully occupied cycle after translation. Thus the same \(C\) is fully occupied in every such translate.

Choose a translate with as few selected edges of \(C\) as possible, say \(k\). They form a matching. If two shared a vertex, translating by its four-place star would preserve six, cancel both edges and add only off-cycle bits. Call a selected nonseed bit incident to just one vertex of \(C\) an attachment at that vertex. Attachments at two distinct nonadjacent vertices must share their non-four-place endpoint. Indeed their four-place endpoints can neither coincide nor be adjacent, by the single attachment of each branch to \(C\). In particular, if both sites have attachments, they have exactly one apiece, using the same outside holder. Two different bits joining the same pair of ordinary sites are impossible.

* If \(k=0\), each cycle vertex has an attachment. For length four each opposite pair has just one at each site, leaving only four bits. For length at least six an attachment would have to share its outside endpoint with the attachments at three or more nonneighbors. Both contradict the bounds on \(Q\).
* If \(k\ge1\), fix a selected edge \(01\), numbering the cycle cyclically. An occupied vertex at distance at least three along the cycle from \(\{0,1\}\) is impossible. A bit there can approach \(01\) by at most one cycle step over its own length, and a branch can attach only at this same vertex. Since \(01\) has two four-place endpoints, proximity requires equal or adjacent four-place endpoints. This excludes all even lengths at least eight. At length six, vertices \(3,4\) force the selected edges \(23,45\) respectively to approach \(01\). In the three-edge alternating matching, each possible attachment site is at distance two from the endpoints of one of these edges. Hence no attachment can occur and \(|Q|\le3\).
* At length four call the vertices \(A,B,C',D\) in cyclic order. If \(k=2\), the matching covers all four sites. To give five nonseed bits, attachments must occur at three or more sites, each then containing two selected bits. Among the two unselected cycle edges, one joins two of these sites, which was excluded by translation.
* At length four with \(k=1\), let \(AB\) be selected and let \(a,b,c,d\) count nonseed attachments at the four sites. We have \(a,b\le1\), \(c,d\in\{1,2\}\). The opposite-site condition gives \(a+c\le2\) and \(b+d\le2\). As \(|Q|\ge5\), both are equalities, \(|Q|=5\), and there is a single seed bit in \(W\). If \(a=0\), then \(c=2\). For \(b=1\) the absent edge \(BC'\) joins two saturated sites, and for \(b=0\) one has \(d=2\) and the absent edge \(C'D\) does so. The case \(b=0,a=1\) is symmetric. Thus \(a=b=c=d=1\). Translate by the star at \(A\), which contains just \(AB\) and the attachment at \(A\) among the selected bits. Its new external attachment is nonseed since the previous seed bit persists elsewhere. Both the old and the replacement attachments at \(A\) must meet the unchanged opposite attachment at \(C'\) at its unique outside endpoint. This would give two different bits between \(A\) and that outside site.

Every case excludes \(W\), proving \(C_H=C_R\).

## Actual supports and next groups

Every rival star is now a source bond of size at most six. Besides source stars themselves, only an adjacent sum involving the seed or an adjacent sum of two four-place stars has that size. The first contains two bits of the common actual seed and cannot be another rival star. A sum of two adjacent four-place stars shares three bits with each, so neither underlying site could itself be present as an actual rival star. In the basis of actual source stars every rival generator therefore has either zero or two coordinates on the missing source sites. These generators cannot span the unit vector of a missing site. Equality of the equation spaces rules out any missing site, and their independent dimensions then agree. Identical actual supports determine the physical loose bits, their distinct holders and all earlier-common-neighbor tests in the current incidence. This gives the same legal groups in both directions. [Incidence and the legal menu](../../VARIABLE-OUTPUT-READER-20261118-001D/VARIABLE-OUTPUT-READER-20261118-001D-P001_separator_inverse/inverse.md)

## A family with arbitrarily many exceptional additions

Permit arbitrarily many binary two-output additions, each on two unary four-place children \(p,q\) of the same four-place site \(t\). Require that neither ordinary meeting \(tp,tq\) already lies on a cycle in the current four-place graph. All other additions make three outputs. The boundary monotonicity above gives condition 1. A new unary four-place site creates no cycle. At each binary exception the old path \(p,t,q\) consists of bridges, so it is the unique old simple path between the two parents. Inserting the new site creates precisely one new four-cycle. Induction gives condition 3.

No high site can parent such an exception, and no unary child of a high site can parent one either, since its only parent would have to be four-place. Thus for a high site its four-place output children are unary, and all their four-place children are unary. At five the two-output certificate above works with any two used outputs. At six the three-output argument applies at each high site with the same three sets of possible ordinary holders. This gives condition 2 even for six-place stars with no loose bits remaining. The structural theorem applies against every mixed rival.

For an explicit supply of the cycles, take a four-place unary site \(t_1\) and make separate unary children \(p_i,q_i\) on two outputs of \(t_i\). Make the two-output exception on \(p_i,q_i\), and the next unary \(t_{i+1}\) on the third output of \(t_i\). Each exceptional pair has the witness \(t_i\), and its two earlier meetings are new bridges. Any finite number of repetitions is possible. The same construction can start on one spare bit in the ordinary seven-site prefix consisting of three unary children of the seed and one three-output pair join for each pair of children. The three pair-join sites retain a legal loose triple, witnessed by those three unary sites. This supplies examples with both unboundedly many cycles and an actual triple to read.
