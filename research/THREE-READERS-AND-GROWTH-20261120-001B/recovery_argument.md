# Reading the holders from single changes

In a finite connected not-all-equal presentation with every rule necessary, the allowed states determine who holds each loose distinction. Forgetting a recovered holder then reveals the holders that met it. This identifies every holding whenever at least one distinction is loose. Across closed presentations the only ambiguity, at any size, is the six pair names of four holders, with the two choices described below. All these conclusions concern the model with the physical gates in the [frozen study](preregistration.md).

Write \(S\) for the allowed states on fixed names. Every name occurs in one or two different holders, every holder has at least three distinct names, and the projection onto each holder is exactly the set of its nonconstant words. Erasing any one holder's rule while retaining every name enlarges \(S\). In particular, no two holders coincide, and neither can contain the other. A proper subset of a holder is completely free. A *candidate* is a set of at least three names on which the projection of \(S\) is exactly the not-all-equal law.

## Completing a partly specified state

In any ordered deduction, a holder with some fixed values of both kinds is already satisfied. A holder with fixed values all equal to \(c\) and one remaining free name forces that name to \(1-c\). A holder with every value fixed and equal gives a conflict. After each forced assignment, its holder is satisfied for the rest of the deduction.

Consider a system of this type in which all full not-all-equal rules have at least three places, except possibly two rules with two places. Fix any set of names to any values. **If repeated forced assignments stop without a conflict, the remaining partial state extends.** To see this, discard the satisfied rules. Each remaining rule has at least two free places. A rule with a fixed value needs only one occurrence of its opposite value among the free places. A rule with no fixed value still needs both.

For the argument only, represent each free name by the pair of remaining holders using it. Give a name used once its own unrestricted end. A name used nowhere can take either value. In a connected part, the remaining names can receive both colours at every holder of at least two names except when the entire part is an odd cycle of degree two. Here is the colouring argument. If there are odd degrees, add one temporary common end meeting each odd-degree holder or end once. Start a walk at that end, pass through every meeting exactly once and return to the start, then colour successive meetings alternately. Each visit to an original holder pairs an arrival and a departure of opposite colours. Removing a temporary meeting from a holder with original odd degree at least three leaves at least one intact pair. When all degrees are even, start at a holder of degree at least four if one exists. It keeps an intact internal pair even when the first and last meetings have the same colour. If every degree is two, the even cycle alternates. On an odd cycle the two meetings at one chosen holder can instead have its one required colour, with opposite colours everywhere else. This works whenever at least one rule in that cycle already had a fixed value. An odd cycle made solely of full two-place rules would need at least three such rules, because one name cannot occur twice at the same holder. The hypotheses permit at most two. The classical colouring lemma supplies the completion argument. Its use here permits a deduction of inconsistency to be transferred back to the unshortened physical rules. [Bondy and Murty, *Graph Theory with Applications*, Lemma 6.1.1](https://www.zib.de/userpage/groetschel/teaching/WS1314/BondyMurtyGTWA.pdf)

For example, take the ternaries \(pab,qbc,rca\), then fix \(p,q,r\) to zero. Among \(a,b,c\), every pair now needs at least one 1. All three equal to 1 works. Changing these three requirements to full two-place not-all-equal rules would make completion impossible. This is the precise exceptional case in the colouring argument.

## When two distinctions have a common holder

For names \(x,y\), look for one fixed word on **all** the other names for which exactly \(01,10,11\) are allowed on \(x,y\). Such a word exists if and only if some holder contains both \(x,y\).

If \(00\) is forbidden while \(01\) and \(10\) are allowed, any rule violated at \(00\) has to contain both names. For the converse, choose a common holder \(H\) and fix every other name in \(H\) to zero. There is at least one such name. If another holder \(J\) contains both \(x,y\), take a completion with \(11\) on them. It exists by the full projection on \(H\). Now \(01\) and \(10\) each satisfy \(H\) and \(J\), the only rules using either name. Everything outside those two places can stay fixed.

Otherwise each name has at most one further holder, and the two further holders, when present, are distinct. Erase \(H\) temporarily. With the specified zeroes on \(H\), all four choices for \(x,y\) now have completions. The physical projection on \(H\) supplies the three choices other than \(00\). Necessity of its rule supplies a word violating only \(H\). Complementing every bit if needed makes all of \(H\) zero in that word.

From the remaining holder of \(x\), if any, omit \(x\), retaining a full not-all-equal requirement on what remains. Do the same for \(y\). Each shortened holder has at least two names. A solution after shortening makes both remaining holders mixed regardless of the values restored at \(x\) and \(y\). Suppose shortening gave no solution. By the preceding completion result, the fixed zeroes initiate a sequence of forced assignments ending in a conflict. Each shortened holder participates at most once, either making a forced assignment or giving the final conflict. If it participates, restore its omitted name with the same value as the common fixed colour responsible for that step. This preserves the entire deduction at that holder. Give either value to any omitted name whose holder did not participate. Since the omitted names belonged to distinct remaining holders, the same sequence still gives a conflict after restoration. This contradicts the four completions before shortening. A shortened solution therefore exists. Restore \(H\) and the three mixed choices \(01,10,11\) on \(x,y\). Its constraint forbids \(00\) with that same exterior. The proof includes repeated meetings elsewhere and any three holders that meet pairwise.

## Identifying and forgetting a loose holder

For a distinction \(x\), consider every forbidden word that changing \(x\) alone repairs. Let \(R_x\) be the union of **all** single-name repairs of those forbidden words. This uses exactly the flip information prescribed in the study. Every \(x\) occurs in such a word. A full local projection allows a physical holder to have \(x\) as its only 1, and changing \(x\) then forbids the word.

Any two repairs of one forbidden word belong to every physical rule it violates. Conversely the preceding three local alternatives supply a forbidden word repairable separately at any given pair with a common holder. Thus \(R_x\) is exactly the union of the physical holders containing \(x\).

If \(x\) is loose, \(R_x\) is its sole holder \(H\), and \(S\) projects there to the full not-all-equal law. If \(x\) is shared, \(R_x=H\cup J\) strictly contains \(H\). A mixed word on this union that keeps all of \(H\) zero cannot extend, so the full-law test fails. Equivalently, any candidate violated at **every** forbidden word repaired by \(x\) must contain each repair in \(R_x\). It must equal \(H\) in the loose case, since it cannot properly contain \(H\). In the shared case there is no such candidate. In the loose case \(H\) certainly fails at every such word, because it alone can change when \(x\) changes.

For a recovered \(H\), forget all names held only there. One of those names can always take a value satisfying \(H\), so the resulting relation is exactly that of the other original holders. Every surviving holder keeps its full projection. For any surviving holder \(K\), a word violating only \(K\) before this operation still violates only \(K\) after restriction. None of its own names were forgotten. Thus the remaining holders keep the necessity of their rules. Every part left after erasing \(H\) in a formerly connected presentation has at least one holder that met \(H\), and those shared names now occur once. Applying the flip reading again in each part recovers every holder in finitely many repetitions. The same state-only reduction applies to any rival presentation, giving literal equality of the named holdings. No step of this reconstruction is a growth step.

## A second derivation of the closed classification

Consider a candidate \(C\) for which every pair of names has a common physical holder in the original presentation. Every holder in a rival presentation meets this test, since the preceding reading depends only on \(S\). The following argument also classifies extra candidates passing this test that cannot participate in a rival presentation.

If all names of \(C\) share one original holder, \(C\) equals that holder. A smaller set would be free. Conversely no physical holder can be a proper subset of \(C\), because \(C\) itself has full local projection. Suppose now that \(C\) is new. Its names' sets of holders each have size at most two. They intersect pairwise, with no common holder. They must be the three pair types among three old holders \(A,B,D\), with every type represented. Indeed, take two distinct occurrence sets with common member \(A\), say \(\{A,B\}\) and \(\{A,D\}\). A singleton occurrence set would give a common member for the whole family. Any occurrence set missing \(A\) then has to be \(\{B,D\}\), and the only sets of size two meeting all three are precisely these three. Thus every distinction in \(C\) is held twice among \(A,B,D\).

Pin \(C\) to zero. There is no completion. The completion result produces a conflict by forced assignments. Trace back from the conflicting row, retaining, for every non-pinned input, the row that assigned it. This records a finite rooted tree of deductions. A row can make just one assignment. The resulting non-pinned name can be used by just one other row, as each name occurs at most twice. The ordered deductions exclude a directed cycle, and the single possible use of any output excludes a reused subargument. Each retained row has one name per incident connection in this tree and has only pinned names at its other places. This counts every place, including the output of a row that made a forced assignment.

Every leaf of the underlying tree contains a pin, since the original arity was at least three. Only \(A,B,D\) can contain pins. The conflict cannot consist of a single row with all places pinned because \(C\) contains no whole original holder. Some retained row has no pins. In fact, if the root has any pins, its common colour is zero and at least one of its zeroes was forced. The row producing this zero used only ones on its other places, so it has no pin. A row without pins has tree degree equal to its full arity and thus at least three.

In any finite tree with at least two nodes, the number of leaves equals two plus the sum of degree minus two over its nodes of degree at least three. Our tree therefore has exactly three leaves, all \(A,B,D\), and just one branching row, of degree three. Any further degree-two row would need a pin because its physical arity is at least three. All possible pinned rows are already leaves. This leaves exactly one three-place row \(I\) meeting \(A,B,D\) once apiece. Each of those three holds its one name shared with \(I\) and only pins of \(C\) besides. Each pin has both holders among \(A,B,D\), and each of their three pair types occurs. These four holders thus have no meeting or loose distinction left over. Connectedness makes them the entire presentation.

Consequently a new candidate of the kind considered can only consist of **all distinctions outside a three-place holder** in a closed four-holder presentation in which every pair of holders meets. The three meetings at that holder must be single. Conversely its complement really is a candidate, even if some of the other three meetings are repeated. A word on the complement forces an input to the three-place holder only when both other groups at the corresponding holder have the same common value. If all three inputs are forced equal, all three groups have that same common value, and the whole word on the complement is constant. Otherwise a mixed completion exists. Every pair of complementary distinctions has a common holder among the other three.

To complete the classification, suppose two irredundant presentations differ. Each must have a holder missing from the other, since a strict superset of a family defining \(S\) would contain an implied rule. Applying the argument in both directions shows that each rival consists of four mutually meeting holders, and all names in either are held twice. Write \(H_i\) for the four original holdings. The possible rival holdings are \(H_i\) or their complements \(C_i\). The two choices with the same index are disjoint, so the four rival holders include exactly one choice per index. Let \(t_i\) be 1 if the complement is used and 0 otherwise. A label shared by \(H_i,H_j\) must still occur twice. Thus \((1-t_i)+(1-t_j)+\sum_{k\ne i,j} t_k=2\) for every pair \(i,j\). All pair sums \(t_i+t_j\) must agree, so all \(t_i\) agree. Distinct holdings require all four complements. Every original holder must then meet each other holder on exactly one distinction, giving the six pair names and no others.

For these six names the two choices do work. Were the three pair labels among three holders all zero, a mixed state at each of those three would force all three labels of the remaining holder to one. Complementation gives the other colour. So every state of the original obeys the four complementary rules. The permutation exchanging each pair label with its opposite exchanges the two presentations, giving equal state counts and hence equal named state sets. There are 18 allowed words. The words with two 1s choose the three ways to cover each holder once, and their bitwise complements supply three with four 1s. Of the twenty words with three 1s, the four original and four complementary triples are excluded. Permuting the three places of a holder and complementing all bits gives its six local words. To violate only a specified holder, give all its incident pair labels zero and all remaining pair labels one. Both presentations are closed.

The independent calculation reads each complete tabulated state relation and compares every pair and every flip with the supplied physical supports. Across the 2,349 presentation representatives on three through ten names, 56,509 of 97,117 pairs have a common holder. The three-alternative test detects exactly these pairs. The single-flip unions identify all 5,981 loose occurrences among 22,483 occurrences. Testing every projected candidate whose pairs have a common holder finds 18 extra candidates, each of the four-holder form proved above. These counts weight the census representatives once and do not measure a rate among named presentations.
