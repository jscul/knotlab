# Which parities ordinary growth makes harder to flip

Take a finite, connected starting presentation. Each distinction has one or two different holders. Each knot obeys even parity. Consider complete, funded rounds of the ordinary rule. A class fact at scale \(k\) is nonconstant and survives every allowed change of at most \(k\) distinctions. In the shortest-change reading it must survive every tied shortest change through every distinction. “Lasting” in the cost claim means that the least size of a flipping change grows without bound along a continuing sequence of funded rounds.

## Ancestry and the named family coordinates

A family joins live starting knots through common witnesses. Every candidate pair occurs together in a proposed group. Every loose distinction left after those groups is taken by a unary birth. Any two births sharing a parent consequently have a candidate pair at the next funded round, provided both remain live. Conversely, all loose distinctions are now held by births, and a common witness to two of these live births must be one of their parents. A route within an old family lifts through the covering births and their shared parents. No birth joins different families. A zero-fresh birth takes all loose distinctions of its connected part and is the sole birth there. Thus a surviving family cannot split. This is the earlier ordinary-growth component argument. [Ordinary operator](../../ORDINARY-DYNAMICS-20261120-001/end_operator.md) · [Closing criterion](../../ERASURE-AND-CLOSING-SIGNS-20261121-001B/closing_law.md)

Write \(L_A\) for the parity of the loose distinctions of family \(A\) at the start. An equation on a sublist of these parities is a sum of starting knot laws, by linear duality. The coefficient of every shared distinction in that sum must be zero. Its two holders therefore have the same coefficient. Connectedness gives one coefficient on all knots. Summing all laws leaves exactly all loose distinctions. For \(m>0\) nonempty families this is one independent equation, and the joint image contains all \(2^{m-1}\) even tuples. With zero families the empty tuple has zero equations on its coordinates. With one family the value is fixed. Two give equality. With at least three the joint relation passes the parity projection test. The additional requirement that each fact be unread at a single knot still needs a separate check. The same linear argument works for **any partition** of all loose distinctions into nonempty groups. It does not choose the groups. ## The exact least flip for any nonconstant sum of family facts

At each funded round, sum all birth laws descending from a chosen union of families. Each previous loose distinction in that union occurs once, and the other terms are precisely its new loose distinctions. Their parities agree. The complementary union has the same parity, since the sum over all families is zero. Any flipping change must therefore use at least one fresh distinction on each side. On restriction to the preceding stage it still flips the chosen sum. The least size rises by at least two.

For the matching upper bound, represent a change by its incidences at the holders. Give each loose distinction one common **bookkeeping** holder. Evenness there follows by summing all the actual laws. Every allowed change decomposes into simple cycles in this representation, including pairs of parallel distinctions. In a minimum flipping change one of its cycles already flips the parity, so the change itself can be taken to be that cycle. It uses the bookkeeping holder exactly once and has exactly two loose distinctions, one on each side. At the next round they go to different births. At each of them the old input parity changes, so its equation is not already implied and it receives a fresh distinction. Add one fresh distinction at each. This extends the same flipping change at cost two and leaves just those two fresh distinctions loose. Induction gives **\(M(t)=M(0)+2t\)** at every reached round, for every nonconstant sum, including sums whose support has several families. This is an argument for the declared ordinary rule. ## A uniform bound for parities outside that span

A starting change preserving all \(L_A\) has even loose parity in each family separately. At each holder with loose distinctions, pairs from that holder span the even subsets of its loose distinctions. For each candidate pair of holders \(a,b\), choose one joint birth and one witnessing holder \(w\). Join the two loose distinctions this birth takes, one from each parent. Together these pairs connect all the loose distinctions of each start family. Their binary sums generate every even loose subset there.

These generators have bounded lifts to **every** funded stage.

- The selected loose distinctions from \(a,b\), together with the shared distinctions from \(a\) and \(b\) to \(w\), are an allowed four-change at the start. The same birth receives both loose distinctions, so the four-change is permanently closed after the first round.
- Two loose distinctions at one holder form a two-change at the start. Distinct children take them, since a birth takes at most one input per parent. Each child's input parity flips, so each receives a fresh distinction. At round one choose one fresh distinction at each. If there is a second funded round, these children share the old parent as a witness. Choose the two fresh distinctions taken by one of their common children. The resulting four-change is permanently closed at round two.
- Subtract the generators having the desired loose subset. The remainder is a start change on shared distinctions alone. It is generated by simple closed start changes. Each remains allowed with its original support at every round.

If a start parity is outside the family span modulo the laws, linear duality gives a change preserving all \(L_A\) that flips it. At least one generator in the preceding list flips it. Its cost at any funded stage is at most the larger of four and the largest size in a chosen basis of simple closed start changes. In particular, the larger of four and the number of starting knots is an upper bound. If a grown parity has no start representative, the earlier proof gives a change of size two or four fixing the start on which it changes. This last argument uses the fixed-start change space, so it does not presuppose that such a parity is a class fact. These arguments give the cost dichotomy. A threshold larger than the bounded costs still has to be below the growing minima for the family facts to be class facts. The two limits are different. At small fixed scales bounded-cost parities can remain class facts indefinitely. Whether a shortest change flips a parity is a further question about its individual distinctions. Finally, even when only the family span survives, choosing additional sums as coordinates changes which minimal relations are present on the chosen list. ## What settles at fixed scale

For a fixed scale \(k\), consider only the projections of small changes onto starting distinctions. At each round, the number of changed fresh distinctions is even, by the sum of all current laws. A nonzero starting change itself has size at least two. A change of size at most \(k\) with a nonzero starting restriction therefore has some generation among the first \(\lfloor k/2\rfloor\) at which it changes zero fresh distinctions. Truncate it there. Since none of the loose inputs changes, this truncation extends with zeros through all further rounds. Conversely every small change at a later round restricts to a small change at the earlier round. Thus the projected small-change space stabilizes by this bound whenever the required rounds are funded. For \(k\ge4\), the fixed-start generators of sizes two and four ensure that these projections determine the entire class space. At scale five this already applies at round two. For \(k=1\), no nonzero change of size one is allowed and the projected space is zero throughout; for two and three, fresh coordinates can still contribute class dimensions. The [stage tests](split_counts.json) distinguish this statement from the conjecture about all scales.
