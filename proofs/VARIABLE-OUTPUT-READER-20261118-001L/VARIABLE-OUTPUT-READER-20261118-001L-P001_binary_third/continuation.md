# When a later junction distinguishes the two exact width-28 histories

One common two-parent addition distinguishes the original histories on the original names. It changes four pair readings. The histories nevertheless have identical sets of possible anonymous futures, at every depth and under either growth law. After any identical sequence of lawful additions on the same names, interchanging 21 and 22 still identifies every physical junction. This section concerns the two histories with exactly equal tables in [001K's high-junction control](../../VARIABLE-OUTPUT-READER-20261118-001K/VARIABLE-OUTPUT-READER-20261118-001K-P001_third_three_fresh/high_check.json).

Write \(K(x,y)=D(x,y)+1\) for the smallest bond size through two distinctions. Sites and distinctions below retain the zero-based labels in the source. A common addition uses the same loose distinctions on both sides and assigns the same names to its fresh distinctions. The narrow law uses two or three parents and makes two outputs at every subsequent addition. The full law also permits unary additions and three outputs.

## The first separating addition

At width 28 the final six-place site, numbered 11, takes 12, 20 and 21 on the left, or 12, 20 and 22 on the right, and makes 25, 26, 27 on both. Site 9 originally made 21 and 22. Its remaining loose distinction is 22 on the left and 21 on the right. The other loose distinctions are 23, 24 at site 10 and 25, 26, 27 at site 11. Both full tables agree on all 378 pairs, and both three-parent menus are empty. The two six-place parity equations differ on the original names. Every addition extends every old allowed state, because at least one fresh value can solve its new parity equation. Thus the difference between the two original named state relations remains observable by projecting back to these 28 distinctions after any common continuation.

Exactly six first additions are available under the narrow law. Take one of 23, 24 and one of 25, 26, 27. Their holders share site 8. The spare at site 9 can supply no first pair, and two distinctions at the same holder cannot supply a pair. The permutations within each of the two loose groups identify all six choices. With fresh names 28, 29, each choice has the following differences among the 435 entries.

| Pair | Left \(K\) | Right \(K\) |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| 21 with either 28 or 29 | 8 | 6 |
| 22 with either 28 or 29 | 6 | 8 |

Consequently bond distance five first separates the named tables. This takes one step, and every narrow first step does it. After the example using 23 and 25, the left binary menu is \(\{22,28\},\{22,29\},\{24,26\},\{24,27\}\). The right menu replaces 22 by 21. Thus the first decision that permits an addition on only one side comes after one common addition. Under the full law the single input 22 is already permitted only on the left at width 28, and 21 only on the right. A common unary first step leaves the exact named tables equal. A common binary first step making three outputs gives six differences, of sizes 7 versus 9.

Transposing 21 and 22 takes the left physical presentation to the right and fixes every other name. Neither transposed bit can ever be consumed on a common history, since each is permanently shared on one side. Induction now identifies every resulting junction under this same transposition, including its fresh names. More generally, transport each left action by the transposition and conversely. This gives a bijection of the entire rooted trees of lawful histories and preserves each bond size and next menu. Two *independently chosen* narrow branches can have different anonymous tables as early as step two. After the common 23, 25 step, take 22, 28 on the left and 24, 26 on the right. At width 32 their counts of pairs with \(K=6\) are 164 and 155. Both anonymous branches are available from either root. The single first-step orbit proves the step-two minimum for this separate comparison. [Pair readings and menus](continuation.json)

## The narrow law at every later step

First keep the original names fixed. Regard the spare at site 9 as unavailable. This leaves five available distinctions and retains every ordinary incidence at site 9. For each state retain the active holders with their available counts, adjacency among them, and the sets of at least two active holders meeting each exhausted site. Repeated identical sets have the same witness effect. These data decide whether all parents are distinct, whether each pair has a common ordinary neighbor and whether a single neighbor meets all three. When a new site is attached, its only new neighbors are its parents. An unavailable site never regains an output. Removing an exhausted holder from the active set, retaining its witness set and restricting the older witness sets therefore gives the exact successor. Any choice among loose distinctions at a fixed holder yields this same record. This is the [finite frontier recurrence](../../VARIABLE-OUTPUT-READER-20261118-001J/VARIABLE-OUTPUT-READER-20261118-001J-P001_seven_to_six/family.md#all-histories-have-at-most-two-shrinking-joins).

Starting at the five available distinctions, the recurrence closes on 27 records, with 58 transitions by parent groups. Every record has at least one successor and every transition takes two parents. Thus common narrow histories extend indefinitely and never admit a three-parent move. Restoring the spare at site 9 gives the full set of futures from one side, with 110 reachable records, 325 two-parent transitions and seven three-parent transitions. Six records admit a triple. The first such record requires six additions, reaching width 40. One shortest left input sequence is

\[
(23,25),\ (22,28),\ (24,30),\ (29,32),\ (31,26),\ (27,34).
\]

Each addition makes the next two fresh names. The triple \(\{33,36,38\}\) is then legal. Transporting the second action to \((21,28)\) makes the same final triple legal on the right. The recurrence visits records in order of minimum depth and checks every proposed parent group and its successor against a literal representative. The named scalar difference also persists after **every nonempty common narrow history**, first visible through \(D=5\) at each depth. The following argument supplies the uniform cutoff without enumerating scalar tables at later widths.

* Site 10 is the only three-input, two-output junction in such a history. The frontier of all loose distinctions has size six at width 28. Each subsequent two-input, two-output addition preserves that size. Its full frontier is a bond, since the ordinary sites are connected and the other side consists of the exterior alone.
* Every newly added junction is a strict descendant of the six-place site 11 along input-to-child arrows. At the outset, site 10 is the only available holder other than 11. At each further step there is still at most one available holder outside site 11 and its descendants. Each addition needs two distinct parents.
* Work on the left, where 21 joins sites 9 and 11. Let \(g\) be either fresh output of the most recent site. It is still loose. The full frontier gives \(K(22,g)\leq6\). We show \(K(21,g)>6\). For a cut choose its ordinary shore \(U\), with the exterior on the other side. The first three members of a shore in birth order have boundary at least seven. At most one site has degree three, the others have degree at least four, and three sites in the simple bipartite ordinary graph span at most two links. Inserting a subsequent site with \(p\) inputs, \(h\) outputs and \(j\) parents already inside changes this boundary by \(p+h-2j\). Only site 10 can reduce it, by at most one.
* If \(11\in U\) and 21 crosses, then \(9\notin U\). If 11 is among the first three, its degree six raises their bound to at least nine. Otherwise its missing parent adds at least two. Even site 10 cannot bring either bound to six. A singleton shore at site 11 misses \(g\), and any two-site shore containing 11 costs at least seven.
* If \(11\notin U\), then \(9\in U\), and the site carrying \(g\) also lies inside. These are distinct and nonadjacent since site 9's last loose distinction was reserved. A two-site shore of this kind costs more than six. A larger shore costing at most six must contain site 10 after its first three sites. Every other subsequent insertion must have all its parents inside. The first three sites thus predate 10 and 11. Follow a directed path from the omitted site 11 to the included latest site. The first included member follows 11 and has an omitted parent, a contradiction.
* Every shore of at least three sites costs at least six by the same initial bound and single possible loss. Shores of at most two sites involving 11 also cost at least six. All equations of weight at most five therefore use only physical sites whose supports are identical on the original names. The two sides have the same minimal equations through weight five. Since the transposition interchanges their two entries through \(g\), this gives \(K(22,g)=6<K(21,g)\) on the left and the opposite reading on the right. Exact identity never returns along a common narrow history.

There is also a sharp cutoff for every narrow history paired with its transported history **after spending the spare**. Before the spend the preceding argument applies. The spare cannot participate in a triple while unused, as proved below. Its first use therefore attaches a four-place site \(W\), with two inputs and two outputs. In the 110-record individual closure, 87 records have six loose distinctions and 23 have five. Each subsequent three-input move reduces this count by one. Together with site 10, there are at most two shrinking moves in the whole history. Thus any shore with at least three sites costs at least \(7-2=5\). Any two-site shore also costs at least five, since the graph is simple and only the seed has degree three.

On the left, each output \(g\) of \(W\) shares a four-place junction with 22. The star of \(W\) remains a bond under all later growth. After removing \(W\), every other site has a path to the exterior, since each has at least two distinct outgoing bits and at most one can lead directly to the removed site. Following surviving outputs terminates at the exterior. The pair 21, \(g\) meets no star of arity at most four. Only sites 9 and 11 contain 21, and \(g\) was born later than both. The unique ternary seed contains neither pair. Therefore \(K(22,g)=4<K(21,g)\) forever, and all entries through \(K=3\) agree. The sharp cutoff on the identity is now \(D=3\). ## What survives under the full law

Along every common full-law history, **all named three-parent permissions agree**. Site 9 keeps its sole spare. Its ordinary neighbors are exactly 5, 7 and 11. The other neighbors of 5 are 3, 4 and 6. The other neighbors of 7 are 0, 6 and 8. All these sites, including 5 and 7, have exhausted their outputs by width 28. Therefore any other loose holder sharing a neighbor with site 9 must meet 11. Two proposed co-parents for 9 would give a universal common neighbor, 11, which forbids their triple. The spare can never participate while held unused. The other transposed bit is already shared. The transposition fixes every other proposed triple pointwise.

This named triple agreement extends to **every pair of transported histories**. Until the spare is used, their actions are common on the original names. Once it is used on each side under transport, 21 and 22 are both permanently shared in both histories. The transposition then fixes all loose names individually, so every named next permission agrees, for all allowed parent counts and forever afterwards.

The full law can nevertheless distinguish a *transported* choice on named pair readings already at \(D=2\). Attach a unary two-output junction on 22 on the left and 21 on the right. With fresh bit 28 the two readings through 22 are \(K=3\) and 5. A permanent bond of size three through 22 and 28 persists on the left. On the right, only the old four- and six-place sites contain 22. Every bond of size three is a single ternary site even in the full law. A two-site shore costs at least four by simplicity. In a larger shore the last three sites make at least six outputs and at most two go to other sites of the shore, by bipartiteness. Thus the right pair can never acquire a three-bit bond. Subsequent transported actions keep this labeled difference at \(D=2\).

Under the full law some common histories preserve exact identity of the named tables for arbitrarily many steps. For example, repeatedly attach a unary junction to a newly loose output in a branch begun at 23. More generally, attach any lawful piece meeting the original only in 23. All within-side entries remain fixed. For a pair in opposite pieces away from the meeting distinction, the minimum satisfies \(K(x,y)=K(x,23)+K(23,y)-2\). The minimum is the sum of two independently shortest bonds through 23 with that bit canceled. Since the root tables agree, all entries in either joined table agree on the original names. Such histories can even have an active triple. Attach three-output unary sites on 23, then on each of 28, 29, 30. Attach two-output binary sites on \((31,34),(32,37),(35,38)\). At width 46 the two exact tables agree on all 1,035 entries. Precisely the eight triples taking one bit from each pair \(\{40,41\},\{42,43\},\{44,45\}\) are permitted. The mark \(\{40,41,42\}\) repeats a holder and is forbidden on both sides. Conversely the common narrow histories above provide arbitrarily long full-law histories with a persistent named pair difference. The shared transposition and the named three-parent conclusion hold for both kinds of continuation.
