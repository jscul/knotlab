# Scalar certificates for a marked inverse

## All realizations of the published width-32 table

**Theorem.** Every reachable presentation of the published width-32 matrix has exactly the same eight legal triples on the matrix's displayed names. They choose one bit from each of \(\{26,27\}\), \(\{28,29\}\), and \(\{30,31\}\). This covers all \(4{,}960\) unordered marks, every symmetry of the matrix, and competitors with arbitrary growth histories. It also applies to surviving marks when the matrix is supplied by a genuinely retained piece of a larger source. Exactly two labeled homogeneous parity spaces of realizations produce this matrix. [Exact certificate](realizations32.json) · [Replay](check_32_realizations.py)

For clarity, the following sufficient certificate works at any finite width. Obtain a scalar matrix \(M\) and a lawful witness presentation with menu \(\mathcal Q\) and parity-equation space \(W\). Produce a list of candidate spaces containing the parity space of every reachable realization of \(M\). Suppose each remaining space is \(g(W)\) for an explicit relabeling \(g\) that preserves \(M\) and \(\mathcal Q\). The relabeled witness realizes that space with menu \(\mathcal Q\). Every competitor with that same binary space has the same allowed states and therefore the same menu by the all-size allowed-state theorem. Under genuine pruning the comparison applies to candidates that survive the boundary screen on both sides. Taking a complete orbit of \(\mathcal Q\)-preserving relabelings is unnecessary when a shorter list covers the spaces. This condition can succeed even when \(M\) changes a real parity equation under a symmetry. [Allowed-state theorem](../../VARIABLE-OUTPUT-READER-20261118-001D/VARIABLE-OUTPUT-READER-20261118-001D-P001_separator_inverse/inverse.md)

Here the displayed witness supplies the matrix. The space-exhaustion stage consumes only that matrix. It uses the predecessor's valid deductions from shortest bonds of sizes three through six. Every junction star is one of these short bonds, and the junction stars generate the entire parity space. The deductions force a subspace of rank 14. There are 26 possible generating supports still outside it, giving only 12 distinct cosets. The source rank is at most \(1+\lfloor(32-3)/2\rfloor=15\), since each site after the first introduces at least two new distinctions. The same bound holds when the first local site retains an incoming boundary bit. Hence the exhaustive candidates are the forced subspace itself and its 12 one-dimensional extensions. [Short-support deductions and rank bound](../../VARIABLE-OUTPUT-READER-20261118-001D/VARIABLE-OUTPUT-READER-20261118-001D-P001_separator_inverse/proof.md#what-the-scalars-can-certify-without-reconstructing-a-history)

For each candidate the checker computes the minimum bond through every pair using its full linear space. A nonzero word is a bond exactly when zero and that word are the only words supported inside it. The forced space alone disagrees in 255 entries. Six extensions disagree in 30 entries each. Four more disagree in 45 entries each. The last two match all 496 entries. They are precisely \(W\) and the image of \(W\) under exchange of bits 13 and 14, as checked by equality of the entire spaces. Both actual histories replay, and direct incidence checks give identical verdicts on all \(4{,}960\) triples. The witness table has no separating distinction. In a rival retained realization its external boundary flags can differ. For a surviving mark, the witness with no cuts supplies the comparison required by the retained allowed-state theorem.

**An unbounded family.** In the displayed witness bit 15 is loose and belongs to none of the eight choices. Append a chain of unary junctions beginning on bit 15, taking two or three fresh bits per junction and using one fresh bit for the next link. The exact scalar separator test identifies the same bridge partitions in every scalar-equivalent full presentation. The large retained piece is the width-32 matrix. The remaining pieces each contain a single junction on three or four bits. A mark on a bridge or spanning components is rejected, and a single-junction piece cannot supply three different parents. Inside the large piece the theorem above applies to every surviving mark. Thus these particular matrices have exactly eight legal choices at width 32 and every width at least 34. The exchange of 13 and 14 continues to preserve the full scalar table by the unary attachment formula, fixes the chain, and still changes the old parity space by projection. This gives genuinely ambiguous parity spaces throughout the family. The exact count of two compatible spaces was established for the width-32 base. ## A necessary distance within every legal triple

**Proposition.** Every two marks of a legal triple have scalar distance at least four, in any finite source. This gives a rejection test applicable under arbitrary scalar isometries. It applies unchanged to the surviving marks of a genuinely retained piece, since pruning preserves their distances.

Let the three distinct loose holders be \(i,j,k\), all on the same bipartition side. A bond through the loose bits of \(i,j\) cannot have size three because each ternary bond is a single ordinary star. Suppose it has size four and let \(U\) be its ordinary shore. This connected shore contains the nonadjacent \(i,j\), so has at least three sites. By the short-cut count below, its latest three sites supply all four boundary bits. Every boundary bit goes outward. If \(k\) were inside, then the latest three would have to be \(i,j,k\), since each supplies a loose bit. Their equal bipartition color would leave no internal meeting, forcing at least six outgoing bits.

Thus \(k\) lies outside. Choose distinct witnesses \(p,q\) of the pairs \(i,k\) and \(j,k\), respectively. The edge-disjoint paths \(i,p,k\) and \(j,q,k\) each cross exactly once, since the two loose bits already take the other two boundary places. If \(p,q\) are both inside, the four distinct sites \(i,j,p,q\) would all supply boundary bits. Otherwise the two ordinary crossings have distinct nonadjacent inside endpoints and distinct outside endpoints connected outside \(U\). When both witnesses are outside, the inside endpoints are \(i,j\) and the outside witnesses meet at \(k\). In the mixed case, say \(p\) is inside and \(q\) is outside, the inside endpoints are \(p,j\). An adjacency there would make \(p\) a universal witness for \(i,j,k\). The outside endpoints \(k,q\) are adjacent.

Such a pair of exits is impossible. Every ordinary cycle is a sum modulo two of ordinary four-cycles. Indeed the two neighbors of the latest vertex of a cycle were parents at its addition and already had a common neighbor. Subtract the resulting four-cycle. The remaining even edge set, possibly empty, splits into cycles on earlier vertices, proving the assertion by induction. The two exits above belong to one ordinary cycle, by connectedness inside \(U\) and the exhibited path outside. At least one summand four-cycle uses the first exit. Since there are only two ordinary crossings, it also uses the second. With four distinct endpoints, its inside edge must then join the two inside endpoints, contradicting their nonadjacency. Therefore the minimum containing bond size is at least five.

If the three sites have \(L_i,L_j,L_k\) loose bits, the same paths yield the additional bound \(D(e_i,e_j)+1\ge L_i+L_j+\min(2,L_k)\). Every shore containing \(i,j\) contains their loose bits in its boundary. If \(k\) is inside, its loose bits also cross. If it is outside, the two witness paths supply two distinct crossings. In the seven-site construction with two loose outputs at each of the three pair junctions, the full ordinary shore has just those six boundary bits, so every marked pair has distance exactly five.

## Three fresh outputs

**Exposed-site theorem.** If a junction \(u\) currently holds three loose outputs, no transposition of one of them with a shared distinction at \(u\) preserves the scalar bond-distance table. This holds at every finite width, including histories in which later additions have taken place elsewhere.

Write \(K(i,j)=D(i,j)+1\), the minimum size of a bond through \(i,j\). A bond in the wired meeting graph is a minimal nonempty parity equation. Define its ordinary shore \(U\) as the side of the corresponding cut that omits the exterior. Both sides are connected. In particular the star of any ordinary site \(v\) is a bond, and its size \(a(v)\) lies between three and six. ## Put the exposed site last

The three loose outputs \(F\) exhaust all outputs that \(u\) could have introduced. It therefore has no ordinary children. Defer its addition to the end. A later pair of parents that used \(u\) as a common witness were already parents of \(u\), so they already had a witness before it. Removing \(u\) from the intermediate histories creates no new universal witness. If \(u\) took three parents, no junction created afterwards could have become common to those three because it would have had to take precisely them as parents with \(u\) already witnessing all three. Thus the old input group \(P\) is still legal at the deferred step, with the same old bits loose. The seed itself can hold all three outputs loose only in the one-site presentation, which has no shared distinction.

Let \(e\in P\) be the proposed shared bit, and \(v\) its holder immediately before adding \(u\). For \(|P|=1\), this is a unary attachment. For every other old bit \(x\), the established attachment formula gives \(D(f,x)=D(e,x)+2\) for \(f\in F\). It suffices to consider \(|P|=2,3\). The bits in \(P\) are loose at distinct sites on one ordinary bipartition side. In the three-parent case no ordinary neighbor witnesses all those sites. [Unary attachment formula](../../VARIABLE-OUTPUT-READER-20261118-001C/VARIABLE-OUTPUT-READER-20261118-001C-P001_triple_inverse/proof.md#failure-of-global-distance-four-parity-at-every-width-from-27)

If \(v\) is the seed, its three-bit star gives \(K(e,x)\le3\) for any other bit \(x\) there. Every bond containing \(f\in F\) includes all three fresh bits, and a bond also containing \(x\) then has size at least four. Otherwise choose any actual input \(x\) of \(v\), arriving from an older site \(a\). It is shared even before \(u\) is added, so \(x\notin P\). We show \(K(f,x)>a(v)\), whereas the star at \(v\) gives \(K(e,x)\le a(v)\).

A bond through \(f,x\) includes the new equation in its sum of junction equations. Its support is \(F\cup J\), where \(x\in J\) and

\[
W=P\mathbin{\triangle}J
\]

is the support of an old parity equation. A putative bond of size at most \(a(v)\) requires \(|J|\le a(v)-3\le3\). The word \(W\) is nonzero since it contains \(x\).

## Short old equations

Every nonempty parity equation splits into disjoint bonds by repeatedly subtracting a minimal nonempty subword over the binary field. Every bond has size at least three, and a bond of size three is a single ternary star. For the latter assertion, a connected two-site shore has boundary size at least \(3+3-2=4\). On a shore with at least three sites, the latest three sites introduced at least six outputs. At most two can have been consumed inside the shore, as their only possible inside recipients form a simple bipartite graph on those three sites. At least four outputs therefore cross. When a cut from such a shore has size four, the latest three sites each introduced two outputs, have two internal meetings, and account for every boundary bit. In particular no input enters the shore. [Short-cut argument](../../VARIABLE-OUTPUT-READER-20261118-001D/VARIABLE-OUTPUT-READER-20261118-001D-P001_separator_inverse/twin_bound.md)

Two different loose bits of \(P\) cannot belong to the same ternary star because their holders differ. All old words of size at most five in the argument below are bonds. A six-bit word containing all three loose bits of a three-parent \(P\) is also a bond, since two disjoint ternary stars can cover at most two of those bits.

### Two old parents

Write \(P=\{e,d\}\), with \(d\) at \(w\). If \(J\) intersects \(P\), the only way \(W\) could have size at least three is \(|J|=3\) and exactly one cancellation. Then \(a(v)=6\), and \(W\) would be a ternary star containing \(x\) and the uncancelled loose bit. If that bit is \(e\), its holder \(v\) has arity six. If it is \(d\), neither endpoint of \(x=av\) is its holder \(w\). Indeed \(w\ne v\) and \(w\) is on the same bipartition side as \(v\), opposite \(a\).

Thus \(J\) must be disjoint from \(P\). The bond \(W\) has size at most \(a(v)-1\le5\). Its shore contains \(v,w\), has at least three sites because these two are not adjacent, and has the incoming bit \(x\) at \(v\). The four-output lower bound plus this input rules out size at most four. The only remaining possibility has \(a(v)=6\), so \(v\) originally made three outputs. At least five outputs now cross. When \(v\) is among the latest three its extra output increases their bound, and otherwise its loose \(e\) is an additional crossing. Including \(x\) gives at least six. This rules out the two-parent case.

### Three old parents

Write \(P=\{e,d,g\}\), with the other holders \(w,t\). If two bits are cancelled, \(|W|\le2\), which is impossible. With exactly one cancellation, \(|W|\le4\). Cancelling \(d\) or \(g\) leaves \(e\) and another loose bit at a distinct same-side site. Their shore has at least three sites and an entering \(x\), requiring at least five crossings. Cancelling \(e\) leaves \(d,g,x\). A cut of size at most four through these bits must have its loose holders \(w,t\) among its latest three. The crossing \(x=av\) must go outwards, putting \(a\) among those three as well. To have the necessary two internal meetings, \(a\) must neighbor both \(w,t\). Since \(a\) also neighbors \(v\), this contradicts the original legality of \(P\).

It remains that \(P,J\) are disjoint. The old bond \(W\) now contains all three loose bits and the entering \(x\), and has size at most \(a(v)\le6\). Size at most five is impossible. If all three holders were the latest three, their common bipartition side would preclude all internal meetings. If any holder were earlier, its loose bit would add a fifth outgoing crossing to the four from the latest three, besides \(x\).

We are left with size six and \(a(v)=6\). Site \(v\) has three parents and produced three outputs. If \(v\) were among the latest three, at least five outputs would cross from those three. The other two holders would have to lie among them too, since \(x\) already takes the sixth place. Once again those three holders have no internal meetings. Thus \(v\) is earlier than the latest three. Its loose \(e\), the four outgoing crossings from that trio, and the incoming \(x\) exhaust the cut. Pick a parent \(a'\ne a\) of \(v\). It is inside the shore, since \(x\) is the only incoming crossing at \(v\). At the birth of \(v\), its parents \(a,a'\) had an earlier common neighbor. The resulting two-edge path from the outside \(a\) to the inside \(a'\) must cross. Both of its edges are strictly older than \(v\), so neither can be any of the six listed crossings. This final contradiction proves the theorem.

## Replacing a piece attached at two vertices

Here is the scalar calculation for applying the theorem to an earlier part of a construction. Let two connected edge sets \(A,B\) meet in exactly two vertices \(u,o\), with \(o\) the exterior. Complete each set with one virtual edge between the two terminals. For a bit \(a\in A\), let \(p_A(a)\) be the smallest number of real edges in a bond of the completed \(A\) containing both \(a\) and the virtual edge. Define \(p_B(b)\) similarly. Write \(\lambda_B\) for the ordinary minimum number of edges separating \(u,o\) in the network \(B\).

A full bond meeting both networks must separate the terminals. Otherwise its shore avoiding both would be disconnected. When the terminals are separated, each shore restricts to a connected set in each network, because that shore has only one terminal through which it can reach the other network. Conversely two such local bonds glue into a full bond on deleting their virtual edges. Consequently

\[
K(a,b)=p_A(a)+p_B(b)\qquad (a\in A,\ b\in B).
\]

For a pair in \(A\) the possible bonds either stay in \(A\), with the terminals together, or separate the terminals and use the other network. In the first case their minimum is the minimum over bonds of the completed \(A\) that omit its virtual edge. In the second, it is the minimum local count of real edges through the pair and virtual edge, plus \(\lambda_B\). Substituting any connected \(B'\) of the same \(\lambda_B\) thus preserves every within-\(A\) entry. The formulas use infinity for a missing candidate.

In particular, suppose \(e,l\in A\) have identical distances to every other bit in the full graph. Equality against any \(b\in B\) with finite \(p_B(b)\) forces \(p_A(e)=p_A(l)\). Such finite values occur here since the full wired graph is 2-connected. If \(\lambda_B=2\), replace \(B\) by two parallel \(u,o\) edges, representing two restored loose outputs at \(u\). The equality persists against both restored bits, and the within-\(A\) entries are unchanged. The swapped pair also leaves entries wholly in the replacement fixed. Whenever this restored graph is a lawful earlier presentation with three loose outputs at \(u\) including \(l\), the exposed-site theorem rules out the proposed transposition in the full graph.

The two-terminal test uses the full table. Its forward implication alone does not certify that every additively separated submatrix comes from such a physical separation. The exposed-site result concerns the stated transpositions. A general marked isometry may also move the distinguishing old bit \(x\) or compare different presentations.
