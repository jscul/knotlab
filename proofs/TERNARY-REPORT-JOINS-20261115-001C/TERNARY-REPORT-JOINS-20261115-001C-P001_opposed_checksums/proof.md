# Opposite checksums of two evaluations of the same products

Status: Proved

## Standard statement

Let \(G\) be a nonempty simple graph on \(n\) Boolean inputs \(x\). Two evaluators receive the same \(x\). Each computes \(Q(x)=\sum_{uv\in E(G)}x_ux_v\) in the two-element field. Their outputs \(s,t\) are required to satisfy \(s+t=1\). This particular instance is unsatisfiable. A matched instance requiring \(s+t=0\) accepts every input with its honest evaluations.

Compare three procedures on this contradiction. The first may ask any adaptive affine question on \(x,s,t\), then use all affine consequences of each whole evaluator separately. If the polar form of \(Q\) has rank \(2r\), its minimum depth is \(r\) and its minimum number of terminal cases is \(2^r\). When every intermediate product becomes visible to affine questions, a matching of \(k\) independent products costs exactly \(2k\) leaves and depth \(1+\lceil\log_2 k\rceil\). A connected path of \(m\) products costs \(\Theta(m)\) leaves and \(\Theta(\log(m+1))\) depth in this second model. Finally, pairing equal products across evaluators gives a checked degree-two proof of linear size on actual strict ternary clauses, with no case branching on the full input.

## Nearest known result

The rank normal form for Boolean quadratics and the small size of affine subspaces contained in inner-product truth sets are classical. Ordinary variable elimination can profit from changing the order in which relations are combined. We use these as comparisons. We prove the bounds here for the particular two-evaluator fixed-point procedure, including all queries of the two checksum bits, and give the source-level proof and private-wire adversary separately. This is a local result in the stated models. [Cohen and Shinkar, The Complexity of DNF of Parities](https://eccc.weizmann.ac.il/report/2014/099/) · [Dechter, Bucket elimination](https://ics.uci.edu/~dechter/publications/r76A.pdf)

## What one terminal case may infer

A case is an affine subspace \(W\) of the visible coordinates, after all global linear equations and the answers to queries. For each of the two evaluators, take its exact relation on these coordinates, existentially eliminating hidden wires. Replace \(W\) by the intersection of the affine hulls of the two relations restricted to \(W\), with empty hull for the empty set. Repeat to a fixed point. If the result is empty the case closes. Otherwise the solver may split it by any affine Boolean form. A refutation is a binary tree with every leaf closed. This grants free Gaussian elimination, all affine consequences of each *entire* evaluator on each current case, and unlimited repetition. It does not allow joining nonlinear relations of the two evaluators before taking their affine hulls, or retaining a newly proved lemma across branches. These operations are tested below. Restricting to Boolean affine questions and exact closure is an explicit model choice.

Both comparisons use the same evaluations and the same condition \(s+t=1\). First only the common inputs and the two checksum bits are visible. In the second comparison every product \(p_e,q_e\) in the two evaluations is also visible. The prefix-sum wires in our strict implementation are affine functions of those products. Whenever the output parity holds, the two padding bits of the terminal equality clauses can take arbitrary values. Lower bounds survive fixing these bits, and the upper bounds ignore them.

## Exact public-case bound on a single contradiction

Eliminate \(t=1+s\), leaving coordinates \((x,s)\). The two exact projected relations are \(s=Q(x)\) and \(s=1+Q(x)\). The polar form is the alternating bilinear form \(B(u,v)=Q(u+v)+Q(u)+Q(v)+Q(0)\). Restricting its arguments to a codimension-\(j\) subspace lowers its rank by at most \(2j\): in an adapted basis this deletes \(j\) rows and columns. Translating the subspace or adding an affine term to \(Q\) does not change its restricted polar form.

Take any nonempty \(W\) of codimension \(d<r\) after eliminating \(t\). If \(s\) is free over its input projection \(L\), then \(W=L\times\{0,1\}\), and \(L\) has codimension \(d\). The restriction of \(Q\) has rank at least two. A non-affine Boolean function's graph affinely spans its whole domain times the output bit: an extra affine identity would either be an identity on the domain or express the output as an affine function. Both exact evaluator graphs therefore span \(W\).

Otherwise \(s\) is affine in \(x\) on \(W\). Now \(L\) has codimension \(d-1\), and \(Q|_L+s\) has polar rank at least four. Each of its two truth sets spans \(L\). Indeed, if one were contained in a proper hyperplane, the function would be constant on the complementary hyperplane. Restriction to a hyperplane lowers the rank by at most two and leaves a nonzero polar form. Neither truth set is empty since the function is nonconstant. Lifting the two truth sets to \(W\) proves that both hulls are all of \(W\). In either case there is no propagation before the first \(r\) independent queries. Every meaningful split before that point has two nonempty branches, so every tree has at least \(2^r\) leaves and depth at least \(r\).

For an upper bound, successively split off polar pairs \(a_i,b_i\) with \(B(a_i,b_i)=1\), restrict to their common perpendicular complement, and continue. The residual form after \(r\) steps is zero, so in a suitable affine basis \(Q\) is a sum of \(r\) independent pair products and an affine function. Fixing one member of each pair makes the remaining function affine. The two evaluator hulls then require complementary values for the same affine expression, closing the leaf. This proves both exact bounds, allowing arbitrary affine recodings of the visible coordinates. An affine \(Q\) gives one leaf without a query.

For the star \(Q=x_0(x_1+\cdots+x_m)\), hence \(r=1\). For the path \(Q=x_0x_1+\cdots+x_{m-1}x_m\), its polar matrix is the path adjacency matrix over the two-element field. Its kernel has zero entries on the alternating positions adjacent to the first endpoint, with the other entries equal; the last endpoint forces those remaining entries to zero when the number of vertices is even. Thus \(r=\lfloor(m+1)/2\rfloor\). Fixing the smaller class of alternating vertices directly attains this bound. These calculations also distinguish a single UNSAT refutation from the related task of exactly representing all satisfying evaluations. ## When each intermediate product is available

Set \(d_i=p_i+q_i\); their total parity is one. To identify an index whose \(d_i\) equals one, query the parity of half the current interval and keep an odd half. The parity of an interval is the difference of its two endpoint prefix parities when the prefix wires are visible. After at most \(\lceil\log_2 m\rceil\) questions one index remains. Split one of that product's input bits. The two evaluator hulls then give \(p_i=q_i=0\) or \(p_i=q_i=x_v\) in the two branches, contradicting \(d_i=1\). The tree has at most \(2m\) leaves. The star also retains its two-leaf upper bound by splitting the common input.

Here is a lower bound that lets each side use the exact affine hull of its full evaluation even after products have become public. Begin with a matching of \(k\ge1\) independent pairs \(x_i,y_i\). In the coordinates \(x_i,y_i,p_i,d_i\), impose only \(\sum_i d_i=1\); thus \(q_i=p_i+d_i\). Call this affine space \(K_k\). The active input bits and the \(p_i\)'s are freely variable. With one parity equation there are \(k-1\) free \(d\)'s. In a larger case, the adversary preserves an affine copy of \(K_k\) with other product gates satisfied on both sides identically. All other coordinates on this copy are affine functions of its parameters.

Each whole evaluator's actual solutions on this copy affinely span the copy. On the first side an affine identity after \(p_i=x_i y_i\) must have all its coefficients of \(p_i\) zero by the distinct quadratic monomials. Independence of \(1,x_i,y_i\) and the \(k-1\) free \(d\)'s deals with the rest. On the other side use the invertible change \(p_i\mapsto p_i+d_i\). Additional gates that are identically satisfied on the copy impose nothing there. Taking hulls on a larger \(W\) that contains the copy still contains that copy, and so does every round of saturation.

For \(k=1\) no leaf closes yet. A query of \(x_1\) suffices for the two branches. For \(k>1\) pull the next affine question back to \(K_k\).

- A constant question preserves the same live copy in one branch.
- A nonconstant question depending only on the \(d_i\)'s partitions the indices into two nonempty classes of sizes \(j,k-j\). In one branch keep the first class live, fixing all other groups to legitimate zero states. In the other branch keep the complementary class live. The odd total fixes the corresponding answers.
- If some coefficient of \(x_j,y_j,p_j\) is nonzero, set \(d_j=0\) and keep \(k-1\) groups live. There exist two legitimate AND states \((x_j,y_j,p_j)\) on which that nonzero form has opposite values: the four AND states affinely span three coordinates. Make this group's state one of these two as an affine function of the remaining contribution to the query, choosing the offset separately in each child. The resulting map preserves the live groups, satisfies the removed gate on both sides identically, and gives the requested constant answer. Each branch thus contains a copy of \(K_{k-1}\).

Induction gives at least \(2k\) leaves. The second case pays \(2j+2(k-j)\), and the third pays \(4(k-1)\ge2k\). At least one child keeps \(\lceil k/2\rceil\) live groups in either case, giving the claimed depth \(1+\lceil\log_2 k\rceil\). The parity-search construction attains both bounds on a matching. Constant queries and empty branches cannot improve a minimum tree. On a connected path keep every third edge starting with the first and fix all other vertices to zero. These \(k=\lceil m/3\rceil\) edges form an induced matching; every unselected edge has a zero endpoint. The copy embeds even with all prefix sums visible and terminal padding fixed. Thus for a path the number of leaves lies between \(2\lceil m/3\rceil\) and \(2m\), and the worst depth between \(1+\lceil\log_2\lceil m/3\rceil\rceil\) and \(1+\lceil\log_2 m\rceil\). The bounds have the same order of growth. A star has at least two leaves by fixing all but one edge and exactly two by the hub split.

There is a useful intermediate observation model. Divide the path into consecutive blocks of edge lengths \(w_1,\ldots,w_b\). Expose the two prefix checksums at each of its \(b-1\) internal boundaries. Parity questions on these visible coordinates locate a block of odd discrepancy in at most \(\lceil\log_2 b\rceil\) queries. Within that block fix its smaller class of alternating vertices. Its local checksum is now an affine function of the remaining inputs, so the two whole-evaluator hulls require equal block parities and close. This tree has at most \(\sum_i2^{\lfloor(w_i+1)/2\rfloor}\) leaves. Taking blocks of length two (and one final singleton when needed) strengthens the private path upper bound to \(2\lceil m/2\rceil\) leaves and depth at most \(1+\lceil\log_2\lceil m/2\rceil\rceil\). For 31 edges, just six extra prefix bits allow four blocks of lengths 8,8,8,7 and at most 64 leaves; the public-only lower bound is 65,536. The bound for such intermediate choices is an upper bound. This result concerns an actual contradiction, and the fixed-point rule has access to every affine consequence that follows from either evaluator separately inside that cell. A procedure that groups constraints from both sides can learn \(p_i=q_i\) locally and reuse it across the whole computation.

## Checked regrouping on strict clauses

The second test uses input bits representing the independent report values themselves. These values can be simultaneously realized by the preceding six-report functions (and their higher-arity extensions), for example on the affine section \(a=b=0,x_i=y_i=1,z_i=R_i\). Bounds and circuits here refer to the specified report-level encoding; private bits of a different upstream implementation are a different observation model.

For \(m\) product gates per side, encode each three-bit AND relation by the four strict clauses excluding its four failed assignments. On each side form a prefix XOR by \(m-1\) XOR gates, each again four ternary clauses. The first prefix is its first product. Require \(s+t=c\) by forbidding its two failing pairs. Split each of those binary clauses into two strict ternaries using opposite literals of a fresh, independent bit. This gives \(16m-4\) strict input clauses on \(5m+1\) bits. All clauses have three distinct variables and the two charges have identical headers. On charge zero the honest products and prefixes extend every common input to a satisfying assignment. Charge one is impossible.

Each product equation \(p_i+x_u x_v=0\), each prefix addition and the terminal parity equation is *derived* as a singleton in the unnamed degree-two proof calculus. For each assignment to a source gate, a failed value supplies its genuine strict exclusion; for a terminal pair the two strict clauses resolve on the padding bit. For a permitted assignment use the Boolean choices of its bits: multiply \(x_u+x_u^{\rm val}=0\) by \(x_v\) in the appropriate disjunct, and add the Boolean equation for \(x_v\) when \(x_u^{\rm val}=1\). This gives the conditional value of each pair using formal degree two. Combine the bit and pair values under their assignment failures, weaken to the full list of failures and the target equation, and cut out the assignments. Every gate has fixed arity and costs a bounded number of primitive steps.

Now add the two derived product equations \(p_i+x_u x_v=0\) and \(q_i+x_u x_v=0\). They give \(p_i+q_i=0\). Together with the two prefix addition equations, this carries equality to the next pair of prefixes. The final equality and the required opposite parity yield \(1=0\) and then the empty line. All steps use original strict source variables or original-variable polynomials, without proof-only product declarations. The cost is \(O(m)\) proof nodes of bounded arity and width and \(O(m\log(m+1))\) bits with binary source and parent indices. On the three-edge path the actual 44 original clauses give a 619-node proof; its first cancellation is at zero-based row 119. A short proof is also possible with exact *local* grouping in plain Boolean logic: the shared arguments of each pair of AND gates force the same output, so derive their two-bit equality once and add the equalities. Even one extra nonlinear observation changes the public-only experiment if it has a certified link to both sides, for example the actual common quadratic \(Q(x)\). The stated lower bounds concern the specified affine questions and per-evaluator inferences. They do not supply a lower bound on general 3-SAT algorithms.

## A control with different product gates

On a path with an even number of edges, keep the first evaluator as written and let the second compute each consecutive pair of terms as one product, \(lm+mr=m(l+r)\). Its input to that product is an actual XOR gate. The two evaluators now have no AND gate with identical arguments. They compute the same scalar quadratic, so the public affine-case theorem applies without alteration. A private query can search for a discrepancy among the three-product group parities, then query its middle bit to make the group's functions affine.

The short primitive proof also survives. Derive \(h+l+r=0\) from the XOR's four strict clauses, multiply this derived singleton by the original bit \(m\), then add the three actual gate equations for \(p=lm, p'=mr, q=mh\). This proves \(p+p'+q=0\) at formal degree two. Updating the two prefix sums uses a bounded number of additional linear combinations per group. For four path edges, 52 strict clauses give a 736-node unnamed proof. For sixteen edges, 220 clauses give a checked 3,184-node proof. This control removes literal reuse of a product gate as an explanation for the short proof.
