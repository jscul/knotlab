# The full scalar table of a source with junctions of arity at most four

**Theorem.** Let \(H\) be a finite reachable presentation all of whose junctions have arity at most four. Let \(R\) be any finite reachable presentation under the same serial rule, with two or three fresh distinctions per addition. Every bijection preserving the full scalar bond-distance table identifies the ordinary junction supports in the two presentations. It preserves the entire allowed-state relation and all actual three-parent choices. There is no restriction on the number of three-fresh additions on either side.

Use the bijection to put both presentations on one set of distinction names. In the classical wired graph, a shared distinction connects two ordinary sites and a loose distinction connects its holder to the exterior. Write \(K(e,f)=D(e,f)+1\), the smallest cardinality of a bond containing the two distinct named bits. A bond is a minimal nonempty cut. A junction star is a bond, so every pair of its bits has \(K\) at most its arity. Ordinary incidence is simple and bipartite. A site of \(R\) will be called low if its arity is at most four and high otherwise. ## Four-bit shores with at least three sites

Here are the needed frontier and nesting facts directly from the growth law and the two-sink theorem. Use a birth order of \(R\). For any ordinary set of at least two sites its latest two sites together send at least three outputs outside the set, since between them they make at least four outputs and can use at most one internally. For a set of at least three sites its latest three send at least four outputs out because at least six are made and at most two are used among these three, by bipartiteness and simplicity. At boundary size four, equality forces all three to make two outputs, with exactly two meetings among them. They form a path, supply the whole boundary, and there are no incoming crossings. Such an ordinary shore is closed under parents and contains the seed.

Restrict the original birth order to a parent-closed set. The resulting construction is legal. The first common witness of two parents already existed at the younger parent's birth, since adding a later first witness would already require a witness for the pair. Such an earlier witness is one of the younger site's parents and is retained. Removing sites cannot create a forbidden common witness. Thus the actual operands and required witnesses are available in the restricted prefix.

If the middle site of the last three sites' path were born first, it would use its two outputs at the other two. Those final sites would be the only sinks and every earlier output would have been consumed. If instead the middle site were born last, the two path endpoints would each have one loose bit, as well as their shared witness at the last site. Add one ordinary binary join on those two endpoint bits. The last site of the prefix and this new join would be the two sinks, again with every earlier output consumed. In each construction the seed has three children, all earlier nonsinks have two or three distinct children, and the last nonsink sends its two outputs one to each sink. Both cases contradict the two-sink theorem. Consequently the path follows birth order \(A\to M\to C\), with one frontier bit at \(A\), one at \(M\) and two at \(C\). [Two-sink theorem](../../VARIABLE-OUTPUT-READER-20261118-001D/VARIABLE-OUTPUT-READER-20261118-001D-P001_separator_inverse/inverse.md#two-elementary-source-restrictions)

For nesting, suppose two such parent-closed shores \(U,V\) are incomparable. Their intersection \(J\) contains the seed. There is no ordinary meeting between \(U\setminus J\) and \(V\setminus J\), since its earlier endpoint would be a parent of a site in the other shore. Counting boundaries edge by edge now gives
\[
                 |\delta J|+|\delta(U\cup V)|=|\delta U|+|\delta V|=8.
\]
If \(J\) has at least two sites, its boundary has size at least four. For just two these are the seed and a child with arities at least three, sharing one bit. For three or more use the latest-three bound. The union also has boundary at least four, so both boundaries are four. Write \(p\) for the number of bits from \(J\) into \(U\setminus J\), and \(q\) for the outputs of that difference leaving \(U\). Then \(4=|\delta J|-p+q\). The analogous counts for \(V\setminus J\) use disjoint inputs from \(J\). Both differences must export at least two bits, and hence both consume and export exactly two. The latest-two bound makes each difference a single site. Build \(J\) and append the two original binary joins. Their inputs are disjoint and their pair witnesses are already in \(J\). The joins consume its entire frontier, again giving the excluded two sinks. If \(J\) is only the seed, each difference consumes at least one of its three outputs and has \(q=p+1\). At least one difference consumes just one and exports two, so it consists of one site. Its original shore would have only two sites. This excludes incomparability in both cases.

## The low junctions are the same physical sites

The preceding short-equation theorem gives equality of all three- and four-bit equations. In \(H\) an ordinary site takes at most two parents and makes at least two fresh distinctions. Its four-bit bonds are its four-place stars and the sums of adjacent ternary stars. The ternary stars coincide in both presentations. Every actual four-place star of \(R\) is a four-place star of \(H\), since the sum of two adjacent ternary stars includes two bits of each, whereas a different ordinary site of \(R\) cannot share two bits with a ternary site. Conversely each four-place star of \(H\) is a four-bit bond in \(R\). If it is missing as a physical star there, its ordinary shore in \(R\) has at least three sites. A two-site shore would give a sum of adjacent ternary stars, which is impossible by the same two-intersections argument in \(H\). [One-sided short-equation theorem](../../VARIABLE-OUTPUT-READER-20261118-001F/VARIABLE-OUTPUT-READER-20261118-001F-P001_finite_interval/structural.md#recovering-every-equation-through-size-four)

Suppose a star is missing. By the preceding argument its shore and those of all other missing stars are nested in \(R\). Take an innermost such shore \(U\), with boundary \(F\). In \(H\), \(F\) is one ordinary junction. In \(R\), \(U\) is parent-closed and contains the seed. Its four outgoing boundary bits come from the path \(A\to M\to C\), one at \(A\), one at \(M\), and two at \(C\). The site \(C\) must be high because a low site here would give two different ordinary stars in \(H\), its own and \(F\), sharing two bits. The distinction between the two supports follows also from connectedness of the wired graph of \(R\), in which distinct ordinary shores have different cuts. At least one of \(A,M\) is high. Otherwise their shared distinction and their separate boundary distinctions give a triangle on \(A,M,F\) in the ordinary graph of \(H\). Consequently at most one bit of \(F\) has a low holder inside \(U\).

Partition the ordinary sites of \(H\) into the low \(R\) sites inside \(U\) and all the other sites. The first class includes the seed of \(R\), a common ternary star. The second includes \(F\). Any ordinary bit crossing this partition belongs to \(F\). If its other holder is a low \(R\) site outside \(U\), this follows from its two physical endpoints in \(R\). If its other holder is a missing star, the corresponding \(R\) shore contains \(U\). A bit in that larger boundary with an endpoint in \(U\) must leave \(U\) as well. Connectivity in \(H\) now forces precisely one crossing bit \(b\), an ordinary bridge. In \(R\) its inside holder is low. A low recipient across the boundary would give \(b\) three different ordinary holders in \(H\), namely the inside site, the recipient, and \(F\). A high recipient has at least two parents. Their earlier common witness gives an ordinary four-cycle through \(b\), so \(b\) is not a bridge. The remaining possibility is that \(b\) is physically loose in \(R\).

The full scalar table recognizes an ordinary bridge in every reachable presentation. For a named bit \(b\), remove it and link two other bits \(e,f\) when \(D(e,f)\ne D(e,b)+D(b,f)-1\). The resulting components are precisely the incidence components upon deletion. An ordinary bridge gives more than one, while a loose bit or an ordinary bit on a cycle gives only one. This contradiction rules out every missing star. Thus the actual ordinary stars of \(H\) are exactly the low ordinary stars of \(R\). ## Removing all high sites gives a useful orientation

Each bit has at least one ordinary holder in \(H\). Therefore no bit of \(R\) joins two high sites or is loose at a high site. Deleting every high site of \(R\) and leaving each of its incident bits loose at the other endpoint gives precisely the incidence of \(H\), on the same named bits. In particular all outputs of a high site had distinct ordinary children in \(R\).

Retain one birth order of \(R\). In the graph of \(H\), direct ordinary shared bits from earlier to later retained sites and all loose bits toward the exterior. This orientation is used only for counting. If a retained site made \(h\) outputs, took \(p\) inputs, and took \(k\) of them from subsequently deleted high sites, its numbers of outgoing and incoming bits in this orientation are \(h+k\) and \(p-k\). Its excess of outgoing over incoming is
\[
                            d=h-p+2k\ge0.
\]
For the seed take \(h=3,p=0\). A low site always has \(p\le2\) and \(h\ge2\). A site with no remaining incoming bits has excess equal to its arity, at least three. Every zero-excess site has exactly two incoming bits, from distinct earlier ordinary sites. The child of any high site has excess at least two. Such a child of excess two has exactly one surviving earlier parent since the only possibility is \(h=p=2,\ k=1\).

For any set \(U\) of ordinary sites let \(I(U)\) be the number of shared bits directed from its complement into \(U\). Every loose bit at a site of \(U\) leaves \(U\). Cancelling the internal contributions gives
\[
                    |\delta_H U|=\sum_{u\in U}d(u)+2I(U). \tag{1}
\]
In particular a cut containing a formerly high output now loose at its child must have that child on its ordinary shore. The estimates below hold for every ordinary shore, so they also hold for the minimum through a named pair.

## A five-place junction forces a discrepancy of at least one

Let \(x\) be a five-place junction in \(R\). Choose two of its children \(s,t\), and the respective previously shared output bits. Both children are low, different and on the same ordinary color. A cut of \(H\) containing those bits has \(s,t\in U\), contributing at least four to the sum in (1). If \(I(U)\ge1\), the cut has size at least six.

Suppose \(I(U)=0\) and the size is at most five. The earliest site of \(U\) has no incoming bit and contributes at least three. It must be one of \(s,t\), since otherwise the total is at least seven. Equality in the remaining budget forces this first site to have excess three, the other selected child to have excess two, and every other site to have excess zero. The second site could not have two earlier parents in \(U\), so has to be the other child. Its unique remaining parent would be the first site, impossible because these children of \(x\) have the same color. Thus in \(H\) every such pair of outputs has \(K\ge6\). The star at \(x\) gives \(K\le5\) in \(R\). This excludes a high junction of arity five anywhere, including presentations with other high sites.

## The earliest six-place junction also forces a discrepancy

If any high site remains, take the earliest one \(x\). It has three old parents and three children. In \(R\) all pairs among these six incident bits have \(K\le6\). Thus the same upper bound must hold in \(H\).

First take two of the children of \(x\) and suppose that each retains an incoming bit in the orientation of \(H\). A shore through their corresponding outputs has sum of excesses at least four. If \(I(U)=0\), its earliest site has no incoming bit, is different from these two children and contributes at least three more. If \(I(U)\ge2\), (1) gives at least eight. In the remaining case \(I(U)=1\), size at most six would give precisely two sites of excess two, the selected children, and all other sites of excess zero. A site with two distinct earlier parents cannot be first in \(U\) on only one incoming crossing. The first must be a selected child, spending that crossing on its sole surviving parent. The second must then be the other selected child since no site of excess zero can have two earlier parents in \(U\) at that point. The two children cannot be parent and child, since they have the same ordinary color. We have shown that any two children both retaining inputs give a pair with \(K_H\ge7\). To preserve all three pair entries, at least two of the three children would therefore have to have no remaining incoming bit. Take one such child \(s\). Its excess is at least three.

Let \(a\) be the latest of the three old parents of \(x\). Already at its birth \(a\) had at least two earlier parents. For each of its pairs with the other two parents of \(x\), a common neighbor existed when \(a\) was born. A first common neighbor could not be born later by taking that pair as inputs, because that very step would already require a common neighbor. These two witnesses must be different or \(x\) would have used a triple with a universal witness. All sites older than \(x\) survive in \(H\) and retain their earlier parents. In particular \(a\) has two of them.

In \(H\) the bits formerly connecting \(s\) and \(a\) to \(x\) are loose at \(s\) and \(a\). Take any cut through this pair, hence containing both sites on its ordinary shore. If \(I(U)\ge2\), the excess at \(s\) gives size at least seven. If \(I(U)=0\), all ancestors of \(a\) lie in \(U\), so the original seed lies there. The earliest nonseed site in \(U\) older than \(x\) must have been unary because only the seed is available as an earlier parent at that point and there are no incoming crossings. This site has excess at least one. Together the seed, that site and \(s\) give at least \(3+1+3=7\).

If \(I(U)=1\) and the cut size is at most six, the total excess is at most four. The seed is excluded because \(s\) already contributes at least three. Among the sites of \(U\) older than \(x\), the first must have only one parent, using the sole incoming crossing. It has excess at least one. Every other such site must have zero excess and thus two distinct earlier parents, both in \(U\). There cannot be a second such old site, since only one earlier site is available when it is added. This contradicts the presence of \(a\), which has two earlier parents. Therefore \(K_H\ge7\) on the displayed pair while the six-place star in \(R\) gives \(K_R\le6\). This excludes every remaining high site.

All junction supports now agree. Their homogeneous equations give precisely the allowed states. Counting single and double holders recovers the ordinary incidence and loose distinctions. Each triple therefore has the same physical holders, the same ordinary witnesses for each pair, and the same verdict on a common witness for all three. For the requested case of two three-fresh additions the sole possible rank increase was one. The argument above excludes a rival of any larger rank as well, at every admissible fresh count in the stated source class.

The source arity assumption matters in this proof. It gives the complete list of small equations, and it makes the retained sites' excesses nonnegative. The theorem leaves comparisons open when both presentations have a junction of arity five or six. The [bounded separator lemma](separator_horizon.md) supplies the remaining bridge step from pair minima through six. It also gives a cutoff of five for the two-addition case, where the sole strict rival makes only two fresh outputs at every join.
