## What stands

1. **Counting within a part is the state's counting where no knot cuts the pattern.** On all 3,789 read sets of the 21
   census types where R makes the knots commute, the closed part rule equals the counts of the state the signed knots fix
   except on 15 read sets of the one type with a cut knot. Products of knots signed by GF(2) contradict themselves on every
   type with an odd forced sign.
2. **R read off the whole is well defined and consistent with earlier readings.** Pairs of distinctions settle as soon
   as either is held twice; only pairs of loose distinctions of two newest knots stay open, for one round. Knot pairs
   change commutation only by the parity of their common children (1,035 changes, no exception). With the whole's R, no
   law read earlier changes sign, and no read set on the commuting closers gained a law from a later layer.
3. **What R contradicts is the knots' own laws.** On 290 of 300 sampled starts (uniform over the census) some two knots'
   operators fail to commute for good, so no state makes every knot hold: all 134 growing starts, all 70 that stall, 86
   of 96 that close. Only a reader who signs by GF(2), as the engines do, is contradicted by a later layer, on every
   commuting closer R forces odd (4 of 4).
4. **The bound.** Where a state exists the tables are quantum (Tsirelson's 2√2; the counting state with products of
   values at most 2, argued); three readers reach 4 on the four-knot closure, which is the quantum value; the two-reader 4
   has no state. Under R values are read together only inside knots, so the tables are contextuality, not separated
   readers.

**Not shown.** Any rule growth itself makes; R past four rounds; the counting state's bound computed for products of
values; other engines or weightings.

**Intuition (an exact link, not evidence).** The flip formula is the literature's statement that two products of
anticommuting values commute when they share an even number of anticommuting pairs; here the pairs are counted by
common children, so a birth with two parents is a single swap. Growth with ordinary births makes swaps one pair at a
time and so changes commutation parity by its own counts of common children: a state needs those swaps to come in
pairs.
