# Six failing pairs and the starting distinctions they need

## The lower bound and the three kinds

Write *F* for the number of failing pairs, *V* for the number of knots after separation, and use only the knots that occur. With coverage two, pairing occurrences is forced. At each knot, connect the predecessor and successor of each visit in a cycle. Each neighbour appears twice, so the connected parts determine the separated copies uniquely up to naming. Cross pairs stay distinct, a consequence of their distinct original endpoints. Minimality forces the failing pairs to stay connected through shared cross pairs, since each component on its own would cover every cross pair evenly. Thus there are 2F cross pairs and the separated Euler count is V − F. Splitting copies preserves every cycle reversal equation. A two-colouring, however, can exist only after splitting.

Here and below the literature's names for the non-orientable cases of Euler count one and zero are the projective plane and the Klein bottle, respectively. The separated count of any non-orientable choice is at most one. If it were negative with F ≤ 6, one would need 2F ≤ (F−1)(F−2)/2, which fails at each possible size. For count one, the cross-pair parity theorem equates even sign and two-colourability after separation. Therefore 2(V−1) ≤ ⌊V²/4⌋. Starting with four distinct knots, the first possibility is V = 7 and F = 6, with every cross pair between classes of three and four. [Cross-pair referee's sign argument](../../CELL-SIGN-20261127-001/referee-R1/hand_check.md) · [Mohar, Simonyi and Tardos, §1 and Lemma 6.1](https://www.renyi.hu/~simonyi/mstquadr.pdf)

In this seven-knot case each knot of the class of four sees all three in the other class. Its three visits use each unordered pair from that class once. Each such unordered pair consequently specifies a matching of the four knots in the larger class. Two equal matchings would disconnect the visits at their shared smaller-class knot. Thus the three matchings are exactly the three different matchings on four knots. Identifying any two knots within a class would coalesce cross pairs, and identifying across the classes would coalesce two knots on a cycle. This gives kind (a) and its three doubled holdings.

For count zero, write the parity of cycle lengths as a mod-two cohomology class *p*, and the orientation character as *w*. The cross-pair theorem gives sign ⟨p ∪ w, [S]⟩. In the literature's two crosscap coordinates the pairing is the dot product over the two-element field and w = (1,1). Its orthogonal complement consists of {0, w}. Thus even means two-colourable or else odd length exactly on orientation-reversing cycles. Two-colourability at count zero would require 2V ≤ ⌊V²/4⌋, hence at least eight knots. In all cases 2V ≤ V(V−1)/2, requiring at least five.

At five knots and count zero every pair is a cross pair, every knot is omitted once, and each pair appears among the other three holdings once as a value. For each omitted knot choose a matching of the remaining four. Up to relabelling, omission of 0 takes 12 and 34, and omission of 1 takes 03 and 24 (swap 3 and 4 if necessary). Omission of 2 then has just two candidates, 01 and 34, or 04 and 13, after excluding 03 and 14. The former repeats 34. The last two matchings are forced to be 02 and 14, then 01 and 23. Write the first pair as positions 1 and 3 of a cycle and the second pair as positions 2 and 4, with the smaller holder first in each. Reversing the cycles of omissions 3 and 4 balances all ten cross pairs. All six labelled versions and their orders are in the [five-knot certificates](record_counts.json).

For six separated knots and count zero, exactly three of the 15 possible cross pairs are absent. Their pattern exhausts five cases. For a fixed choice of absent pairs, form the three cycles on each four-knot subset, discard any cycle using an absent pair, and select six distinct cycles giving two visits at each remaining pair. Keep connected choices with one local cycle at each knot. The following small certificate distinguishes existence from the claim of exhaustiveness. ## Credit and scope

The literature's smallest *simple quadrangulation* on the count-zero non-orientable surface has six vertices. Liu, Ellingham and Ye also give the minimum order for count −1. Matsumoto, Nakamoto and Yonekura exhibit the bipartite seven-vertex example in their Figure 2. Fijavž and coauthors give the corrected list of eleven irreducible simple quadrangulations for the count-zero case in Figure 4, documenting the omission from Nakamoto's earlier list of ten. A six-vertex example must be irreducible, since a valid contraction would produce a smaller one on the same surface. This places both six-knot kinds in the known list. The abstract minimum, the doubled holdings, and the birth-rule comparison answer different questions. The seven-pair example of count −1 was not matched to a particular published embedding.
