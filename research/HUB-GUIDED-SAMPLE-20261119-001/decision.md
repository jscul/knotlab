## What was proved

For a fixed growing end, each maximal group has expected final sampling mass one. The sampler starts with uniform ranks in an exactly counted two-block part, restores the other junctions one by one, branches to genuine children, and thins the population with exact integer rescaling. A group’s final sampling probability is proportional to its final particle multiplicity within that run. Multiplying a sampled indicator by the run’s total mass gives an unbiased count of that property. Every produced group has a unique parent in the reverse construction, even when its restriction to the original part is not maximal. The result holds on every finite roster to which the growth law applies. [Weighting and group probabilities](weighting.md) · [Proof and implementation checks](formal.md)

Three disjoint families on start-03 give a deterministic lower bound of **2,174,695,764,693,287 births**. Two structural counts used full rankers that return a literal group on every draw. A separate exact scan accounts for every residual pair. There are 203 held groups, no isolated junctions, 1,241 residual pairs and one offshoot. Thus the number of births is exactly the maximal-group total plus 1,039. [Disjoint families](structure.md) · [Exact pair correction](births.md)

The earlier $10^{-351}$ figure referred to one specified legal group’s search path under uniform descent. Its 58.9-fold spread came from a different, population-weighted sampler. Both earlier calculations stand. Every one of its sixteen estimates was below the new structural lower bound. The present group-valued hub sampler illustrates why landing on real groups is insufficient. Its primary panel found 1,408 legal large groups, with a 5,245-fold spread. A second integer-weighted branching design had a spread of about 945 million across 24 runs. ## The other growing ends and the shape of a group

The same prefix method was applied to the first unread round of every other selected start. The stage-specific panels each have 16 independent runs of 128 particles, and each starts from an exactly counted two-block part. Seven of those birth totals were subsequently determined exactly. The other six remain estimates. Start-01 gives an exact example of nearly complete sampling failure by a different proposal. All 64 hub draws landed on held groups. The stage actually makes 249,882 births. The three renamings 02, 05 and 08 give a positive calibration, with three independent sample means around their common exact answer of 1,061,953. On start-03 the 28,008 large groups that formed round eight were all checked. Two blocks cover 24,955; the other 3,053 require at least three. Partner replacements occur in 27,588, but none of the 27,913 groups with at least eight parents admits independent partners for three quarters of its members. For round nine, all 24 mass-weighted physical draws from the final panel required three or more blocks. Each had 9,599 to 12,155 parents and only 4 to 15 witnessed independent partner replacements. The 48 draws from the two initial particle orders also required at least three blocks. Nevertheless, the exactly counted 13-and-4 overlap already supplies 183,833,974,142,787 legal round-nine groups that need precisely two blocks. The two readings explain how a small number of independent replacements can occur in a group of thousands, and why a zero frequency in a finite sample cannot classify every group. ## What one sample can tell us

A start is one possible arrangement. We can follow that same arrangement and draw groups from its current growing end. If we keep track of how each group was drawn, the draws can tell us roughly how many junctions come next and show which old blocks those groups cross. An exact count inside part of the growing end can also prove a minimum for the whole round. Even a very large next round leaves open whether that particular growth continues forever. Learning how often other starts behave this way requires a separately named weighting on starts.
