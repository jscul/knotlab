# The four-place cycle exposes the false six-distinction equation

**Theorem.** On the named cycle, bond distances through three rule out the proposed parity equation on six distinctions. The conclusion holds after every lawful mixed continuation and after any renaming of the current table. The same proof works inside a larger prefix wherever the six named junctions have the specified attachments. Independently, at every finite width, distances through five recover *all* junctions, states and legal next groups in any source with at most one two-parent, two-output join and three outputs everywhere else. The comparison in the latter theorem admits every reachable mixed-output rival. [False-support theorem](obstruction.md) · [All-size inverse](cycle_inverse.md)

**Inputs and comparison.** Start with a ternary even parity constraint. An addition uses one loose distinction from each of one, two or three different holders and creates two or three fresh distinctions. Each pair of holders must already have an ordinary junction meeting both. For three holders no one ordinary junction may meet all three. A bond is a minimal nonempty support of a parity equation. The bond distance of two distinct bits is one less than the size \(K\) of their smallest common bond. A statement through distance \(d\) permits recording every larger entry identically as “farther.” The reader receives only this table on the present distinctions and, for a single verdict, the proposed unordered group. Sources and permutations in the proofs certify the statements under all renamings. They supply no inputs to the reader.

## The uniform join reader

The proof first finds two bits of each five- or six-place junction whose measured bond size equals its arity and for which no outside bit is close enough to both. The short-equation theorem handles the smaller junctions. Thus all source junctions supply equations in the rival. For reverse inclusion, a shortest extra rival equation would need five or six bits. There is only one ternary junction in these sources. Translating the hypothetical support by the forced equations then puts at most two of its bits at any one ordinary junction. A five-bit support would put at least four of its bits at one junction. At six, the remaining possibility concentrates at the four-place sites. When each link between such sites lies on at most one cycle, the putative equation must concentrate on a single cycle. Adding equations along that cycle either shortens the support or produces a pair too far apart to belong to the new support. Once the spaces agree, the possible small bonds and independence of the actual junction equations identify the physical junctions themselves. Their current memberships give all permissible joins. [Proof and concrete families allowing arbitrarily many exceptional binary additions](cycle_inverse.md)

The reverse argument applies whenever the seed is the sole ternary site, all bonds of size at most six enclose at most two ordinary sites, every high site has an identifying pair, and the four-place graph satisfies the cycle condition just given. This includes an infinite family with arbitrarily many exceptional binary joins and saturated high sites. The independent local check on a four-cycle took all sixteen translations of each six-set for every pattern of external holder coincidences allowed by ordinary simplicity and bipartiteness. It excluded all 1,024 candidates passing the initial weight and pair tests among 564,564 word-and-exterior combinations. The analytic proof covers all finite sizes. The shortest extra equation can also be confined to one part of the four-place graph even when cycles overlap. At least five of its bits would have to touch a single maximal two-connected block. Toggling any of that block's four-place equations keeps the problem inside it. For the six-site block with eight internal meetings, the 64 local translations rule out the extra equation under every exterior attachment model. The other three small overlapping block types also have no survivor. Thus arbitrary combinations of bridges, simple cycles of any length, and blocks on at most six junctions satisfy the same all-size reader under the stated short-shore and identifying-pair conditions. ## What remains in the unrestricted question

For a source with a single ternary site where each addition makes at least as many distinctions as it takes, the short-shore property follows by counting the change in its boundary each time one selected site is added. In such a source, pair readings through four fix **all equations on five or fewer distinctions** against every reachable rival. If the source has maximum arity five, any opposing full-table rival must contain both a six-place site and a three-parent site that makes only two fresh distinctions. This statement allows arbitrary interactions among the four-place sites. Under the additional high-site identifying-pair condition, an extra six-bit equation would occupy at most twelve four-place sites of ordinary diameter at most three, including an ordinary cycle of length four or six. [Proofs, exact comparison classes and histories](nonshrinking.md)

There is a necessary event on the remaining single-ternary branch. Two presentations in which every bond through size six encloses at most two sites can already be read from distance five. If some three-or-more-site shore instead has six or fewer boundary distinctions, its first three sites have boundary at least seven. At its first crossing below seven, a three-parent join must consume three of exactly seven boundary bits and supply two. The resulting six-bit equation is minimal. This singles out a possible rival to examine without scanning all widths. [Seven-to-six proof](nonshrinking.md#exactly-when-three-sites-can-have-a-short-boundary)

No pair of reachable full tables with a legal-to-forbidden triple is exhibited here. An opposing example still requires a single bijection preserving every pair entry and changing a witnessed triple. Establishing that two bits can be exchanged in a shorter table, or that some equation changes, addresses a weaker question.
