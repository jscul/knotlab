# Locating a shortest extra equation in one part of the four-place graph

Assume three properties of the source. The seed is its only ternary junction. Every bond with at most six distinctions has at most two sites on its ordinary shore. Each five- or six-place junction has a pair of distinctions whose minimum is its arity and whose common neighborhood at that threshold is exactly its support. The competitor may be any finite source of the original mixed law. This section allows the ordinary graph \(G_4\) induced by the four-place sites to have intersecting cycles. Every comparison uses the whole bond table clipped through \(D=5\).

**Confinement lemma.** If the rival has an extra equation, there is one of size six of which at least five seed-free bits are incident to a single maximal two-connected block of \(G_4\). All equal-weight translations of this word by stars in that block keep their seed-free bits incident to the block.

By the [short-word proof](cycle_inverse.md#what-an-extra-equation-would-force), every source equation holds in the rival. A shortest extra word \(W\) has six bits, all mutually within \(K=6\), with at most two at any holder and one at the seed. These same tests hold for every weight-six translate by source equations. Each nonseed bit \(e\) has a nonempty set \(V_e\) of one or two four-place endpoints. If \(V_e,V_f\) neither meet nor have a link between them, the two bits share their single non-four-place holder. Thus each bit has at most one such partner. Their four-place endpoints belong to one connected component.

Within that component, take the classical block incidence tree. Its nodes consist of all ordinary vertices and all maximal two-connected blocks, counting each bridge as a two-vertex block. Join each vertex to the blocks containing it. To verify the tree property, two maximal blocks cannot have two vertices in common, since their union would stay connected after deleting any one vertex. Each ordinary link lies in a cycle or is a bridge, so the incidence graph is connected. A cycle of distinct blocks meeting successively at distinct vertices would again have a union connected after deleting any one vertex. The other direction around the cycle preserves the connection when a meeting vertex is deleted. The union would contain more than one maximal block, a contradiction.

For each \(e\), let \(U_e\) consist of its one or two four-place vertex nodes and all block nodes incident to these vertices. It is a subtree because two holders of a bit lie on an ordinary link in one block. If two subtrees were disjoint, their endpoint sets would neither meet nor be adjacent. Each of the two bits would have used its only possible distant partner. The other three or more subtrees would each meet both. On the unique connecting path the two nearest endpoints are block nodes, since every block incident to a holder was included. They are different, so every connecting subtree contains an intermediate ordinary vertex. A bit contains that vertex in its subtree only when the vertex is one of its holders. This contradicts the two-bit occupancy bound. All the subtrees therefore intersect pairwise. Subtrees of a tree with this property have a common node. Root the tree and take a subtree whose nearest node to the root has greatest depth. The root paths to its intersections with each other subtree show that they all include that nearest node. A vertex can hold only two bits, and the two vertices of a bridge can hold at most four. Thus the common node is a block \(B\) containing a holder of each of the five or six seed-free bits. Every bit toggled by a star at \(B\) touches \(B\), proving the translation assertion.

## Applying a local test inside a larger arrangement

A component of \(G_4\) left after removing the vertices of \(B\) can attach to at most one vertex of \(B\). Otherwise a path between two attachments through that component could be added to \(B\), and the enlarged subgraph would still be connected after deletion of any one vertex. In particular the graph induced by \(B\) and its immediately adjacent outside four-place vertices consists of \(B\) and leaves. Two such outside vertices at the same attachment have the same ordinary color, and an ordinary meeting across different attachments would enlarge \(B\).

In an equal-weight translation, form the graph induced by the four-place endpoints of the seed-free bits. The forest argument shows this graph must contain a cycle. It lies in the preceding graph of \(B\) with leaves. If \(B\) is a simple cycle, that entire cycle is therefore occupied in each translate. The [cycle elimination](cycle_inverse.md#confinement-to-one-cycle) now applies without any restriction on the other blocks.

For any fixed block, list its internal distinctions and all its external slots, with four distinctions per vertex in total. For a candidate word, at most one extra bit off this list may occur, and it must meet the unique seed. Every pair of seed-free bits in an equal-weight translate must share a holder or have adjacent four-place holders. For external slots at different, nonadjacent core vertices, the only remaining way is an identical non-four-place holder outside the block. Ordinary simplicity permits a shared outside holder to take at most one slot per core vertex; bipartiteness permits only core vertices of one color. Any exterior four-place holder is a singleton on the ports in this test by the previous paragraph. A partition of ports of each color, including singleton parts for loose bits, thus encompasses the possible outside identifications. One part may be the seed, with at most three incident distinctions. Pair comparisons involving a seed bit may be admitted freely when testing a necessary condition. Allowing extra comparisons this way only enlarges the candidate set.

The **six-site block with eight internal distinctions** has two sets of three sites and every meeting between the sets except one. Each set has four exterior slots. The local test considers all six-sets, adds each of the 64 sums of the six true site equations, rejects any shortening, and tests occupancy and pair bounds at every weight-six tie. Over all 640 exterior port models, 36,060 candidates pass the initial occupancy and pair tests. All fail a pair test after a weight-preserving translation. This is a finite proof for an arbitrary surrounding arrangement satisfying the hypotheses. In particular it applies to the repeated examples of widths 24, 45 and 87 and to any number of repetitions against **every** reachable rival. ## Every two-connected four-place block on at most six sites

A second finite test covers the three other nonsimple-cycle possibilities at these sizes. To see the list is complete, a two-connected block on at least three vertices is bipartite and has minimum degree two within the block. At four sites this forces a four-cycle. At five it forces two sites on one side meeting each of three on the other. At six with sides two and four it forces every cross-meeting. With sides three and three, each vertex can miss at most one meeting, so the missing pairs form a matching. Missing three gives a six-cycle, missing two gives the seven-meeting block, and missing one gives the eight-meeting block verified above. With zero missing, the latest of the six four-place sites would have three earlier neighbors. Every four-place site arose on at most two parents under this law. Thus the ninth meeting is unreachable.

For the five-site complete two-by-three block, the complete two-by-four block, and the three-by-three block missing two disjoint meetings, the check uses the same necessary translated-word tests and all exterior models. Respectively 25,968, 458,880 and 11,872 candidates pass the initial tests. Every one fails a tied test. The three finite reductions cover blocks inside arbitrary surrounding arrangements and no extrapolation in width is involved. [Other three block proofs and exact controls](small_blocks.md)

**All-size block theorem.** Under the three opening source conditions, the current table through \(D=5\) identifies all junctions, states and legal next groups against every reachable rival if each of the maximal four-place blocks is a bridge, a simple cycle of any length, or has at most six sites. The graph on all four-place sites may contain arbitrarily many of these blocks and they may meet at articulation sites. In this source class, a counterexample would need a maximal four-place block with at least seven sites and more than a simple cycle. The number seven here counts junctions in a block. The parity rule still has at most six distinctions per junction.
