# Erasing a distinction with a recorded answer

**Proved for finite words and the stated complete instruments. Independent scientific referee pending.** Measuring a complete set of change answers and dropping the measured distinctions reproduces their supplied probabilities, gives a state conditional on each answer and can create a relative minus sign on surviving words. One joint reversal usually asks a different binary question. An ordinary growth step that spends a loose mark or brings no fresh marks preserves the identity of every old distinction. An ordinary merger can forget its input register while disclosing no sign. The exact limitation of the observed singleton records on a possible alternative instrument is given in section 5.

The positive-uniform probability formula for *all* individual change outcomes, including disjoint ordinary reads, was previously proved by the freedom study. The calibration study showed why a measured probability does not specify the continuation. Our additions are the retained and discarded state comparison, the arbitrary signed preparation, the distinction between the two readings of a set, and the test of candidate erasures against their complete ordinary output records. ## 1. The complete erasure

Write a normalized word preparation as $c(z,r)$, where $z$ lists the $k$ erased bits, $r$ lists all remaining bits, and $G=2^k$. Labels are binary, their sums inside signs are modulo two, and $h$ ranges over the $G$ bit lists. Define a linear output row for each answer,
$$
(K_h c)(r)=G^{-1/2}\sum_z(-1)^{h\cdot z}c(z,r)=b_h(r).
$$
On receiving answer $h$, the probability is $p_h=\sum_r|b_h(r)|^2$ and the remaining state is $b_h/\sqrt{p_h}$ when $p_h>0$. A zero-probability output has no conditional state. A retained register contains $h$. A fine question on retained erased bits would also leave their normalized character eigenstate. This is a specified instrument. A subsequent reset could have the same original answer probabilities with different remaining states.

For two erased words $z,z'$, the sum of $(-1)^{h\cdot(z+z')}$ over $h$ is $G$ on equality. On inequality, toggling any position where $z,z'$ differ cancels the sum in pairs. Thus $\sum_h K_h^*K_h=I$ and, including exterior coherences,
$$
   \sum_h b_h(r)\overline{b_h(r')}=\sum_z c(z,r)\overline{c(z,r')}.
$$
All outcomes together have mass one. Throwing away every answer leaves exactly the ordinary quantum partial trace over the erased bits. Simply forgetting an ordinary bit or losing its history can give this unconditional result without making any of the individual $b_h$ physically available. For a mixture $\rho$, replace $b_hb_h^*$ by $K_h\rho K_h^*$ and take its trace. Conditioning on a simultaneous ordinary read of some retained bits restricts $r$ to those values with its original joint normalization. For $k=0$ there is one empty character, probability one and state $c$. For no surviving bits the post-state of a possible character is scalar up to global phase.

For the equal positive preparation on a nonempty allowed set $S$, let $S_r$ be the completions at a fixed $r$ and $n=|S|$. Then $b_0(r)=|S_r|/\sqrt{Gn}$. The **amplitude** counts ways. The conditional ordinary mass on $r$ is proportional to $|S_r|^2$. For $h\ne0$ the numerator is the difference between the numbers of even and odd $h\cdot z$ among the completions. Jointly with $r$ its squared magnitude divided by $Gn$ is the prior counting identity. On combining the outcomes, $r$ instead has mass $|S_r|/n$. Its conditional distribution need not be uniform when each $r$ is possible.

## 2. One simultaneous reversal

For a nonempty erased set let $t$ reverse every bit of that set. On the old space the binary reading has projectors $P_s=(I+sX_t)/2$ for $s=+1,-1$. Its probabilities are $(1+s\langle X_t\rangle)/2$ and its retained states are $P_sc/\|P_sc\|$ when possible. For an empty $t$, $P_+=I,P_-=0$. To remove one binary degree of freedom for $t\ne0$, pick one representative $w$ in each pair $\{w,w+t\}$. The two amplitudes on the pair address after recording the outcome are $(c(w)+s c(w+t))/\sqrt2$. This is a complete, normalized pair transform. It retains the exterior and $k-1$ relative bits of the erased set. Changing a representative multiplies a minus-branch amplitude at that address by $-1$. Probabilities of the addresses are invariant. Subsequent interference across surviving addresses requires the registered convention or the corresponding phase correction. Simply renaming which member is first at every pair introduces only a common minus sign in that branch.

Each character in section 1 is an eigenstate of simultaneous reversal with sign $(-1)^{|h|}$, where $|h|$ counts ones. Consequently $P_s$ is the sum of the individual character projectors whose signs multiply to $s$. Coarsening the **probabilities** by this product gives the binary probabilities. Recording only the all-clean result versus its complement gives a different binary effect for $k\ge2$. With $k=2$ and singleton 00, all-clean has probability $1/4$, while product-plus has probability $1/2$. For either product sign the unresolved binary projection on the retained two bits is proportional to $|00\rangle+s|11\rangle$. The next ordinary read gives 00 and 11 with probability $1/2$ each. Individually measuring both characters, recording them and then pooling by their product gives all four ordinary pairs with probability $1/4$ each on either product answer. These are inequivalent instruments on retained erased bits.

If only “all clean” versus “one or more marked” is recorded, the latter has probability $1-p_0$ and unnormalized remaining state $\sum_{h\ne0}K_h\rho K_h^*$. Normalize by $1-p_0$ only when positive. There is generally no single signed exterior vector representing this complementary branch. On a fixed exterior, two scalar rows without an additional record can resolve identity on at most a two-dimensional input, because the sum of their two outer products has rank at most two. Erasing $k>1$ marks with just two *reported* outcomes therefore requires residual labels, a discarded environment, or a more general multi-row instrument. It cannot sum and difference all $2^k$ alternatives into only two scalar amplitudes while preserving total probability on every input.

After all of $E$ is dropped, the product-pooled conditional exterior states **agree**. Expand $P_s\rho P_s$ in the orthonormal character basis of the erased bits. Its partial trace kills terms involving two different characters. The surviving terms sum to $\sum_{h:(-1)^{|h|}=s}K_h\rho K_h^*$. One can instead use the pair transform with a reference defined on the erased coordinates, then discard its $k-1$ relative bits. The same reduced expression results. An exterior-dependent change of phase convention on the pair address is an extra conditional operation if its phase correction is omitted.

## 3. What the knot test says

K1 says that the original allowed set omits a word. K3 says it contains a word. K2 says that for **each** single distinguished variable, including the contact, every assignment of all the others has at least one completion. For amplitudes strictly positive on exactly $S$, this is equivalent to the all-clean output having full ordinary support after each such one-variable erasure. It then has full support after every nonempty erased set, since every smaller projection of a full projection is full. Conversely, demanding fullness for all nonempty erasures includes the singletons. For one prescribed larger erasure, take $S=\{000,011\}$, with displayed high bit first. Erasing the two high positions allows either value on the low bit. Erasing the low bit leaves only two of four exterior words. K2 fails. The original relation and all local law fixtures were evaluated using the canonical knot checker. Fullness is a support statement about the relation to which K2 was applied. The conjunction of several individually lawful knots need not have that property. In the four-knot not-all-equal example, setting all six child-only marks to zero forces all three old marks to one, violating their old knot. That exterior has zero ways under every erasure outcome. An empty original relation fails K3 and has no normalized uniform preparation. On a signed pair on one bit, opposite coefficients cancel on the clean result even though the ordinary existential projection is nonempty. Adding an environment retaining the old alternatives can reduce their interference. For an unsigned preparation with an orthogonal tag on each old $z$, the clean probability for a one-bit erasure is $1/2$ even if both ways were possible. With general amplitudes and accessible tags the correct formula applies to each retained tag, or to their reduced density if inaccessible. ## 4. Two laws and what a marked result records

For an isolated even-parity knot on $d\ge3$ marks and any $1\le k\le d$, the erased words on each exterior $r$ form one parity class of size $2^{k-1}$. Its character sum is $2^{k-1}$ at $h=0$ and $(-1)^{|r|}2^{k-1}$ at the all-ones character. Every other sum cancels by changing two erased bits on which $h$ differs. The two nonzero answers have probability $1/2$ each. Flipping all $k$ marks preserves parity for even $k$ and changes it for odd $k$. Therefore the binary plus probability is respectively one or $1/2$. The all-clean result can thus occur when a single erased bit is completely determined by the exterior. A clean report alone does not show that nothing recorded that bit.

For the isolated not-all-equal law put $n=2^d-2$ and $G=2^k$. For $0<k<d$ each nonconstant exterior has all $G$ completions. Each of the two constant exteriors lacks only its matching constant completion. Thus
$$
 p_0=1-\frac{2(G-1)}{Gn}, \qquad p_h=\frac{2}{Gn}\quad(h\ne0).
$$
On constant-zero and constant-one exterior respectively the nonzero character numerators are $-1$ and $-(-1)^{|h|}$. For $k\ge2$ these marked outcomes arise from fibers of size $G-1$, even when there were three or more possible erased words. When $k=d$, the sole exterior has $n$ completions, $p_0=n/2^d$, and for nonzero $h$ the probabilities are $4/(2^dn)$ at even $|h|$ and zero at odd $|h|$. For the single binary reversal, any nonempty proper mask maps exactly two allowed words to the two forbidden constants, giving plus probability $1-1/n$. Reversing the whole knot preserves the law and yields plus surely. These formulas include the three-place values $5/6$ for one clean mark, $3/4$ for two clean marks, and $5/6$ for the binary flip of those two marks. For both laws an empty erasure is the identity.

The one-bit unsigned marked output is exactly the signed difference of the two potential completions. It has nonzero amplitude exactly where just one value extends the exterior. The marked port alone never says which old value was forced. Both singleton orientations yield it half the time, and a common sign on every surviving amplitude has no effect on subsequent probabilities. Under the supplied instrument it can generate a **relative** sign on the remaining words from an originally positive state. At the even ternary the clean and marked exterior vectors are proportional to $(1,1,1,1)$ and $(1,-1,-1,1)$. A further supplied single-bit change read of the exterior returns plus certainly for the first and minus certainly for the second. At the not-all-equal ternary the vectors are $(1,2,2,1)$ and $(-1,0,0,1)$ with first-outcome probabilities $5/6,1/6$. Flipping both surviving bits distinguishes the two vectors with certainty using the supplied change reading. The difference row of the first instrument is precisely the step that created the sign. A native derivation requires a physical source for that operation and both of its ports.

## 5. What complete ordinary records permit

Fix two different input words $u,v$ with equal masses. Keep the apparatus, histories, program and event fixed. In the signed comparison put coefficients $(1,s)/\sqrt2$, $s=+1,-1$. A complete ordinary instrument assigns distributions $p_o,q_o$ on its jointly available outcomes $o$ to the two singleton words. In ordinary history arithmetic either crossed sign, or the dephased mixture, gives $(p_o+q_o)/2$. Coarse graining, discarding old words or paths, and copying an already determined answer into two ready indicator registers all preserve that equality. Ordinary files can document a history without creating a register accessible at the cut. The previous sign proof covers repeated or adaptive sign-blind kernels. [Complete ordinary-history theorem](../../../proofs/SIGN-RECORDS-AND-RECEIVER-TICKS-20261120-001A/SIGN-RECORDS-AND-RECEIVER-TICKS-20261120-001A-P001_record_obstruction/proof.md)

One may ask a stronger and different question. Among **all** positive normalized measurement effects consistent with these fine singleton distributions, what contrast could an output pool $F$ have? Let its $o$th two-by-two effect have diagonal entries $p_o,q_o$ and off-diagonal $x_o$. Positivity gives $|x_o|\le\sqrt{p_oq_o}$, and completeness gives $\sum_o x_o=0$. The plus-minus probability difference of $F$ is $2\operatorname{Re}\sum_{o\in F} x_o$. Its sharp absolute maximum is
$$
 2\min\left(\sum_{o\in F}\sqrt{p_oq_o}, \sum_{o\notin F}\sqrt{p_oq_o}\right).
$$
To attain it mathematically, assign nonnegative real $x_o$ in $F$ totalling the smaller capacity and nonpositive values totalling its negative outside. Each chosen magnitude fits its own interval. The resulting positive matrices sum to identity. The matrices' positive square roots realize a complete instrument on these two inputs. Orthogonal inputs, if present, may use their prescribed diagonal outcomes in separate blocks. This construction fixes only the named fine-output probabilities on singleton inputs. A prescribed map of output states, a coherent coupling to existing memories or a separately fixed transition amplitude can impose additional constraints. The binary-totals-only bound replaces each sum by the corresponding square root of pooled masses and can be strictly looser. If one side has disjoint fine output support, all compatible measurements returning that pool have zero contrast. For example, let both singletons give one common success with probability one half. On failure let one singleton give only result A and the other only result B. The binary totals alone allow unit contrast, whereas the joint record containing A and B forces zero. This algebraic example explains why retaining both sets of outcomes is material to the physical test.

A perfect difference of one on the two signed inputs requires a binary positive effect to be the plus (or minus) projector on their pair. Indeed its expectation 1 on one eigenvector and 0 on the other forces it to act as identity and zero on those vectors. Its singleton probabilities must both be $1/2$. A biased unsharp effect can have weak contrast with different singleton probabilities. A fixed complete ordinary word and apparatus have no intrinsic fair sign coin. An independently randomized apparatus or a specified conditional sign instrument can give fairness. The source of that extra randomness is an observable distinction in the experiment.

A normalized useful comparison is to append fresh records $|f_u\rangle, |f_v\rangle$ conditionally to the old words while keeping their old identities. Before applying any eraser, every ordinary output still has zero sign contrast. A supplied complete **joint reversal eraser** on the old difference sees singleton probabilities $1/2$ and signed plus chances $(1+s\operatorname{Re}\langle f_u|f_v\rangle)/2$. If each fresh record is positive uniform on a set of sizes $N_u,N_v$, the overlap is the number of shared fresh strings divided by $\sqrt{N_uN_v}$. An orthogonal which-word copy makes the overlap zero. If the data and such a copy are both subjected to supplied complementary erasers, their individually fair signs $a,b$ have joint chances $(1+sab\,\operatorname{Re}\langle f_u|f_v\rangle)/4$. With identical fresh records the two signs agree certainly on the plus input and disagree certainly on minus. With unequal records only the available overlap returns. Ignoring the second outcome removes even that correlation from the first report. This interference conditional on complementary measurements has a known physical optics counterpart. Here the evidence concerns specified knot-law words and which data ordinary growth retains. [Walborn and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevA.65.033818)

## 6. What a native growth step erases

For any ordinary funded step, each paid loose old distinction obtains a second identified holder. Each old holder, its rule, and the value of each marked old variable in a complete word extension are preserved. A zero-fresh birth adds only a holder of already present inputs. The full word may therefore still be queried to recover an old input mark on every branch. In a close, termination of the list of new loose marks makes the observed growing end empty. This amounts to a coarse reading of many full words. In a continuing step, the fresh marks may themselves carry information about the old word. Neither step names both complementary character ports. For either law this conclusion follows directly from the physical update and from the complete-record theorem in section 5, without assuming the fresh extension weighting used for our numbers.
