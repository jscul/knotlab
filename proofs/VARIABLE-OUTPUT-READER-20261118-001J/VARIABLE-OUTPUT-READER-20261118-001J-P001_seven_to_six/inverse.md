# Reading a six-bit boundary after further additions

Write \(K(e,f)=D(e,f)+1\) for the minimum cardinality of a bond containing two distinct named bits. In the classical wired graph, all loose bits end at the exterior. An ordinary shore of a cut consists of the sites on the side away from the exterior.

## Five-bit equations at the first shrink

Suppose the seed is the sole ternary site, and this is the first step at which any cut with at least three sites on its ordinary shore has size at most six. That step must be a three-parent, two-output addition. Three selected ordinary sites already have boundary at least seven by their degrees and bipartiteness. A new selected site can lower the previous boundary by at most one, and equality requires all three parents inside. Thus at this step every ordinary shore of three or more sites still has boundary at least six. In particular, all bonds through size five have shores consisting of one site or two adjacent sites.

Under this last short-shore hypothesis alone, **every equation through size five transfers to any reachable rival matching \(K\) through five**. This includes sources already containing several three-parent, two-output sites. The three- and four-bit equations transfer by the short-equation theorem. For a five-place site \(v\), two of its bits have \(K=5\) and no outside bit is within five of both. If one bit is loose choose it and any other. Otherwise \(v\) has five different ordinary neighbors. If the seed meets \(v\), the other four neighbors have the seed's color and avoid it. If it does not meet \(v\), at most three neighbors of \(v\) can meet the seed. Choose two neighbors that differ from the seed and avoid it. Every short outside partner of either chosen bit must meet its opposite endpoint, because a short two-site bond between distinct endpoints through five would need degrees three and four. No other bit can meet both same-color neighbors. This forces the five-place star as a rival bond. All source five-bit equations are now present, since the remaining ones are sums of the seed and an adjacent four-place site. [Short-equation theorem](../../VARIABLE-OUTPUT-READER-20261118-001F/VARIABLE-OUTPUT-READER-20261118-001F-P001_finite_interval/structural.md#recovering-every-equation-through-size-four)

In the other direction, any rival five-bit word is a bond because the minimum nonzero equation size is three. If it meets the common seed in two or more bits, adding the seed gives a common word of size at most four. Otherwise at least four of the bits avoid the seed. Any pair of these that did not meet at a source site would require a size-five two-site bond using a bit at the seed. Pairwise meeting bits in a simple triangle-free graph share a holder. The holder has size at most five because any two bits at a six-place holder have distinct, same-color opposite endpoints and cannot occur in a smaller bond. Its forced star differs from the rival word in at most two bits, so the two words agree. A rival star of size at most five must moreover be an actual source star, since a sum involving the seed contains two of its bits. [Five-bit reverse argument](../../VARIABLE-OUTPUT-READER-20261118-001I/VARIABLE-OUTPUT-READER-20261118-001I-P001_cycle_obstruction/nonshrinking.md#five-place-source-equations-survive-arbitrary-rivals)

There is also a description of the new six-bit bonds with large shores. Write \(P\) for the three named inputs and \(L\) for the two fresh outputs. Each such bond equals \(L\cup J\), where \(P\cup J\) was an old seven-cut and \(|J|=4\). In fact the old ordinary shore had at least three sites and contained all three distinct same-color input holders. Conversely, every old seven-cut containing \(P\) gives a new six-word of this form. The new word is a bond because a nonminimal six-word would need two disjoint ternary bonds, and the seed is the only one. The old seven-cuts are precisely the minimum cuts separating all three input holders from the exterior. Every such shore has connected sides at size seven, since a decomposition into two bonds would have sizes three and four, each containing at most one input of a legal triple. [Pair bound for legal triples](../../VARIABLE-OUTPUT-READER-20261118-001E/VARIABLE-OUTPUT-READER-20261118-001E-P001_marked_symmetry/scalar_inverse.md)

The old minimum shores are closed under intersection and union by cut submodularity. For incomparable shores \(U,V\), there are no ordinary meetings between their two differences, since equality is necessary in submodularity. Each nonempty difference takes as many bits from the boundary of \(U\cap V\) as it exports. It takes at least two, since its own cut has at least three bits and double its intake equals that cut size. The intakes are disjoint and draw on the four bits outside \(P\). Each difference therefore has cut size four and consists of a single four-place site, by the assumption before the first shrink and the unique ternary site. In particular any two of the old minimum shores having different numbers of sites are nested.

## The original nine-site equations remain visible at every width

Use the following names in the width-21 example. Begin at \(T\) with two unary, three-output children \(a,b\). Make the two-output binary site \(U\) on \(a,b\), then the two-output binary site \(c\) on \(T,U\). Make \(A,B,C\) on the pairs \(ab,ac,bc\), respectively, with two outputs each. Their pair witnesses are \(a,b,c\), and no site meets all three. Make the last two-output site \(t\) on \(A,B,C\). The six initially loose bits \(F\) consist of one bit at each of \(U,A,B,C\) and both outputs at \(t\). The ordinary graph on these nine sites will be denoted by \(G\).

**Theorem.** After any finite lawful continuation, a bijection to a binary equation space preserving just the pair readings through \(K=5\) forces all nine original site equations. Their sum \(F\) remains a bond under that bijection. Additional ternary sites are permitted, and any number of the six original boundary bits can have become shared.

The graph \(G\) has nine vertices, ordinary degree at least three and no triangles. Every nontrivial ordinary cut in \(G\) has at least three edges, and equality isolates a vertex of degree three. Indeed, the smaller side has at most four vertices. For two, three or four vertices its minimum degree sum is respectively six, nine or twelve, and the maximum number of internal edges is respectively one, two or four. All three lower bounds for its cut exceed three.

Every equation involving a later site has two or more later-born bits from its latest selected site. Its restriction to the shared ordinary edges of \(G\) is still the cut of its selected original vertices. Thus any such equation through an original ordinary edge has size at least five. Its appearance cannot change the whole row of readings through four at that edge. In the original presentation the only bonds through four are the seed and the seven four-place stars. This follows by the first-shrink estimate above, since the preceding construction uses no decreasing join. All eight stars have a pair of shared ordinary edges whose closed four-neighborhoods intersect exactly on the star, using a pair minimum of three at the seed. Indeed the other endpoints of the two edges are distinct, have the same color, and cannot share an edge. Thus the unchanged rows force all eight equations in a rival.

Let \(e_A,e_B,e_C\) denote the three ordinary inputs to \(t\). A new equation of size at most five through \(e_A\) must have exactly three crossings in \(G\) and exactly two later-born bits. It uses precisely one later site, because the latest two later sites would export at least three bits between them. Its original side is consequently one of \(\{A\},\{t\}\) or their complements in \(G\). Either complement exports at least four of the old six frontier bits. The one later site can consume at most three of them, in addition to exporting its own two, so either complement costs too much. On the side \(\{t\}\) the two outputs of \(t\) cannot both be used by one later site. On the side \(\{A\}\), its remaining output must go to the later site and every input to that site must come from \(A\). The sole possibility is a unary, two-output child of \(A\) on its original spare bit.

In the original presentation the only bonds of size at most five through \(e_A\) were the stars of \(A,t\). The same conclusions for \(e_B\) allow only its own possible unary child. Those two optional children have distinct fresh labels. Thus the intersection of the two closed five-neighborhoods is exactly the star of \(t\), and \(K(e_A,e_B)=5\). This forces the ninth star as a bond in every matched binary space.

Their sum is always the equation \(F\). Every member of \(F\) occurs in one of the forced bonds, so no singleton equation on any of these bits is possible. A two-bit equation would be visible in the pair table. A decomposition of \(F\) would therefore require two ternary bonds. In the source no two bits of \(F\) can ever belong to a ternary bond. All ternary bonds are single ternary stars, none of the original ternary star's bits belongs to \(F\), and any later ternary site takes just one input. Thus the forced equation \(F\) is minimal in the rival as well.

## All six bits can become ordinary nonbridges in one marked example

Use one of the six bits at each of \(U,A\) to make \(X\), and those at \(B,C\) to make \(Y\). Next make \(D\) on \(t,X\), then \(E\) on \(t,Y\), using both outputs of \(t\). All four additions are binary and make three fresh bits. Each original boundary bit is now shared on an ordinary four-cycle, since every binary join has an earlier witness for its two parents. In this width-33 presentation the old shore remains bounded by \(F\). To obtain a nonempty marked menu in the same ordinary part, append four more binary three-output sites, in the order

\[
             J:(D,E),\qquad M:(X,J),\qquad R:(D,E),\qquad N:(X,R).
\]

Here \(D,E\) have witness \(t\), and \(X,J\) and \(X,R\) each have witness \(D\). At width 45 the nine legal triples are precisely the single remaining bit of \(E\) together with one of the three outputs of each of \(M,N\). The shared witnesses of \(E,M\), \(E,N\), and \(M,N\) include \(J,R,X\), respectively, and no site meets all three. Every ordinary addition after the initial three sites takes two or more parents. From the fourth site onward the ordinary graph is two-vertex-connected, as the fourth site completes the initial square, and adding a site adjacent to two distinct earlier sites preserves this property.

The full pair table through \(K=6\) fixes the entire equation space against unrestricted reachable rivals at all three displayed widths. The following direct check only uses the named histories and finite bit sets. Enumerate all symmetric differences of the source stars, retain the minimal nonempty supports through size six and take their pair minima, pooling greater values. For each actual star of size \(d\), two bits have minimum exactly \(d\) with their closed \(d\)-neighborhood intersection equal to the star. Thus every source generator is forced. For each \(m=3,4,5,6\), list the \(m\)-element sets whose every pair has minimum at most \(m\). The entries below give the total number of such sets and the number outside the true equation space in parentheses.

|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 21 | 9 | 0 | 1 (0) | 7 (0) | 28 (24) | 1,398 (1,388) |

Every outside candidate \(W\) in every column has \(|W\mathbin{\triangle}S|<m\) for at least one word \(S\) in the true space. This last check can restrict to \(|S|<2m\). Thus no candidate can be a shortest extra rival word. Any strictly larger reachable rival space would have an extra generator of size at most six. Its shortest extra word would be a bond, since the unique ternary bond excludes a decomposition into two disjoint ternary bonds. This proves equality of spaces. Triple permissions then agree by allowed-state invariance. At widths 33 and 45, \(F\) cannot be an actual six-place site in any matching reachable rival. After wiring the exterior and merging parallel edges, the source is three-vertex-connected. Deleting the exterior and an ordinary site uses the ordinary two-vertex-connectivity just shown. Deleting two ordinary sites uses the two-site removal theorem. In this graph every augmented vertex star is a bond whose deletion leaves a connected cycle matroid. In any connected alternative graph with the same labeled cycles, each such bond must also be a star. Otherwise both connected sides would retain an edge, with no surviving cycle meeting both sides. The graph rank determines the vertex count, so these are exactly the rival augmented stars. The six-bit cut separating the original nine sites and the later sites is none of them. Moreover two different loose holders each carry a parallel pair, so the exterior is uniquely identified as the common endpoint of those two parallel classes. All actual sites are therefore determined in these interior examples. This marked conclusion persists at arbitrarily large widths. For example, attach further sites to the 45-bit arrangement through one unused output at \(J\), allowing an arbitrary mixed branch whose sole ordinary connection to the displayed part is that bit. The bounded separator theorem recovers this bridge, all ensuing bridge partitions and the marked part from the readings through six. After cutting, the measured table on that part is the original restricted table, including its retained boundary bit. Every corresponding rival part still has star generators of size at most six, which is all the finite extra-word screen used. Its locally wired cycle sets and its stars therefore agree with the 45-bit part, and no outside site can witness a pair of different holders within that part. Thus the nine original marked choices retain their verdicts under every clipped isometry. A global six-place site with support \(F\) would persist in the retained part, since none of its six bits is a bridge. Its exclusion persists as well.
