## Proof audit and fixes

**A and B hold.** In [Lemma A, lines 54–60](../proof.md#L54), the three cases are mutually exclusive by core rank and vector equality. Matching shortening with lift permits exchanging the names of the two nodes. In S3, $U$ ranges over the eligible common parents. Pure nodes can have several. Rank-zero parents of a pure rank-one node are the original knots, and none holds a pendant base. The chain totals of a pairwise-sharing family consequently have spread at most one. At one total, a pair with equal core and different vectors forces every further member to have that core. The equal-vector alternative follows the same way. In [Lemma B](../proof.md#L68), a non-pure member meeting two pure members must use its unique pure parent to meet both. Two different such pure parents in the same group force the two non-pure members to have the same chain and a common lower core parent holding its base. That is exactly the configuration excluded by N1. A pure group thus stays maximal, and a pure uncovered pair stays uncovered.

**1. Block on pair maximality in C.** The statement says “Then P(y) pairwise shares, has no common parent when it has three members or more, is a maximal family that pairwise shares, and, when it has two members, lies in no group.” Restrict maximality to three or more. The sentence “By Lemma A, w has the chain total of the shortenings or of the lifts” also omits a possible lower total. [Lemma C, lines 83–84 and 92–93](../proof.md#L83)

One in-class witness has round-one pairs $01,02,03$ and one chained offshoot at each of knots 1 and 2. Let $D$ be the rank-4 core holding $0,1,2$ and both bases, and $J$ its rank-5 child also holding 3. No parent of $D$ holds both bases. The two parents of $(D,e+h)$ are $(D,e),(D,h)$. The pure $J$ shares $(D,0)$ with both. The pair is uncovered and extends to a pairwise-sharing triple. Its extra member has chain total zero, one below both shortenings. Both laws agree with the corrected reading for all 77 births through round 7. [Checked witness](witness.json)

For the general repair, a neighbor one total below the shortenings must have the form $(D',v)$, with $D'$ a child of $D$. For every $e$ in the support of $b$, the difference $b-v$ must be two units in total and must include $e$. Three different active chains are impossible. With two, $e,h$, this forces $v=b-e-h$. The extra node then has the same parent $(D,v)$ as the pair. If lifts are present the lower total cannot meet a lift. The upper total in the absence of lifts would have to supply an eligible lift itself.

For maximality with several extra members, any adjacent pair with different vectors has the following possible lower and upper nodes, with $T$ a core parent of $C$ in the second row.

| Pair | Unique shared parent $p$ | Unique proposed child $y$ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $(C,c+e),(C,c+h)$, $e\ne h$ | $(C,c)$ | $(C,c+e+h)$ |

S1–S3 put every common neighbor either among the children of $p$ or in $P(y)$. Only the displayed pair belongs to both. For the first row, at the same total this is the unit-move calculation below, and at either other total S2 forces the indicated core and vector relation. In the second row the lower-total neighbors either have core $C$ and are shortenings, or have vector $c$ and a core that is a child of $T$. The higher-total neighbors either have core $T$ and shorten to $p$, or have vector $c+e$ and are lifts. Comparing vectors rules out a meeting between members exclusive to opposite families except possibly $(D,c),(S,c+e)$ with $D\ne C,S\ne T$. Such a meeting would make $C,D$ common children of $T,S$. The lifts on $T,S$ already share a core parent holding the base of $e$, contrary to N1. Thus every larger pairwise-sharing family containing the pair lies wholly on one side. N2 makes all the lifts in $P(y)$ meet. With three or more members the unique lower witness of the chosen pair cannot parent the third. This proves the group assertion. With two, every possible enlargement retains the pair's common parent. That proves uncoveredness.

**2. Block on D under its advertised hypotheses.** “Under N1 and N3, every group of round-t extended nodes with a non-pure member, and every uncovered pair with one, is P(y) for exactly one y of round t + 1.” Add N2. The 13-knot witness meets both stated hypotheses. Each of its two ordinary three-parent groups contains both shortenings, which force the putative child to be $(U,e+h)$. The conclusion says “What is new is that the counting survives group cells (sets of births that are not connected sets), on a class read off the start, and that the obstruction is one family.” The uniqueness claim needs the same restriction. The failed-lift example gives a second cause, without chained twins. [Lemma D, lines 108–109](../proof.md#L108) · [Discussion and conclusion](../proof.md#L203)

With N2 restored, the two-total case works. If two upper vectors differ, their common core is fixed, their lower member's vector is their unique common shortening, and that core with that vector parents the whole family. This case has at least three members. With one upper vector and two lower cores, either one upper core gives a common parent, or two upper cores and the two lower cores violate N1. The surviving family has one lower core and one upper vector, determining $y$. Its membership in $P(y)$, corrected C, and maximality or uncoveredness give equality.

At a single positive total and one core, fix vectors $c+e,c+h$ with $e\ne h$. Any third distinct vector is $c+g$ or $c+e+h-j$, with $g,j$ outside $\{e,h\}$. A member of the first kind and one of the second differ by more than one unit move. Thus the whole family consists of extensions of one common parent or shortenings of one child. The latter child is recovered by the componentwise maximum of two shortenings. At one positive vector and several cores, a maximal group projects to a maximal family for N3. Any eligible extra core would give an extra extended node at that vector, and extending a group preserves absence of a common parent. ## What is still open about N3

A failure of N3 needs a family with an ordinary common parent that misses some required base. If there is no ordinary common parent, extending the family to a maximal ordinary pairwise-sharing family already produces a common child. This gives a sharper test on a core that satisfies N1 and the singleton cases of N2. For each individual base, test triples whose pairs share a parent holding that base. Each such triple must have a common parent holding the base or a common child.

To see why the triple test suffices, fix a base and a pair in a pairwise-sharing family for that base. The pair has a unique common child by the core rule and N1. Its eligible common parent is also unique. Two such parents would meet below on the base by singleton N2, violating N1. Every third member must therefore have this parent or belong to this child. If one member misses the parent and another misses the child, those two members and one member of the original pair violate the triple test, by the same uniqueness. Hence the whole family has a common eligible parent or a common child. For a nonempty set of bases, choose one of them. If the family has no common child, the singleton test gives a common parent. Every pair's witness holding the full set must be that same parent. Conversely, a failing singleton triple extends to a failing maximal family. This reduction still requires checking the triples.

I also tested removing chain bases that participate in chained twins while preserving the positive-rank core. A new ordinary pair to a private live knot replaces each removed chain offshoot. Five starts with known N3 failures had a chained-twin witness at every base. A second weighting drew 1,200 designs with four to nine group cells and one to three marked private live knots, using seed 2026112703 plus the draw index. Of these, 768 finished, 188 excluded every remaining base choice at a checked rank, and 244 were **not run** to a decision.
