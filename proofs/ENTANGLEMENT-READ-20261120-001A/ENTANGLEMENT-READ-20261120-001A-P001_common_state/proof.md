# What separated readings can share

**Standard statement.** At every finite stage of the two Boolean growth rules, every new allowed assignment restricts to an old one and every old assignment extends. Two independently set binary readings of one weighted stage have a joint distribution for all four potential answers. For uniform affine states with two selectors embodied in the state, invariance of the two entire local registers under the other selector is necessary and sufficient for a shared-randomness model of the full register tables.

**Nearest known result.** The two-setting Bell bound and the obstruction to a common distribution from contextual tables are classical. The earlier program study PROBABILITY-001E supplied a four-context table, and FUZZY-EMERGENT-LOGIC-20261007-001P changed the setting preparations and updated the responding system. Here we identify the state-extension property of ordinary growth, separate passive conditioning from independently set readings, and give an explicit coupling for uniform affine sources. The argument below is self-contained. The mathematical comparisons are background. [CHSH, 1969](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevLett.23.880) · [Fine, 1982](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevLett.48.291) · [Abramsky and Brandenburger, 2011](https://arxiv.org/abs/1102.0264)

## 1. A step preserves the old allowed states

Let Ω be the nonempty set of complete assignments to the distinctions of a finite, lawful presentation. Take a funded complete round of the ordinary rule with its allocation fixed, using either even parity throughout or unsigned not-all-equal throughout. The grouping, available loose distinctions, fresh counts and the tests for implication use the presentation and its allowed relation. They do not select a particular member of Ω. A funded birth spends distinct old loose distinctions and adds a constraint on them and its new private distinctions. A zero-fresh birth is permitted only when its constraint is already true in every old state. Otherwise it receives at least one fresh distinction. These are the parts of the ordinary rule the proof uses. [Ordinary operator](../../../research/ORDINARY-DYNAMICS-20261120-001/end_operator.md) · [Not-all-equal growth](../../../research/NAE-GROWTH-20261119-001/nae_growth.md)

**Extension lemma.** Forgetting the fresh distinctions maps the new allowed set onto Ω. Old constraints are retained, so restriction lands in Ω. Fix any old state. A zero-fresh constraint is satisfied. For a parity birth with \(f\ge1\) fresh bits, assign \(f-1\) arbitrarily and determine the last from the parity equation. For a not-all-equal birth the old inputs are nonempty. If two already differ, take any fresh values. If all agree, choose one fresh value opposite to them. Each birth's fresh distinctions are its own, so all these completions coexist. This proves surjectivity and, by composition, the same result through every finite sequence of performed rounds. A stall contributes no new round. A reached closed presentation still has its allowed states.

The two laws differ on the *number* of lifts. A parity birth has exactly \(2^{f-1}\) per old state. For a not-all-equal birth the count is \(2^f\) when the old inputs already differ and \(2^f-1\) when they agree. The counts multiply over private births. Thus uniform weighting of a grown parity stage projects to the uniform old weighting. Uniform weighting of a grown not-all-equal stage can favor old states with more extensions. For example, attach one fresh not-all-equal distinction to a pair. On an old relation with the four pair values equally weighted, the numbers of extensions of 00, 01, 10, 11 are 1, 2, 2, 1. Equal old weights can instead be transported by splitting each old weight across its own extensions, as in weighting L. Weighting T biases one marked distinction of the completed state. Each prescription still supplies one common distribution if it is fixed before choosing the external indices. Existential support alone specifies none of them.

An exhausting parity birth can depend on distinctions beyond a fixed number of local steps. The proof already grants each reader the complete state, its fixed presentation, and any information computed jointly from them. The step bound on separation and even the absence of a chain therefore do not affect the following obstruction. Mixing preparations, schedules or measurement-independent random seeds gives the same conclusion if the same mixture is used at every setting and all four local readings are defined on it. No statement about physical signal propagation follows from a static joint distribution.

## 2. Independently chosen settings have a common explanation

Write \(A_0(w),A_1(w),B_0(w),B_1(w)\) for signs in \(\{-1,+1\}\) on the same state \(w\). In reading I they are signs of two marked distinctions per side. In reading II they report whether the marked pair equals 00 or 11. Choosing \(x,y\) selects \(A_x,B_y\), leaving the state, weights, retained trials and each other reader's rule fixed. With any one nonnegative normalized weighting \(\mu\), the distribution of the quadruple under \(\mu\) gives a common explanation because its two-variable marginals reproduce the observed tables. In particular

\[
\sum_b P(a,b\mid x,y)=\sum_{w:A_x(w)=a}\mu(w),
\qquad
\sum_a P(a,b\mid x,y)=\sum_{w:B_y(w)=b}\mu(w).
\]

Both are independent of the other index. These equalities are mathematical no-signalling for the exposed outputs.

For every single quadruple,

\[
A_0(B_0+B_1)+A_1(B_0-B_1)\in\{-2,2\}.
\]

Exactly one of the two parentheses vanishes. Averaging gives \(|E_{00}+E_{01}+E_{10}-E_{11}|\le2\). In any orientation with an odd number of minus signs, the four signed products in a single state have product −1, so their sum is again ±2. These arguments permit arbitrary structural dependence between the two registers. They also permit factorizable stochastic local readings given a common source, since one can pre-sample a response for each local index conditional on that source and project the resulting quadruple. The bound concerns exact expectations. The same common histogram enforces it exactly for a finite census.

## 3. What a common relation guarantees

For any finite family of readings on a single nonempty allowed set, let \(v(w)\) be the list of all prescribed answers. Each observed support on a context \(C\) is the exact projection of \(v(Ω)\). Every \(w\) gives a single assignment meeting all these projected supports. Moreover **every individually supported local event** extends to such an assignment by choosing any preimage of the event. Thus exact projections exclude both absence of all global assignments and a supported event with no extension. The same proof handles support after weighting by replacing Ω by the states of positive weight. A finite positive witness count and existential projection have the same support. Signed cancellation is a different semantics. If it ultimately supplies one nonnegative law on simultaneous answers, §2 still applies to that law.

**A selection that fails inside actual knots.** Take a central ternary knot on \((a,b,c)\) and three more on \((i,p_i,q_i)\) for \(i=a,b,c\). Use either even parity on all four or not-all-equal on all four. Each displayed support has the full ternary law on projection. A central state extends to the three outer knots independently, and every outer state can be completed at the central knot. Each lawful ternary projection satisfies K1–K3 under forgetting. On each outer knot choose its event \((i,p_i,q_i)=(1,1,0)\). The outer supports are disjoint, each event extends, and even any two extend by setting the remaining central bit to 0. Choosing all three forces 111 at the central knot, forbidden under both laws. The full presentation has global states with central restriction 000 under parity or 001 under not-all-equal. This collection of individually chosen pieces illustrates exactly what can fail inside a single structure.

**The literature's GHZ and Mermin–Peres requirements.** In the GHZ sign convention used here the four triples XXX, XYY, YXY, YYX have targets \((+,-,-,-)\), with a separate X and Y at each of three readers. In the Mermin–Peres square the three row products are positive and the three column products are \((+,+,-)\). Each required triple is individually possible, each has four allowed assignments, and their uniform local distributions agree on every overlap. In each system every observable occurs exactly twice. Multiplication of all required equations would give \(+1=-1\). Neither signed family can be all the exact projections of one ordinary finite allowed system, under any growth size or local marking. A single parity knot does supply the individual hiding of a constraint from every pair, as lesson 8.5 describes. [Mermin, 1990](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevLett.65.3373)

The numbers and repairs follow from the same argument over bits. Two contexts are joined for this count when they share an observable. Every observable occurs in exactly two contexts and all contexts communicate by such overlaps. The only linear dependence among the parity equations is their total sum, since in any vanishing sum the two contexts of each shared observable must have equal coefficients. With \(n\) observables and \(m\) contexts, deleting any one context or reversing any one target sign therefore gives \(2^{n-m+1}\) common assignments. The counts are 8 for GHZ and 16 for the square. In each repaired system all four allowed tuples of every retained context occur. After fixing such a tuple, removing its context leaves the other contexts connected by shared observables. Their remaining parity targets have even total and can be solved, since the remaining incidence matrix has all even-total right-hand sides in its image. Equal fiber sizes give uniform local weights on the four tuples. Dropping a context gives the same assignments as enforcing its repaired sign.

**Weights can fail with freely extendable supports.** Mix the fair table supported on \(a\mathbin\oplus b=xy\) with uniform noise of positive weight \(\eta\). Every pair in every context then has positive probability, and all 16 prospective sign assignments meet the observed supports. Both one-party marginals are uniform. Yet its CHSH score is \(4(1-\eta)>2\) for \(0<\eta<1/2\). Here only the probability tables prevent a common explanation. Mere overlap agreement, even for weighted single outcomes, therefore gives less information than a common weighting. Section 5 constructs the fair table.

## 4. When the settings are also distinctions

Embodied selectors are bits \(X,Y\) of the state. With positive cell masses the conditional experiment uses \(\mu(\cdot\mid X=x,Y=y)\). Independent external settings on the same ensemble use \(\mu\) in every cell. Appending independent random setting registers to a prepared source is harmless. Conditioning on recorded settings inside a constrained state can change the distribution of its potential answers, even if the two selectors are fair and mutually independent. In particular every conditional table whatsoever admits one probability distribution of **actual** tuples \((X,Y,a,b)\), by multiplying the four tables by any positive setting probabilities summing to one. Only a common distribution of the four **potential** answers gives the conclusion of §2.

**Affine-register theorem.** Let the complete state be uniform on a nonempty finite affine space over the two-element field. Let \(X,Y\) be affine selectors with all four pairs attainable, and let each side of the experiment expose a finite register of fixed affine functions of the state. The full conditional distributions of the registers admit a common random source with a response at each side depending only on its own selector **if and only if** each entire register has the same distribution at fixed own selector for either other selector. This includes a zero-dimensional residual space and arbitrary, possibly nonlinear, local functions of the registers, such as reading II.

*Proof.* Identify the affine state space with \((x,y,t)\), where \(t\) ranges over the kernel of the selector map. Such coordinates exist by full rank on the two selectors. Under uniform weighting \(t\) is uniform on that same vector space in every cell. The full registers are

\[
U=c+\alpha x+\beta y+L_A t,\qquad
V=d+\delta x+\epsilon y+L_B t,
\]

with addition modulo two and fixed linear maps \(L_A,L_B\). At fixed \(x\), the two laws of \(U\) are uniform on two translates of \(\operatorname{im}L_A\) differing by \(\beta\). They agree exactly when \(\beta\in\operatorname{im}L_A\). Similarly the two laws of \(V\) at fixed \(y\) agree exactly when \(\delta\in\operatorname{im}L_B\). Choose \(v,u\) in the residual space with \(L_Av=\beta\) and \(L_Bu=\delta\). In each cell take \(t=s+ux+vy\) for a single uniform source \(s\) independent of the selectors. This translation preserves the full joint conditional law, while giving

\[
U_x=c+(\alpha+L_Au)x+L_As,\qquad
V_y=d+(\epsilon+L_Bv)y+L_Bs.
\]

All four potential registers now coexist as functions of \(s\). Any local read of its own register and index inherits the same explanation. Necessity follows by marginalizing any such local-source representation. When the residual space is zero the hypotheses force \(\beta=\delta=0\), so the same construction applies.

A direct check of the affine single-mark reading also locates where an excess would have to arise. On a cell, \(E_{ij}\) is zero unless the residual linear part of \(A_i\) equals that of \(B_j\). Equality gives correlation ±1. Any excess requires at least three nonzero correlations, so the three corresponding coefficient equalities force equality of all four residual parts, and all four products become deterministic. Now \(A_0\oplus A_1\) is deterministic given the selectors. Hiding the other selector in Alice's full register forces the \(y\) coefficient of this difference to vanish. Similarly the \(x\) coefficient of \(B_0\oplus B_1\) vanishes. The xor of the four selected product exponents over their four cells is the sum of exactly these two coefficients. Hence the four correlations have product +1 and every odd-minus CHSH sum has magnitude 2. This calculation also covers constant or repeated marks as functions, even if their names are distinct.

The full-register hypothesis matters for reading I. For uniform independent bits \(x,y,t\), take the bit registers \((A_0,A_1)=(t,t+y)\) and \((B_0,B_1)=(t,t)\). Selecting by \(x,y\) gives \(a=t+xy, b=t\). Both selected single-bit distributions are uniform. The difference \(A_0+A_1=y\) reveals the other selector in Alice's full pair.

**Singleton-pattern corollary.** For reading II on the same uniform affine source, hiding the remote selector in both *selected answers* already implies the CHSH bound. If both full registers hide it, the coupling just proved suffices. Otherwise consider a register that leaks the remote selector. Its two conditional supports at each fixed own index are distinct translates of the same subspace, hence disjoint. The one accepted word for that index (00 or 11) can occur in at most one of them. Equality of the two yes probabilities forces both to be zero. This works at both own indices because the subspace and the remote shift are fixed. That reader therefore always answers no. With the other reader's selected-bit distribution fixed across the remote selector, the constant answer and the two other-reader marginals have a common explanation. Reading only a single mark accepts multiple words of its full register and need not have this property.

Both results use uniform affine states and affine register contents. A later local ask can be nonlinear. An arbitrary weighting of an affine support is outside the theorem.

## 5. One changed setting rule, and what supplies it

Start with a fair shared bit \(R\) and two equal outputs at all four setting pairs. Keep the preparation fixed. Only at 11 flip Bob's output with probability \(t\), using an independent bit \(C\) of weight \(P(C=1)=t\). The supplied joint rule is \(a=R,\ b=R\oplus xyC\). The four correlations are \((1,1,1,1-2t)\), so \(S=2+2t\). Both exposed output marginals remain fair. The unchanged rule gives 2. At \(t=1\) the score reaches 4 and exceeds the quantum upper bound \(2\sqrt2\). The full equal/opposite table is the literature's Popescu–Rohrlich example. [Popescu and Rohrlich, 1994](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF02058098) · [Cirel'son, 1980](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF00417500)

Zero altered setting rules leave the original score unchanged. Altering a ±1 product on probability mass \(t\) in just one cell can move its expectation by at most \(2t\), attained above. This is the stated least change in number of setting rules and the sharp magnitude change at that mass. The construction supplies access to the pair of settings and a hidden fair bit with a chosen weighting. With \(P(R=1)=p\), Bob's probability of 1 at \(y=1\) changes from \(p\) at \(x=0\) to \(p+t(1-2p)\) at \(x=1\). For \(p=0,t=1\), Bob reads Alice's setting there directly. Alice stays independent of Bob's setting throughout. Even with the fair bit, exposing both \(R\) and Bob's output would disclose the flip. Marginal no-signalling describes the declared exposed outputs.

The full four tables even rule out a quantum realization for every \(t>0\), including small values whose score lies below \(2\sqrt2\). For any bipartite quantum state \(\rho\) and local binary observables, form \(u_i=(A_i\otimes I)\sqrt\rho\) and \(v_j=(I\otimes B_j)\sqrt\rho\). Their Hilbert–Schmidt norms are at most one, and their real inner products equal the correlations. In particular the absolute CHSH score is at most \(\|v_0+v_1\|+\|v_0-v_1\|\le2\sqrt2\) by Cauchy–Schwarz and the parallelogram identity. The first three correlations being 1 forces \(u_0=v_0=v_1=u_1\) with norm one. The fourth must then also be 1.

**A single distribution of actual states can give the same obstruction.** The following is an analytic alternative to the added joint flip. Put even parity on two disjoint four-place knots \((X,A_0,A_1,P)\), \((Y,B_0,B_1,Q)\). Choosing the first three bits on either side fixes its fourth. Before a weighting is supplied there are 16 complete states at each pair of embodied selectors. Give weight \(k\) to a state when \(A_X\oplus B_Y=XY\) and weight 1 otherwise, for \(k\ge1\). Each setting cell has eight states of either kind. Fixing either complete marked output pair leaves two states of each kind in that cell. Thus the selectors are mutually independent and fair, and both full local pairs remain uniform under either other selector. The embodied correlation is \((-1)^{xy}r\) with \(r=(k-1)/(k+1)\), giving \(S=4r>2\) exactly for \(k>3\). Giving zero weight to failures gives \(S=4\). Using independent external selectors on this *same* weighted source gives correlations \((-1)^{ij}r/4\), since only embodied cell \((i,j)\) correlates that pair of prospective answers. Its external score is \(r\). The new weighting compares the two realized answers to **both** realized selectors and is absent from the ordinary U, L and T prescriptions.

For a concrete quantum-compatible table, set \(k=5\). Each outcome pair satisfying the setting parity has probability \(5/12\). Each of the other two pairs has probability \(1/12\). Both single outcomes are fair. The four correlations are \((2/3,2/3,2/3,-2/3)\) and \(S=8/3\). Here is a realization of the whole table. In the quantum comparison take \(\lvert\Phi^+\rangle=(\lvert00\rangle+\lvert11\rangle)/\sqrt2\), Alice's observables \(Z,X\), and Bob's \((Z+X)/\sqrt2,(Z-X)/\sqrt2\), using the Pauli matrices. On this state the \(ZZ\) and \(XX\) expectations are 1, both mixed expectations are zero and the single outcomes are fair. In the mixture \(v\lvert\Phi^+\rangle\langle\Phi^+\rvert+(1-v)I/4\), every correlator is scaled by \(v\). Taking \(v=2\sqrt2/3\) gives exactly the stated four distributions. More generally the whole family with \(0\le r\le1/\sqrt2\) is realized by \(v=\sqrt2r\). A larger \(r\) exceeds the quantum bound. This is an agreement of tables for the supplied weighting and the specified quantum measurements. The ordinary growth rules specify neither the weighting nor these measurement operations.

The distinction for the owner's premise is narrow. Global rules of logic can retain information invisible to each part. A Bell violation also requires the setting tables to defeat a common explanation for the prospective answers. Independent readings of these grown states always have that explanation. Conditional readings and explicitly added joint rules can remove it in the precise ways above. No choice of physical measurement law, quantum state or signal speed has been derived here.

## Evidence and scope

The uniform statements above are symbolic proofs. The companion checker enumerates finite controls, reconstructs local supports, exercises the canonical knot test and checks the quantum-compatible example. For the ternary four-knot example, the numbers of full states extending any fixed selection of zero, one, two and three outer events are respectively \((32,8,2,0)\) for parity and \((162,27,3,0)\) for not-all-equal.

For two bits in each output register, every uniform affine conditional law is specified by a subspace \(H\subseteq\mathbb F_2^4\) and three translates modulo \(H\), for the base value and the two selector shifts. Every such choice is realized by a uniform value in \(H\) plus those three terms, so enumerating the subspaces and representatives covers every distinct conditional law, at any residual dimension. There are 14,137. Exactly 3,025 pass whole-register invariance. For each the checker reconstructs all four joint distributions from a local model with one common source. Of the 2,304 laws yielding CHSH excess under reading I, 256 hide the remote selector in both selected bits. Of the 2,280 excesses under reading II, zero do. An independently written check enumerated the 65,536 affine parameterizations with one residual bit. Of 4,096 reading-I excesses, 2,048 passed both selected-bit tests. None of 4,800 reading-II excesses did. The two counts use different named populations and agree on their common range. Neither population imposes the growth study's separation and bond conditions.

The frozen empirical placements are a different evidence family. Finite parity growth under the uniform stage weighting satisfies the affine theorem whenever its two affine selectors have all four cells. A nonuniform tilt or a not-all-equal state relation can require a different argument for embodied settings. The general external-index proof covers both laws and all three weightings.
