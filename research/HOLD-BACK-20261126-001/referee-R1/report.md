## Fixes

1. **Block — the comparison with a constant shortfall at every join.** The README says “On these starts (no takes) a join is given two and an offshoot one, so it caps joins at two; it is short only where it is owed more than it was given.” The advisor’s answer says “C and E: every join with D ≥ 2 is short by exactly one.” Register entry A284 says “Chosen with the advisor as one form from each of two classes (a shortfall that grows with what is owed beyond what was given; a constant shortfall of one at every join).” The formulas in the code match the register **with the floor**. Let `p` count the distinctions given, `D` the demand and `L = max(3 − p, 1)` the floor. INHERIT gives `max(L, min(D, p))`. NET-OWING gives `max(L, D − p + 1)`. Every one of the 3,263 first-round offshoots was given one distinction and received two fresh ones under INHERIT. I inspected the common first-round birth inputs before applying either ration. Of 2,531 joins across the 533 preparations, 2,226 had two parents, 200 had three, and 105 had four. Demand ranged from zero to eight. The two formulas disagree on at least one birth in round 1 in **all 533** preparations.

   A join given four distinctions and owed four received four fresh distinctions under INHERIT and one under NET-OWING. The ration that holds back one at joins would give three. In all, 163 joins were fully funded under NET-OWING, 2,099 were short by one and 269 by more. The README’s claimed equivalence to that earlier ration and the restriction of the “keep one back” difference to offshoots or takes both fail. One-per-parent does equal INHERIT without takes. Keep the negatives for the two formulas. A comparison between the two advertised classes requires an actual constant-shortfall representative, subject to the floor. 2. **Block — a supplied fixed threshold is necessary.** The decision’s position table says “Late hearing from afar belongs to a supplied threshold, not to holding back as such”. The README says “A form that binds only above a number the knot reads from itself (as the cap binds above three) was not among these; it is what the result points to.” INHERIT already supplies an example. Its formula equals `min(max(D, L), max(p, L))`, so its effective ceiling is the birth-local `max(p, L)`. The tested formulas show that some hold-back rules lose the hearing. The four matched pairs also show that keeping an unchanged pair linked is insufficient.

   To check necessity, after seeing the four-pair results I used a ceiling of one plus the minimum number of binary distinctions needed to distinguish the requests. Its fresh count is `max(L, min(D, 1 + ⌈log₂(max(1, D))⌉))`. The ceiling is calculated from demand and uses no separate cap parameter. It agrees with the cap on **all 11,629 unchanged and 181,670 post-addition birth decisions** on the four cap histories. Since the allocation is the only rule changed, induction gives the same histories and the same 18 late hearings. At a two-parent join owed five, this rule gives four and the cap gives three. I also regrew all four cap-positive census pairs under this rule. They retained **6/42**, all with later births in both runs. Thirty allocations differed from the cap’s prescription on the new histories, all on additions without a hearing. Thus the data locate a consequential allocation at demand four. They leave open which rules can change an allocation on a hearing history and still preserve that hearing. The alternative rule is a post hoc counterexample to necessity, with no claim that it is preferred by the model.
