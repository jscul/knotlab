# What a paid value changes at the next knot

Under not-all-equal, changing just distinction 231 can change which fresh values knot 111 can hold and the distribution of a value it later pays. Under parity, changing 231 alone violates the old holder’s equation. A separately prepared change of two distinctions reverses the parity of the two values knot 111 later pays. In every lawful comparison it makes the same two payments to the same two children under the recorded allocation. The inherited distinction at R is the same 231 that H held, so its value is exactly \(x\). Reading that particular inherited mark uses the designated payment. For not-all-equal the sole excluded case is a constant five-bit holding. With \(c=d=k\), three fresh words are common and \(kk\) certifies \(x=1-k\). When \(x=k\), at least one fresh value must differ from \(k\). With \(c\ne d\), both inputs allow all four words. For parity, \(u\oplus v=c\oplus d\oplus x\), so the two fresh supports are disjoint for a fixed background. Each has two words. Within a fixed old word either individual fresh mark can have either value under both laws. In the parity table the two columns compare matched arms only in the compensated control.

Knowing \(c\oplus d\) suffices to decode the parity result from \(Y\). Knowing the common value \(k\) and that \(c=d=k\) licenses the not-all-equal inference from \(kk\). If only \(Y\) is supplied and \(c,d\) are unknown, each input admits all four fresh words across the listed backgrounds under either law. The table gives conditional comparisons without fixing the fresh output in advance.

Under the specified weighting, each allowed word in a table entry has probability the reciprocal of the entry’s size. This is a forward draw uniform on each newborn’s allowed fresh words, independent across their disjoint fresh banks given the older word. At \(c=d=k\) the not-all-equal probability that 263 will pay value \(k\) is \(1/3\) for \(x=k\) and \(1/2\) for \(x=1-k\). ## Why changing values cannot choose an ordinary payment

Consider a finite connected presentation in the domain of the ordinary complete-cut rule, with each distinction held by one or two knots. Fix the law. Two lawful valuations of the same holdings have the same live knots, loose marks and witnesses for groups. Required groups follow from these witnesses. A shortage at any knot stalls the entire cut in both valuations. With sufficient funding, the remaining loose marks each fund a unary child. The complete parent lists in turn determine the next demands. Each ordinary nonclosing child with \(p\) parents and demand \(D\) receives \(\max(D,3-p,1)\) fresh marks. These operations inspect the holdings alone. [Ordinary transition](../../../experiments/ORDINARY-DYNAMICS-20261120-001A/ORDINARY-DYNAMICS-20261120-001A-E001_ordinary/end_operator.py)

A start with no loose marks is already closed. At the connected parity law’s sole closing birth all loose distinctions are paid at once. Summing all old even-parity equations cancels the twice-held marks, so those loose distinctions already have even parity for **every** lawful old word. Every implied parity equation is a sum of old equations. By connectedness, any nonempty proper selection of holders separates two coholders and leaves their shared distinction in the sum. Hence only the sum over all holders can impose a nonempty equation entirely on loose distinctions. The cited ordinary theorem gives a fresh mark under not-all-equal on its nonempty funded inputs. Thus the zero-fresh check concerns implication by the full old relation. Even an old word that happens to satisfy a proposed relation cannot independently turn off the grant. [Closure and admissibility proof](../../../research/ORDINARY-DYNAMICS-20261120-001/end_operator.md)

For every complete funded allocation, all lawful old words extend as above, including through the implied closing birth. Couple two histories by the same allocation and fresh names. Their next holdings are identical, whatever lawful fresh values each takes. Induction gives equal structural histories at every further finite cut, including any stall or closure. When allocations are open, match every bijection of a parent’s loose marks to its children with itself. This gives equal **sets** of possible physical payments. An indefinitely growing structural history likewise lifts by choosing a lawful completion at each cut. A distribution over allocations would require a choice of weighting.

The earlier state-sensitive law obtains 29 versus 40 first births by adding an explicit veto. Its separate allocation control hands an occupied mark to a designated child. Both supply an operation that consults values. Ordinary growth here transmits a bit, can constrain both values later spent, and can change the chance of a particular value being spent under the declared weighting. Its own decision to spend, its physical payment options, its grants and its births all obey the proved structural invariant.
