# The cutoff on a ring and the cutoff on these sources

Both studies retain the numerical values of bond distances up to a cutoff and replace every larger value by the same symbol. The new minimum-width theorem applies to all finite histories in its source class and compares each with every reachable serial rival. Its cutoff is four, the largest ordinary site in the class has five distinctions, and a legal-versus-forbidden triple demonstrates the lower bound. The older serial theorem with a six-place site has an analogous sharp class-wide cutoff of five. Under the serial law every pair consisting of an input to a site and an output from that same site has bond distance equal to its arity minus one, even if the output has since acquired another holder. This explains one way the arity gets written into the pair table. The lemma alone gives neither a reader nor a necessary cutoff for every individual source. The other study starts from a ring of four four-place knots and one five-place knot and uses its own round rule. Its published reader recovered every junction at the selected rounds through 25. At round 30 the referee recovered all 1,960 junctions from the exact table through distance ten. The largest junction there has eleven distinctions. At distance nine the reader missed one junction. The checked implementation therefore uses a longer range when larger junctions appear on those rings. These comparisons concern that implementation on the reported rings. A lower bound for every possible reader would take a pair of reachable presentations or marked choices that agree at the shorter cutoff. ## A direct comparison on the new examples

We applied the published, unmodified incremental ring reader R3 to nine serial histories. We computed the short bonds on each history independently and supplied only the resulting pair table. For each input we used the original and reversed naming and eight deterministic shuffles. On eight histories the first cutoff giving every actual junction under all ten namings was the largest arity minus one. The ninth is the first minimum-width arrangement with two shrinking joins. At width 27 it has twelve junctions, with two five-place sites. At distance four, nine of the ten initial namings succeed. The source law and the full pair table permit just one set of ordinary junctions up to the supplied names by the theorem above. [Checker](ring_comparison.py) · [All nine comparisons](ring_comparison.json)

A single transposition shows how the incremental reader can go wrong there. Its candidate junctions include the five loose distinctions
\(\{21,22,24,25,26\}\). This is a true bond, the sum of all twelve site equations. Two actual junctions on \(\{10,16,19,21,22\}\) and \(\{14,18,23,25,26\}\) each have two bits in common with it. The reader sorts tied candidates by their supplied names and refuses any later candidate meeting an accepted one twice. With the original naming it accepts both ordinary sites. Exchanging 0 and 24 puts the exterior bond first. It then reports that bond as a junction and drops both ordinary sites. Undoing the exchange restores exactly the original measurements on all 351 pairs. Supplying every exact distance through the maximum of eight reproduces both outputs under the respective namings. The example is outside the original ring study's stated class. [Explicit swap and both returned answers](ring_comparison.json) · [Published implementation](../../../experiments/RING-READ-20261118-001B/RING-READ-20261118-001B-E001_incremental/reader3.py)

Why does a distance-only decision still exist? Treating the five loose distinctions as an ordinary junction would exchange the ordinary and exterior roles of two sites. In any reachable realization those two choices would give complementary lawful three-parent joins on the same six loose distinctions one step earlier. The closed transition check excludes that configuration. The growth law thus resolves exactly the tie that a label-ordered reader resolves inconsistently.
