## Words

A **description** is a list of definitions in the language of `dlang.py`: knots (K), calls of earlier definitions, and SELF, by
which a definition with three ports holds itself inside each of its knots, to a **depth** t. A **nested family** is a description
with SELF, read at every depth. Its **limit** is the infinite pattern with SELF repeated forever. **Dead**: allows no state (A72).
The **tie share** is the share of pairs of distinctions equal in every state or unequal in every state. The weighting gives a
description probability 2^−length and a pattern the sum over its descriptions (A79); it is one weighting of many (A71).

## The question

Under a weighting that makes patterns with short descriptions common (the owner's "simple realities are more common", true here by
definition), what is typical and what is rare: how often patterns die, against patterns of the same size under the uniform
weighting and with the same degrees, how much the law ties in those that live, and how nested patterns fare with depth and in the
limit? The language holds the owner's 17:26 picture: SELF puts a definition's pattern inside each of its knots with no layout, and
shared arguments let one distinction meet any number of knots. The knots-alone weightings test varies the law over periodic bases;
this one fixes the law and varies the finite pattern.

## The question in standard terms

For positive NAE-3-SAT instances generated by a straight-line hyperedge-replacement grammar under a prefix-free code, sampled with
probability 2^−length: the unsatisfiable fraction against uniform and degree-matched instances of the same size, backbone sizes,
and the satisfiability of iterated-substitution families at every depth and in the limit.

## Answer

Under a weighting by short descriptions, the typical pattern is one knot, alive, and simplicity is common by definition. What the
weighting changes is the rare, large patterns: almost all of them are nested, a knot holding the same pattern inside itself, and
they die far less often than patterns of their size under the uniform weighting (largely by the language's own structure, the
referee found: a random small definition seldom constrains its ports more than a knot does, and then it lives at every depth), when they die it is by a small contradiction, and
those that live are less tied. Reuse alone does nothing: only the nesting changes the outcome, so A71's challenge is answered here
(the outcome at a given size depends on the weighting, and only through self-substitution). Nested families nearly always live at
every depth; one in a hundred lives a while and dies, within nine levels; and a few die at every finite depth but live in the limit.

## Reading

**Interpretation, not a theorem.**
- **The owner's three fates, for logic fractals.** "Resolve to nothing" is rare (0.04%), "go for a while then die" is one in a
  hundred and never deeper than nine levels, and "go on forever" is the rule. The finite lattice of three-port relations is why:
  a fractal's fate is settled within a few levels, and after that it repeats.
- **Limits.** The families that die at every depth but live in the limit are the owner's "similar to limits" in exact form: each
  finite truncation fails only through its bottom knots, and the infinite pattern, which has no bottom, lives.
- **Why nesting protects.** Every described pattern has bounded treewidth (at most 11), so a contradiction must be local; a
  nested pattern repeats a few small definitions and needs one of them to be locally dead. Uniform patterns of the same size can
  die globally. That is the expected mechanism, stated before the run; the size of the effect is the finding.
- **Less tied.** The same bounded width that protects from death leaves more freedom: the law fixes fewer pairs. Short
  descriptions are not more rigid.
