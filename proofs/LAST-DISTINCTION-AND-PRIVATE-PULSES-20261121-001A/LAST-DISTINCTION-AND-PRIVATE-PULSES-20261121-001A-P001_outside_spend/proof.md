## What makes a last-slot witness conclusive

Use the recorded rule that recomputes its options after each birth. Two distinct live holders can be candidate parents in the same multi-parent group only if they meet a common physical holder. The rule takes maximal jointly unwitnessed pairwise candidate sets of at least three and the pairs still uncovered. It removes a group if an earlier child already includes all its parents. A unary is offered at a holder when its loose count strictly exceeds the number of the remaining groups containing it. Each selected birth takes one actually loose distinction at each parent. Counting the new holder’s current groups among the still-live holders, with the new one treated as provisionally live, gives demand $d$. A nonimplied child with $p$ parents receives $\max(1,3-p,d)$ fresh distinctions. Each old position becomes either permanently shared or remains as it was. A sufficient condition for permanent loss is as follows. Every realization of the complete target still needs an unmade child on one exact set of physical parents $P$. One of them, $D$, has just one loose distinction $x$. Both $P$ and a different birth $O$ are currently permitted. $O$ contains $D$, uses $x$, includes an identified outside contributor, and omits at least one parent in $P$. There is no prior child containing $P$. Taking $O$ exhausts $D$ forever. Since $O$ is itself not the required payment, the target cannot be built in any continuing history. The argument uses the precise unpaid obligation. Knowing that one future lineage might use $D$, without excluding an earlier or substitute payment, would not suffice. In the special case that $P$ is a unary there cannot be such a fork: one loose slot and any competing admitted multipart group using it exclude unary eligibility by the surplus rule. ## A boundary for the first outside birth

In the fully paid host-3 preparation the attached source holder $H$ meets only original 3. Before any child of $H$ is written that ordinary neighbourhood is fixed. A candidate group containing $H$ and a pure clock donor $D$ requires the two to share an ordinary witness, hence $D$ must physically meet original 3. Of the clock's source holders only 2 and 4 do. No positive-age clock role can meet original 3: if it did, original 3 would be one of its actual parents, making it part of its ancestry. The complete first marked return has only originals 0, 2, 4 and 6 in its ancestry. Later writing a descendant of $D$ cannot make an existing original its child.

Now suppose each earlier birth is one distinct true role of the first turn, in any sequence that is actually admitted. Each of originals 2 and 4 starts with three loose distinctions. Exactly two required source pairs ever take slots from each: at 2 these are $(0,2),(2,4)$, and at 4 they are $(2,4),(4,6)$. If any of those pairs remains unpaid, its donor still holds at least two slots. Consequently the *first* outside birth after such a prefix can touch an original clock donor but cannot take its last slot while a necessary child of that donor remains unpaid. ## Fair continuation and lawfulness

Each displayed nonclosing step takes a proper subset of the loose distinctions of a connected presentation. No nonzero parity equation then constrains the selected inputs; a parity sum supported entirely on loose distinctions includes either every holder or none. Under not-all-equal, check the old joint projection on the chosen input set. The inherited two-colouring argument applies when taking one input per parent leaves at least two other places on every old holder. A component in which an odd cycle of degree-two holders remains can choose its single duplicated colour opposite the fixed input there. Positive fresh supply extends each old allowed state, and full old input freedom supplies the intended new local projection. The replay separately checks the actual budgets and projections under the two laws.

After a chosen finite unrestricted prefix, number the possible fixed-parent actions by natural numbers and take the least available action. Every particular action spends finite positions at fixed parents and can occur only finitely often. A continuously enabled action cannot be suppressed indefinitely by finitely many smaller codes. The resulting maximal continuation is weakly fair on the raw pool. Applying the same argument to the joins-first pool proves weak fairness after priority on a prefix compatible with that priority. This proof of existence does not require any statistical weighting on choices and does not turn a prefix with a priority violation into a joins-first history.
