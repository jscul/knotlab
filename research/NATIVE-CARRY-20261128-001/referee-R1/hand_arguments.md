# Hand check of the four arguments

**1. The sign and a neighbouring exchange.** List each knot's distinctions together, in the proposed order of the knots. Cancel the two occurrences of each distinction by bringing them together. For a failing pair, one mate passes the other distinction an odd number of times exactly for the alternating orders `xyxy` and `yxyx`. For all other orders of the four holders the contribution is even. Thus the sign bit counts alternating failing pairs modulo two. In the literature's circular drawing these are precisely the crossing chord pairs. Distinct copies at a repeated meeting contribute separately. Count pairs, including at a multiple crossing.

Exchanging neighbouring knots *u* and *v* changes alternation exactly once when they hold different distinctions of a failing pair. Every other failing pair contributes zero change. The bit change is therefore the cross-pair count modulo two. Adjacent exchanges generate all orders. Conversely any specified *u* and *v* can be placed next to each other, so invariance in every order forces even coverage at every pair. A noncommuting declaration still has a sign *in a specified order*.

**2. Count at a pair.** Write $d_u=\sum_{w\in S}m_{uw}$. Choose a distinction from *u* to a knot other than *v* and one from *v* to a knot other than *u*. The product $(d_u-m_{uv})(d_v-m_{uv})$ counts each assignment once. The two other holders coincide at *w* in exactly $m_{uw}m_{vw}$ assignments. Subtract the sum over $w\ne u,v$. Every remaining assignment has four distinct holders and is counted once at this cross pair.

Reducing multiplicities modulo two preserves coverage **parity** and the sign at a fixed order. Each pair of disjoint meetings contributes the product of their multiplicities to the count when their four holders alternate. Existence of a cell is a separate condition. Two distinctions shared by *a* and *b* and one shared by *c* and *d* give two cells and an even minimal set. Erasing the doubled meeting gives zero cells. An odd example outside the stated multiplicity range is four mutually meeting knots with one meeting tripled. Its three opposite-pair multiplicity products are 3, 1 and 1. Every cross pair is covered evenly, the whole sign is odd, and its five cells differ from both reported kinds.

For odd minimal sets, reducing multiplicities gives a simple odd minimal set on the same knots. The odd sign supplies a surviving cell. Any proper qualifying subset in the reduction would retain its cell and even coverage before reduction. The reverse implication for minimality needs a cell-existence check. For multiplicities at most two, the small two-kind conclusion can be lifted as follows. Four mutually meeting knots with odd multiplicities then have one at every meeting. For two knots each meeting the same three others, every one of those six multiplicities is one. An extra shared distinction between the two meets every original distinction and is inert. A doubled meeting among the three gives a proper even set on the three and either one of the two. The reduced four-knot restriction has no cells, while the doubled meeting and the remaining single meeting supply cells. This excludes such a doubling by minimality. This argument uses the small classification of simple odd minimal sets, including its variant with an inert meeting.

**3. Fixtures.**

- On four knots there are three ways to divide the holders into disjoint pairs. In a simple pattern let their cell indicators be $p,q,r$. Even coverage says $p+q=q+r=r+p=0$ modulo two. A cell is required, so all three must occur. Four mutually meeting knots give exactly those three cells, with one alternating in every order. No proper subset has a cell.
- For the literature's K₂,₃, two knots each meet the same three others. The two on the first side have coverage 6, any two of the three have 2, and a pair from different sides has 2. There are six cells. Put the two first, then the three. There is one alternating pair for each choice of two of the three, giving sign $3\bmod 2=1$. No four of the five meet pairwise, so the set is minimal.
- The ring of four has two cells and adjacent knots have coverage one. In the ring of five with one additional knot meeting all five, adjacent ring knots have coverage $(3-1)^2-1=3$. The full pattern has 20 cells. There is no qualifying proper subset. No four meet pairwise. The five ring knots alone give coverage one at a neighbouring pair. For the additional knot and any four ring knots, the two interior knots of the remaining four-knot chain again give coverage three.
- A ring of three and a separate meeting have three cells. The only positive coverages are two. A doubled meeting and a separate meeting have two cells, again with positive coverages two. Putting the separate meeting last gives zero alternations for each fixture. Neither has a proper qualifying subset.
- For the literature's Petersen graph, use the two-element subsets of five labels as its ten knots. Two meet precisely when their labels are disjoint. Each has three neighbours. Meeting knots have no common neighbour, and nonmeeting knots have exactly one. Thus their respective coverages are 4 and 8. There are $\binom{15}{2}-10\binom{3}{2}=75$ cells. To compute one sign, use the equivalent listing $a_0,\ldots,a_4,b_0,\ldots,b_4$, with meetings $a_i a_{i+1}, b_i b_{i+2}, a_i b_i$ modulo five. The five $b$-meetings contribute 5 interleavings among themselves and the five $a_i b_i$ contribute 10 among themselves. Between the latter meetings and the $a$- or $b$-meetings there are 3 and 7. The total is 25.
- **Petersen minimality by hand.** Consider any qualifying restriction after dropping knots with no remaining meeting. It inherits the absence of rings of three or four. Two knots with a common neighbour are nonmeeting and have exactly one common neighbour. Even coverage then forces both to have odd degree. A degree-two knot can therefore meet only leaves. If one neighbour had another neighbour, the degree-two knot would end a two-step chain and would have odd degree. Its entire connected component would be a three-knot chain. Every nonempty component has an odd-degree knot (a degree-two knot forces leaves, and otherwise the degrees are one or three). Two components would have a pair of odd-degree knots with no shared neighbour, giving odd coverage. Hence there is just one component. A three-knot chain has no cell. In the remaining case all degrees are odd, so any two nonmeeting knots must have a common neighbour. If a leaf occurs, every other knot must meet its sole neighbour or be that neighbour. This is a star, since rings of three are absent, and has no cell. What remains is a component with degree three everywhere. All three original neighbours of every member belong to it. Connectedness of the original pattern forces all ten knots.

**4. The one-way obstruction.** An order with zero interleavings for *all* pairs gives zero for any chosen cells in that order. For a commuting choice the sign is then zero in every order. The literature's outerplanarity criterion supplies precisely such an order on a simple meeting pattern. Repeated copies can be read with the same holder order. In particular a non-outerplanar pattern can still lack any whole-choice set, as the ring-of-five closure shows. Detection of a forbidden minor does not classify minimal sets under deletion of knots. For example, the literature's subdivisions of K₂,₃ give arbitrarily large induced-minimal non-outerplanar graphs. Between the two ends there are three internally disjoint paths, each with at least two steps. Removing an internal vertex breaks one of the three paths and leaves just one cycle with paths attached. Removing an end leaves a tree. Both admit an outer order without crossings.

**What is selected.** Given a qualifying subset, declare its cells to fail and all other pairs to commute. This extends the algebraic choice to the finished closure. A pair involving an outside knot gets no contribution, and deleting distinctions with no failing partner preserves the sign. At birth, every failing pair must also have been eligible when decided. Further, for separated readers the two members of every failing pair must belong to the same reader, and no knot may hold two of that reader's distinctions. The cells of four mutually meeting knots form three disjoint pairs. The six distinctions of the two-against-three pattern are joined in one six-step ring of failing pairs, forcing all six into one reader while one knot holds three. It supplies an odd product of checks. This particular choice cannot be played by separated readers under the earlier rule.
