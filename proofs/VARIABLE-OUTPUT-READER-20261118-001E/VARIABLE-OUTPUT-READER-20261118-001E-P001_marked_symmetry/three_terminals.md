# Exchanging a piece at three vertices

## A three-edge star and a triangle give different full tables

In a reachable presentation the number of pairs at bond distance two is exactly three times the number of trivalent ordinary junctions. Every three-bit bond is the star of one such junction. Each supplies three pairs and two ordinary stars share at most one bit. [Short-cut proof](../../VARIABLE-OUTPUT-READER-20261118-001D/VARIABLE-OUTPUT-READER-20261118-001D-P001_separator_inverse/separator.md)

Consider the classical star–triangle exchange with terminals \(a,b,o\), where \(o\) is the fixed exterior. Replace a trivalent ordinary center and its three bits by the three pairwise terminal bits. The center disappears and the degrees of \(a,b\) each increase by one. The number of trivalent ordinary junctions drops by one plus the number of \(a,b\) that were formerly trivalent. Thus **no permutation of the entire edge set** can identify the two scalar tables if both presentations are reachable. With all three terminals ordinary the triangle itself violates ordinary bipartiteness.

There is a further restriction on simultaneous exchanges whose ordinary gadget vertices are pairwise disjoint. Every ordinary cycle is a binary sum of ordinary four-cycles, by replacing its latest site through the earlier common neighbor of its two parents on the cycle. Consequently every edge on an ordinary cycle lies on some four-cycle. A four-cycle through a disappearing center would use the two ordinary bits \(av,vb\) and an alternative two-bit path \(a,w,b\). Both bits of that alternative are fixed in a disjoint exchange. Inserting \(ab\) would produce an ordinary triangle. Hence each disappearing two-bit path consists of bridges. Apply the same argument in reverse to every new center. Replacing a two-bit ordinary path by one bit only suppresses its degree-two ordinary vertex, so an ordinary bridge corresponds on the other side to a path of two bridges. The scalar separator theorem recovers the bridge flags. Since a star here has two bridges and a triangle has one, even a balanced set of exchanges cannot have a scalar isometry that matches each gadget's three bits to its changed counterpart. Any unrestricted counterexample on this route would have to reassign distinctions across pieces.

## What cut data suffice for a general three-terminal replacement

Let two connected wired pieces \(A,B\) meet only in three distinct vertices. For a singleton terminal \(i\), place the other two terminals on the opposite side. For a specified set \(S\) of zero, one, or two bits of \(A\), let \(p_A(i,S)\) be the smallest size of a cut crossing \(S\) for which every component of either side contains a terminal. Let \(q_A(i,S)\) be the same minimum with the two terminals on their side connected in \(A\). Unattained minima are infinite. Use the same definitions in \(B\).

For this terminal partition and prescribed bits \(S_A,S_B\), the least cardinality of a full bond crossing the prescribed bits is

\[
\min\{q_A(i,S_A)+p_B(i,S_B),\quad p_A(i,S_A)+q_B(i,S_B)\}.
\]

Indeed the one-terminal side is connected as soon as both pieces satisfy the component condition. On the other side the two terminals must be connected within at least one piece. Conversely these conditions make both full sides connected, which makes their cut a bond. For a pair split between pieces, minimize the displayed quantity over the three singleton terminals. A full bond meeting both pieces cannot keep all terminals together, since its other side would be disconnected.

For a pair in \(A\) also allow the full bond with all terminals together. Its opposite side is a connected nonempty set inside \(A\). Every component on the terminal side of \(A\) must touch a terminal, and the intact \(B\) connects all those terminals. This gives a fourth candidate depending only on \(A\) and the pair. Thus matching these within-piece minima and all the displayed one- and two-mark terminal profiles is sufficient to replace a piece in every connected host under the same edge bijection. This condition is stronger than matching the three unmarked terminal cut values.

## A cyclic cut-and-reglue operation with the exterior on the boundary

Suppose a piece \(R\) has an order-three automorphism cycling its three terminals \(o,a,b\), with \(o\) the exterior. Keep the edge identities in both pieces and interchange the attachments at \(a,b\), fixing \(o\). Suppose both presentations are reachable and \(R\) has an interior vertex. **This natural correspondence preserves every legal triple**, regardless of whether it preserves the scalar table.

Every component of \(R-\{o,a,b\}\) meets the exterior by an edge. Removing the two ordinary sites \(a,b\) from the wired graph leaves all ordinary vertices connected to \(o\). Such a component can only exit at \(o\). Rotation then shows that every component touches all three terminals. [Two-site removal](../../VARIABLE-OUTPUT-READER-20261118-001D/VARIABLE-OUTPUT-READER-20261118-001D-P001_separator_inverse/inverse.md#two-elementary-source-restrictions)

The ordinary graph \(R-o\) is bipartite. By rotation, deleting any other terminal from \(R\) also gives a bipartite graph. For each terminal pair, paths through any interior component consequently have one parity, and rotation makes that parity the same for all three pairs. Color one interior component. All its neighbors of any one terminal have the same color, by bipartiteness after deleting another terminal. The three terminal-pair parities are therefore the three pairwise differences of three binary colors. They cannot all be odd and hence are all even. In particular \(a,b\) have the same ordinary color, no two terminals share a direct edge in \(R\), and every loose mark of \(R\) has an interior holder of the opposite color.

For marks confined to \(R\), the outside piece cannot witness a pair of their interior holders. For marks confined to the other piece, only the unordered terminal pair \(\{a,b\}\) can gain an interior witness from \(R\), and this pair is fixed by the exchange. In a mixed triple a holder at \(a\) or \(b\) has the wrong color to join a marked rotor holder. Otherwise all marked holders are interior. Suppose two holders are on one side and one is on the other. The two cross-pair witnesses must be the distinct ordinary terminals \(a,b\). The lone holder must meet both terminals. Each of the other two must meet just one, in opposite assignments, since a terminal meeting both would witness the whole triple. Those two holders must have an internal pair witness. These exact conditions are invariant when the attachments at \(a,b\) are exchanged. A shared mark stays shared throughout. This proves the assertion for the fixed-exterior correspondence.

If the exterior lies strictly outside \(R\), every marked loose bit belongs to its host. Purely ordinary terminals cannot be joined directly in \(R\), since their rotational orbit would form a triangle. A rotor interior witness can therefore affect only a subset of the three terminal holders. Rotational invariance already makes existence of a witness on a given pair the same for all three pairs, and the condition for the whole terminal triple is fixed under any permutation. This placement also preserves the marked verdict.

If \(o\) lies strictly inside \(R\), every loose bit belongs to \(R\). The rotation fixes \(o\). Indeed the latest ordinary site supplies a parallel pair, every parallel pair uses \(o\) by ordinary simplicity, and the common endpoints of all parallel pairs form a nonempty invariant set of size at most two. In \(R-o\) the three terminals either lie in distinct components or all in one. In the former case an ordinary six-cycle lies inside one singly attached component or in the host. A legal triple in the host would have to use all three terminal holders. Both possibilities survive any terminal permutation. In the latter case the rotation preserves both colors of the component, placing all terminals in the same bipartition class. There are no direct terminal edges. With zero or one terminal holder the host cannot witness a pair, and with three the host's pattern on the entire terminal set is just permuted. With two terminal holders and one interior holder, only the terminal pair could need a host witness. Either the rotor supplies every terminal pair by rotation, or it supplies none. In the latter case a legal six-cycle using a host witness would have exactly two host edges and four rotor edges. No four-cycle can cross the separation, since it would require a common rotor neighbor for a terminal pair. Sums of four-cycles wholly within single pieces have even incidence on the host alone, whereas the two-edge host path has two odd ends. This excludes the proposed six-cycle and finishes this placement.

The boundary argument assumed a rotor interior. With only the three boundary vertices, a nonempty cyclic rotor consists of the three direct edges. The edgewise reflection exchanges the two loose bits at the adjacent ordinary holders. Such a transposition is visible if either holder is trivalent, because its two other incident bits both have bond distance two from its loose bit and the other holder shares at most one of them. It is also visible if their shared ordinary bit is a bridge. Its two recovered components each contain another fixed bit, so exchanging just the two loose bits fails to preserve the partition. The nonbridge case at higher degree, matchings that move the exterior relative to rotor labels, and pieces without cyclic symmetry are outside the exclusions proved here.
