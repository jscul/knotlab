# Five two-output additions may be anywhere

**Theorem.** Let every addition of a finite mixed-output source supply three fresh distinctions except for at most five additions. Require each of those exceptions to use three parents and make two new distinctions. They may meet one another, use the same witnesses or occur in different parts. No restriction is imposed on the rival. Agreement of every current pair distance through five, under any one renaming, identifies every ordinary junction and the whole next menu. The same conclusion holds with arbitrarily many such exceptions when any two are at least three ordinary meetings apart, by the [separate short-shore argument](high_inverse.md).

It suffices to show that an ordinary shore with three or more sites has boundary size at least seven. This supplies the third hypothesis of the unrestricted-rival theorem. The only ternary site is the seed, and every four-place site is a single-parent, three-output site, supplying the first two.

Use the classical ordinary incidence graph. It is simple, bipartite, and contains no copy of the complete bipartite graph with three vertices on either side. In such a copy its last-born site would have to use all three opposite sites as parents. A different site on its own side would already be a common neighbor of all three, forbidding the join.

For a set of \(t=3,4,5,6,7\) sites the maximum numbers of internal meetings are consequently at most \(2,4,6,8,10\), respectively. Bipartiteness gives the first three. At six sites, a three-by-three split permits at most eight and a two-by-four split permits at most eight. At seven sites, a two-by-five split permits ten. In a three-by-four split at most two of the four sites meet all three across from them, giving at most \(2\cdot3+2\cdot2=10\). Other splits are smaller. Every site but the seed has degree at least four. For every such \(t\), the cut size is at least \(4t-1\) minus twice the corresponding number of internal meetings. This lower bound is seven in all five cases.

In a larger shore \(U\), consider its last seven sites. They come after the seed. Together they introduce at least \(21-5=16\) distinctions, at most ten of which can meet another site of \(U\). Any later use inside \(U\) would be at one of those seven. Thus at least six outputs cross the boundary. If the whole boundary had size at most six, all six would be these outputs, all five exceptions would be among the seven sites and no input to any site of \(U\) would enter from outside. Consequently \(U\) contains every parent of every site it includes, and following parents reaches the seed. Restricting the birth order to \(U\) still gives a lawful history. Indeed the first common neighbor of two sites exists when the younger is born, because adding any later first common neighbor would require them to have an earlier one. That witness is thus retained as a parent of the younger site. Restricting cannot introduce a common neighbor for three parents.

Let \(n_1,n_2\) count all single-parent and two-parent additions in this prefix and \(e\le5\) count its exceptions. Its number of loose boundary distinctions is exactly \(3+2n_1+n_2-e\). There is a three-parent join in the prefix since all five exceptions occur there. Even before its *first* three-parent join, \(2n_1+n_2\ge9\). To see this, its three parents and three different pair witnesses form an induced six-cycle. A further meeting among these six would give one witness common to all the parents. These cannot be the only six earlier sites. The latest site of that cycle would have had two earlier parents whose only other route in the six-cycle has length four, so it could not have joined them. If there were seven earlier sites, the seventh adds at most three ordinary meetings to the six-cycle by bipartiteness. Among the six earlier additions, all unary or binary, at most nine meetings in total forces at least three unary additions. If there were eight or more earlier sites, there were at least seven earlier additions; the first two must be unary because the seed and its first child have no common neighbor. Both counts give the stated bound. Later unary and binary additions only increase that count.

The boundary of \(U\) is thus at least \(3+9-5=7\), contrary to the six exports. This proves the short-shore condition and hence the theorem at every width. If a short shore ever defeats this proof in a history where only three-parent joins may supply two fresh distinctions, at least six such joins must lie **inside that shore**. Indeed all counts above can be restricted to \(U\). Six is a necessary count from the proof. Existence of a defeating six-exception shore remains open.

A two-parent join making two fresh distinctions changes another hypothesis of the theorem. At width 35 one such site makes an outside distinction close to all three outputs of a six-place junction, so no pair of its outputs alone isolates that junction. Twelve other pairs on the very same six-place junction each isolate it through distance five. Deducing forced parity equations from short entries certifies the entire equation space there and again at width 38, where the same configuration coexists with twelve legal triples. The certificate uses the actual table and permits unrestricted rivals. It explains why the failed output-pair argument is still compatible with a full distance reader.
