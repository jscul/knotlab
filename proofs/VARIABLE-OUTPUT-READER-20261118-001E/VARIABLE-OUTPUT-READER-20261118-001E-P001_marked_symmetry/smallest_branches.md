# A smallest opposing pair needs at most two attached ternary junctions

Consider two reachable full distance tables and an isometry carrying a legal triple in the first to a forbidden triple in the second. The presentations may coincide. Apply the published single-distinction separator test to both. The three marks pass the screen in the first, hence in the second under the isometry. Let \(C_1,C_2\) be their corresponding retained parts with all boundary distinctions left in place. Their complete matrices are isometric, and their local triple verdicts still differ. [All-size separator and pruning theorem](../../VARIABLE-OUTPUT-READER-20261118-001D/VARIABLE-OUTPUT-READER-20261118-001D-P001_separator_inverse/proof.md#which-branches-to-remove)

**Reduction.** There exist two reachable full presentations with the same opposing verdicts that consist of these parts plus at most two single ternary junctions attached by bridges on each side. If both local parts have a legal three-place start, no attachment is needed. More precisely the reduced common width is \(m+2j\), where \(m\) is the size of a retained part and \(0\le j\le 2\). It is at most the original width.

Use the given isometry to put both retained parts on one set of names. An original bridge that reaches one of the parts belongs to both that part's restricted table and the set of scalar-detectable separators. Its image is therefore a boundary distinction of the other retained part. It is loose in both local incidences and is never marked.

In an original growth history, sever every bridge and keep its bit as a loose bit at each end. A multiple-parent join never creates a bridge to a parent because any second parent and its common earlier neighbor give a four-cycle around that link. The initial site of a retained part away from the original seed has one incoming bridge and either two or three outputs, so local arity three or four. Every subsequent site has its original parents there. Any pair witness for two parents in the part is also in the part, since a discarded branch attaches through a single bridge. Thus restricted birth order gives a lawful local history with that initial arity. [Construction after cutting](../../VARIABLE-OUTPUT-READER-20261118-001D/VARIABLE-OUTPUT-READER-20261118-001D-P001_separator_inverse/inverse.md#components-retained-after-genuine-single-distinction-cuts)

If its local initial site has three distinctions, run this history starting at a genuine three-place seed. If it has four, remember the incoming boundary bit and put a single ternary junction on that bit and two additional distinctions. Start at this added ternary junction, then add the local initial site on the specified incoming bit and three fresh bits. Follow the remaining local history. Doing so recreates every junction of the retained part.

Choose the union of the remembered incoming bits from both sides, identifying them under the isometry. This gives at most two distinct anchors. On each side build its part by the preceding recipe, then put a single ternary junction on every other selected anchor using two fresh bits. Each is a legal unary attachment on a distinct locally loose bit. The complete incidence on each side now has exactly one ternary junction at each chosen anchor, independently of which one was first in its history. The original marked bits are untouched. No new junction can witness a pair of distinct old parents because each new junction has only one ordinary neighbor. The two triple verdicts persist. Attaching identical one-distinction parity pieces at corresponding anchors extends the complete scalar isometry by the exact one-bit attachment formula. In particular all entries between attachments match as well.

The original already had a distinct omitted branch containing at least two extra distinctions for each of these anchors on either side. Branches at different anchors cannot reconnect away from the part because their attachment bits are bridges. Thus replacement does not increase width. If the two histories require different four-place starts, the two attachments allow both orientations. The reduction returns a pair of presentations on isometric tables. If the starting isometry acted on a single physical presentation, the reduction can give two different presentations. A minimum specifically for a self-isometry would require that additional identification.
