# Short parity equations in an unrestricted rival

Use the classical graph representation of a presentation. Ordinary vertices are its junctions. A shared distinction joins its two holders, and a loose distinction joins its sole holder to the exterior. An equation is a support on which every allowed row has even parity. Equations form the cut space. A bond is a minimal nonempty cut, and \(K(x,y)=D(x,y)+1\) is its minimum size through two different distinctions. The ordinary shore of a bond is its side away from the exterior. Ordinary incidence is simple and bipartite. The junction stars are bonds and generate the cut space. [Cut representation and star criterion](../../VARIABLE-OUTPUT-READER-20261118-001D/VARIABLE-OUTPUT-READER-20261118-001D-P001_separator_inverse/separator.md)

## Recovering every equation through size four

**Theorem.** Suppose one reachable presentation \(H\) has no four-bit bond whose ordinary shore contains at least three junctions. Any other reachable presentation \(R\) with the same pair entries through \(K=4\) has precisely the same equations of size at most four, under the given identification of distinctions. The history, junction sizes, and causes of small bonds in \(R\) are unrestricted. This includes any source \(H\) whose joins always make at least as many distinctions as they take.

Every nonempty equation of size at most five is a bond. A nonminimal equation splits into disjoint bonds, each of size at least three. The three-bit bonds are exactly the trivalent stars, and their supports are read from the scalar matrix in every reachable presentation. Indeed, pairs with \(K=3\) meet at a trivalent site, and three pairwise incident distinctions in a simple triangle-free graph have a common endpoint. For this incidence argument one may give every loose bit its own temporary leaf instead of the exterior. The leaves play no role when computing cuts. [Ternary recognition](../../VARIABLE-OUTPUT-READER-20261118-001D/VARIABLE-OUTPUT-READER-20261118-001D-P001_separator_inverse/proof.md#what-the-scalars-can-certify-without-reconstructing-a-history)

By the shore hypothesis on \(H\), its four-bit bonds are four-place stars or the sums of two adjacent trivalent stars. The latter classification follows by adding the degrees of the two sites and subtracting two for their shared bit. In particular any pair with \(K\le4\) in \(H\) either meets at a site of arity three or four, or has a trivalent endpoint apiece joined by an ordinary distinction. This accounts for every pair involved below.

### A pair forcing each four-place star

Take a four-place site \(u\) in \(H\). It has either two inputs and two outputs or one input and three outputs. Two different outputs \(x,y\) of \(u\) always have \(K(x,y)=4\). They meet only at \(u\), so do not share a ternary equation. A competing four-bit bond \(W\) on \(x,y\) can use at most one bit from each ternary star. If it used all three, their sum would be a one-bit equation. With exactly two, subtraction gives a second ternary star and \(W\) would be the sum of two adjacent ternary stars known on both sides. Such a sum cannot contain \(x,y\). Their different child endpoints, when present, have the same ordinary color and cannot be adjacent.

All members of \(W\) must be pairwise at \(K\le4\) in \(H\). If one chosen output is loose, any other member must meet it at \(u\). The star at \(u\) is then forced. Suppose both outputs have children.

- If both children have arity at least four, a bit outside the star at \(u\) close to both outputs must meet both children. They have the same color, so there is no such bit.
- If both children are trivalent, write them \(a,b\). A bit outside the star cannot directly meet \(a\) or \(b\), by the preceding restriction on \(W\). It must meet a trivalent neighbor of \(a\) and a trivalent neighbor of \(b\). Those neighbors have the same color as \(u\). Hence they must be the same site. But \(a,b\) each had only its input from \(u\) at birth. Any other common neighbor was added later and used at least two parents, so has arity at least four.

For a three-output four-place site, a loose output or two children in the same one of these two classes always exist. It remains to handle a two-output site with one trivalent child \(a\) and one child \(v\) of higher arity. Any outside bit close to both outputs and admissible in \(W\) joins \(v\) to a trivalent neighbor \(b\) of \(a\). The site \(b\) was born unary on \(a\), since \(a\)'s only parent was \(u\). Then \(v\) was born after \(b\) and used both \(u,b\) as parents. There is at most one such \(b\). Two would make the three parents \(u,b_1,b_2\) all meet \(a\), precluding their join. The unique possible outside bit \(bv\) is too far from either of the two inputs \(ru\) of \(u\). It cannot meet \(ru\) at an endpoint. To have \(K(bv,ru)\le4\), \(r,b\) would have to be adjacent trivalent sites. The older \(r\) precedes \(u\), whereas \(b\)'s only earlier neighbor is \(a\). Thus no fourth member can accompany \(x,y,bv\). The only four-bit bond on the chosen pair is the whole star of \(u\).

This forces every original four-place star in \(R\). Every sum of adjacent ternary stars also belongs to \(R\). All equations of \(H\) of size at most four therefore survive.

There is a direct reader without site names or output directions. Read the ternary stars, form the four-bit sums of intersecting pairs of them, and keep those sums. For every pair with \(K=4\) absent from these sums, list its four-bit supersets whose pairs all have \(K\le4\) and which meet each ternary star in at most one bit. If the list has one member, keep it. That member must be the bond attaining the pair minimum. Under the theorem's hypothesis these operations find all four-place stars, since the distinguished pair just proved to exist in each star passes that test. The possible sums and the stars exhaust the four-bit equations of \(H\).

### Excluding an additional four-bit equation

Suppose \(W\) were a new four-bit equation in \(R\). It meets each ternary star in at most one bit, because two would express it as a sum of two ternary stars already in \(H\). It meets each original four-place star in at most two bits, since an overlap of three produces a two-bit equation and an overlap of four gives the star itself.

Every distinction of \(W\) must touch a trivalent site in \(H\). A distinction without a trivalent endpoint can be close to the other three only at its four-place endpoints. Even if it has two such endpoints, each can supply at most one other member of \(W\). Since every member of \(W\) touches a trivalent site, it has at most one four-place endpoint. Thus the pairs of \(W\) meeting at four-place sites form a matching, of size at most two.

The ordinary graph induced by trivalent sites is a forest. The latest site of any alleged cycle in it would have two earlier neighbors, while every trivalent site other than the seed was born unary. For each of the four bits of \(W\), its trivalent endpoints form a nonempty connected set in this forest, a singleton or the ends of the bit itself. The four sets are disjoint. Contract each to one vertex. The resulting forest has at most three direct links among the four vertices. Thus neighboring trivalent sites can account for at most three additional pairs of \(W\). This covers at most five of the required six pairs with \(K\le4\), a contradiction.

To verify the sufficient history condition in the theorem, the last three sites of a shore with at least three sites provide at least four outgoing distinctions. At size four the shore is parent-closed. Restricting the original history to its sites builds it as a lawful prefix, since an earlier pair witness is already present when the younger parent is born. In such a prefix the first two additions to the seed must be unary, since the seed and its first child have no common ordinary neighbor. Its loose count has already risen from three to at least five. If every join makes at least as many distinctions as it takes, that count never decreases. [Prefix and four-bit shore lemma](../../VARIABLE-OUTPUT-READER-20261118-001E/VARIABLE-OUTPUT-READER-20261118-001E-P001_marked_symmetry/four_bonds.md)

**A complete one-sided inverse.** If every site of \(H\) has arity at most four, its entire equation space is contained in that of every reachable scalar rival. In this case every join takes at most two parents, so the history condition holds. If at most one addition in \(H\) makes three fresh distinctions, the two spaces coincide and every triple verdict agrees in both directions. Indeed at width \(w\) with \(j\) junctions and \(s\) three-fresh additions, \(w=3+2(j-1)+s\). Every reachable space has rank at most \(1+\lfloor(w-3)/2\rfloor\). The original \(j\) stars are independent, since the latest selected site in any nonempty sum has a fresh output in its support. For \(s\le1\) this attains the upper bound. The allowed-state theorem then gives the actual menu. This corollary uses only the scalar readings through \(K=4\) and imposes no arity or fresh-count promise on the rival. ## What a five-bit bond through two eligible distinctions forces

**Theorem.** Fix three physically loose distinctions forming one legal triple, at holders \(a,b,c\). Let a five-bit bond contain the chosen bits of \(a,b\). If its connected ordinary shore \(U\) omits the third holder \(c\), the pairs \(a,c\) and \(b,c\) have unique common witnesses \(p,q\), both outside. There are a witness \(t\) of \(a,b\) in \(U\) and a site \(z\ne c\) outside meeting \(t,p,q\). The whole bond consists of the two marked loose bits and the three shared distinctions \(ap,bq,tz\). If the shore contains \(c\), it is instead a lawful parent-closed prefix with all three marks among five frontier bits. Its frontier has four distinct holders, with multiplicities \(2,1,1,1\).

Indeed \(a,b\) have the same color and are different. Thus their connected shore has at least three sites. Its latest three make at least six outputs, of which at most two can meet later sites of this shore. First suppose an incoming distinction is directed from an earlier outside site to a later inside one. It leaves room for only four outgoing ones. Equality puts every outgoing distinction at the latest three, all making two outputs, with two ordinary meetings among the three. Since both \(a,b\) own an outgoing marked bit, those sites are \(a,b,h\), where \(h\) meets both. The third marked holder \(c\) is outside, as otherwise it too would be one of the latest three, all on the same color side.

We use an elementary restriction on crossings in the ordinary graph. Every ordinary cycle is a binary sum of four-cycles. Remove the latest site on the cycle by replacing its two parent meetings with their earlier common witness. The resulting even set decomposes into cycles on older sites, giving induction. Take all crossings from \(U\) into the ordinary component of its complement containing \(c\). Link two such crossings if they share an endpoint, or if both their inside endpoints and their outside endpoints meet in pairs. This graph on crossings is connected. There are at most three ordinary crossings in total. Each four-cycle crosses the chosen component boundary zero or two times, and in the latter case its two crossings satisfy the stated link condition. If the graph on crossings were disconnected, a cycle would cross each link component evenly by the four-cycle span. Yet any two crossings can be joined into one cycle through paths inside \(U\) and inside the chosen outside component. Using one from each of two link components contradicts evenness. [Four-cycle span and cut argument](../../VARIABLE-OUTPUT-READER-20261118-001E/VARIABLE-OUTPUT-READER-20261118-001E-P001_marked_symmetry/terminal_cap.md)

Choose witnesses \(p,q\) for \(a,c\) and \(b,c\). They are different. If both were inside, one of \(pc,qc\) would be an outgoing crossing. Its inside endpoint would have to be \(h\), giving all three marked holders a common witness. Suppose instead \(p\) is outside and \(q\) inside. Since \(q\ne h\), \(qc\) is the unique incoming crossing. It does not link \(ap\), since \(a\) cannot meet \(q\). A third crossing \(xy\), directed outwards, would have to link both to make the crossing graph connected. If its inside endpoint \(x\) has the color of \(a\), then \(x=a\) or \(y=p\) to link \(ap\). Linking \(qc\) in this case requires \(x\) adjacent to \(q\), ruling out \(a\). The remaining possible outgoing holder is \(b\), which would meet \(p\), again a common witness. If \(x\) has the color of \(q\), its only possible outgoing holder is \(h\). To link \(qc\) then requires \(y=c\), so \(h\) would witness all three. The case with \(p,q\) reversed follows by exchanging \(a,b\).

It remains to exclude inside witnesses when the shore is parent-closed and \(c\) is outside. Here every crossing at an inside site is one of its outgoing distinctions. If both witnesses were inside, the four different holders \(a,b,p,q\) would supply the two marked bits and the two shared bits \(pc,qc\). The latest three sites of the shore provide at least four of its five boundary bits, so three of these four holders must be precisely those latest sites. Any three of these holders induce at most one meeting: the only meetings among the four are \(ap,bq\), since a cross-meeting would witness all three marks. The three latest sites must then export at least five bits, leaving none for the fourth holder.

If \(p\) were outside and \(q\) inside, the unlinked crossings \(ap,qc\) would again need a third crossing \(xy\) in the component of \(c\), with \(x\) inside. With the color orientation of \(qc\), it must have \(x=q\) or \(y=c\). Since \(x\) must meet \(a\), the former is impossible. The latter makes \(x\) another, inside witness for \(a,c\), which the preceding case excludes. With the color orientation of \(ap\), it must have \(x=a\) or \(y=p\). As \(x\) must meet \(q\), this forces \(y=p\) and \(x\ne a,b\). The four distinct outgoing holders are now \(a,b,q,x\). The only meetings among them are \(bq,qx\). Again the latest three must contain three of the four, and must induce at least two meetings to leave a boundary bit for the fourth. This forces those latest three to be \(b,q,x\), leaving the older \(a\) with *two* boundary bits, its mark and \(ap\). There is room for only one. This excludes the mixed case and its reverse.

Thus whenever \(c\) is outside, both \(p,q\) are outside. The crossings \(ap,bq\) do not link. A third crossing must link both. If oriented by color like \(ap\), it would have to meet each of those two edges, producing \(aq\) or \(bp\), which would witness all three. With the opposite color orientation, its endpoints must be adjacent to \(a,b\) on the inside and to \(p,q\) on the outside. Call them \(t,z\). Since \(t\) meets both \(a,b\), its meeting with \(z\) implies \(z\ne c\). The three crossings exhaust the ordinary boundary and force uniqueness of \(p,q\): any further two-step route from \(a\) or \(b\) to \(c\) would require a further crossing.

The result is attained at seven sites with two fresh distinctions at every addition. Starting at \(z\), add \(t\) on \(z\), add \(a,b\) separately on \(t\), then \(p\) on \(z,a\), \(q\) on \(z,b\), and \(c\) on \(p,q\). The first two pair joins use \(t\) as witness and the last uses \(z\). The shore \(\{a,t,b\}\) has the single incoming distinction \(zt\). Exactly the two loose bits at \(a,b\) and the three stated meetings leave the shore, and both sides are connected. Its three holders \(a,b,c\) have the required distinct witnesses and no common one.

Finally suppose the shore contains all three marked holders. The incoming-crossing case has already excluded this, so the shore is parent-closed. Restricting the original history to its sites builds it as a lawful prefix. Each pair keeps a witness on restriction: its first common neighbor already existed when the younger parent was born, since any later first witness would have required an earlier one. Removing sites cannot add a common witness. Hence all three marks are eligible on the prefix. At least one marked holder is older than its latest three sites. Otherwise those three would be on the same color and send six outputs across the boundary. Only one older output fits. Rename the older marked holder \(c\). Equality forces the latest three to be \(a,h,b\) with two outputs each and the two meetings \(ah,bh\). If \(h\) were the oldest of the three, it would spend both outputs on \(a,b\). The two unused bits after choosing the marks would then be held by \(a,b\), who meet \(h\) and could make a legal pair. That pair and the legal triple would be complementary choices covering the whole frontier, excluded by the two-sink theorem. In each remaining orientation precisely one of \(a,h,b\) has two frontier bits, the other two have one each, and the older \(c\) contributes the fifth. A five-bit frontier that contains a legal triple is attained in the predecessor's thirteen-site example.

**Observable consequence.** If a true marked triple has one pair with \(K=5\) and another with \(K>5\), the five-bit bond for the former pair necessarily has the seven-site pattern above. A bond containing all three marks would bound all three entries by five. If exactly two of the three pairs have \(K=5\), all three ordinary pair witnesses are unique, by applying the outside-shore conclusion twice. The converse question, determining which proposed marked triples are legal from these entries and the rest of the scalar table, remains open. ## A six-bit equation can surround arbitrarily many sites through a legal triple

Start with three unary children of the seed. In each following group add three sites, one on each pair of the previous three. Every site after the seed makes two fresh outputs. The first three pair joins have the seed as their witness. In every following step any pair from the preceding group shares the unique older site used by both. Each old holder supplies exactly two distinctions, one per child. Every group formed by the three pair joins has a legal triple using one loose bit at each of its sites. The three distinct older parents witness its three pairs, and a common witness would have had to be a parent of all three new sites.

The six loose distinctions at the last three sites form a bond around the whole ordinary graph. For two chosen bits at different terminal holders its size attains the pair minimum. Their two holders force four loose crossings. Bringing in the third holder gives its two loose crossings. Leaving it out gives two further crossings along the two separate two-step paths to it. Thus \(K=6\) on those pairs at every depth. Starting with the second group of pair joins, the three unique pair witnesses have no common ordinary neighbor themselves: the preceding triple was already legal, and each new site met just two of those witnesses. Therefore equality at six forces neither a small shore nor the additional site \(z\) in the five-bit theorem. Each member of this family nevertheless has its full parity space and its menu forced by the one-sided rank corollary above.

## Separate derivation for a terminal junction

**Theorem.** Let \(v\) be any current junction with \(p\) input distinctions and all \(h\) fresh distinctions still loose. Let \(f\) be one of these outputs. If a current legal triple has three holders different from \(v\), every one of its marks \(l\) satisfies
\[
                         K(l,f)>p+h.
\]
In comparison, every actual input \(e\) to \(v\) has \(K(e,f)\le p+h\) by its star. The hypothesis allows \(v\) to have consumed a different distinction at the holder of \(l\), or to have had no meeting with that holder.

Regard \(v\) as last. A site with all its outputs loose may be deferred because each pair using it as a witness already met through an earlier site. A different site cannot have subsequently become the first alternative common neighbor of all three of its parents, as such an addition would already have been forbidden. Let \(P\) be the \(p\) input bits as they stood in the prefix and \(F\) the \(h\) fresh ones. The three marked bits were loose in the prefix. Each pair of holders already had a witness there, and no site met all three. Write \(u,b,c\) for those holders, with \(l\) at \(u\). Take their distinct pair witnesses in the prefix. [Terminal reordering and prefix witnesses](../../VARIABLE-OUTPUT-READER-20261118-001E/VARIABLE-OUTPUT-READER-20261118-001E-P001_marked_symmetry/terminal_cap.md)

Assume a bond through \(f,l\) has size at most \(p+h\). Its equation must use the last star, so it has support \(F\cup(C\mathbin{\triangle}P)\), with \(C\) an ordinary-shore cut in the prefix. The shore \(U\) of this cut contains \(u\), since \(l\) is loose and does not belong to \(P\). Put \(j=|P\cap C|\). The assumed size gives \(|C|\le2j\). Follow the two separate two-step paths from \(u\) through its respective pair witnesses to \(b,c\), and then take the loose mark at each of those sites to the exterior. These two paths, the bit \(l\), and the \(j\) crossings on \(P\) are disjoint. Hence \(|C|\ge j+3\). Necessarily \(j=p=3\), \(|C|=6\), and all three holders of \(P\) lie in \(U\).

If both \(b,c\) lie in \(U\), their marks exhaust the two remaining crossings. There is no ordinary crossing. Thus \(U\) is the whole connected ordinary prefix, whose loose distinctions are exactly the two disjoint legal triples \(P\) and the marks. The complementary-frontier theorem precludes the marked triple from remaining eligible after the join on \(P\). If just one of \(b,c\) lies in \(U\), its mark leaves at most one ordinary crossing, whereas the ordinary six-cycle through all three holders crosses twice. [Complementary-cap theorem](../../VARIABLE-OUTPUT-READER-20261118-001C/VARIABLE-OUTPUT-READER-20261118-001C-P001_triple_inverse/complementary.md)

If both lie outside, the only two ordinary crossings are one on the two-step arm to \(b\) and one on the arm to \(c\). They are both incident to \(u\). To see this, the marked six-cycle crosses, so the four-cycle span supplies an ordinary four-cycle using the first crossing and hence also the second. If the four endpoints were distinct, their inside endpoints and their outside endpoints would each be adjacent. Two crossings taken at the far ends of the arms would have two same-color witnesses as their inside endpoints. Taking one near \(u\) and one at the far end would put one pair witness next to the remaining marked holder, giving a common witness of all three. The only possible shared endpoint on the two arms is \(u\).

Return to the completed wired graph. Delete the two ordinary sites \(u,v\). There is still a site in \(U\setminus\{u\}\), since the three distinct holders of \(P\) were in \(U\). No such site has a path to the exterior: the only ordinary crossings of the old cut were at \(u\), its three \(P\) bits now reach \(v\), and its only other loose crossing was \(l\), at \(u\). This contradicts the all-size result that after any two ordinary sites are deleted every remaining site is connected to the exterior. This last step removes the prior same-holder assumption. [Two-site deletion theorem](../../VARIABLE-OUTPUT-READER-20261118-001D/VARIABLE-OUTPUT-READER-20261118-001D-P001_separator_inverse/inverse.md#two-elementary-source-restrictions)

**Common-prefix consequence.** Start both sides at the same named ordinary presentation, then add one terminal junction on each side with the same parent count and the same fresh count. If their scalar tables agree under the identity on the prefix and a matching of the fresh outputs, all triples of old distinctions have the same verdict. Suppose an old triple were legal on the first side and forbidden on the second. If the second consumed a mark, its distance to a fresh output contradicts the theorem. Otherwise its three holders and pair witnesses persist. Only the second junction itself can be a new common witness, so that junction used one input from each marked holder. The first junction must have missed at least one of these holders, or it too would have prevented the triple. At that missed holder, substitute the input used by the second junction for the old mark. It is loose on the first side and has the same holder, so the triple with this substitution remains legal. Its scalar entry to a fresh output again gives a contradiction. Exchange the sides for the reverse implication. A general isometry may change the prefix or move a fresh output to an older bit. A legal triple containing an output at the terminal junction is also outside this consequence.
