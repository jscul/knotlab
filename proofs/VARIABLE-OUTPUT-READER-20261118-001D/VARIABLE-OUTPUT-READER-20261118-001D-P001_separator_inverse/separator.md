# The scalar test recovers every one-distinction separation

**Theorem.** On every finite presentation reachable under the three-distinction seed and the one-to-three-parent, two-or-three-fresh parity rule, delete any distinction `t`. Link two other distinctions when

\[
D(x,y)\ne D(x,t)+D(t,y)-1.
\]

The connected parts of these links are exactly the parts of the ordinary incidence left after deleting `t`. In particular the scalar pruning step keeps every legal three-parent choice at every size.

## The graph used in the proof

Use the classical graph description of the parity equations. An ordinary vertex is a junction, a shared bit joins its two ordinary holders, and all loose bits have a common additional endpoint called the outside. Ordinary adjacency never includes the outside. The ordinary graph is connected, simple and bipartite. Indeed each new vertex takes at most one bit from any parent, and all its parents are in the same bipartition class since they pairwise have a common ordinary neighbor. Orient every shared bit from its earlier to its later holder and every loose bit toward the outside. Every vertex has two or three outputs, including three at the seed. A nonseed has one to three inputs, and hence total degree three to six.

For a set \(U\) of ordinary vertices, summing their parity equations gives the cut \(\partial U\), with the outside always excluded from \(U\). These sums give every valid dual word. A bond is an inclusion-minimal nonempty such support. Every ordinary star (the whole set of bits incident to one ordinary vertex) is a bond. After removing that vertex each other vertex can follow an output to the outside, since at most one of its outputs can end at the removed vertex and birth order strictly increases. Thus each side of the star is connected, which makes its cut minimal. Every pair of bits lies in a bond. For a direct graph proof, subdivide two distinct bits. Some simple cycle goes through both subdivision vertices in the wired graph because removing any one ordinary vertex leaves it connected as just proved, and removing the outside leaves the connected ordinary graph. Subdivision preserves this property, including for parallel loose bits (the seed alone is immediate). In a finite graph with this property two vertices lie on a common cycle, by the two disjoint paths theorem. In the original cycle, removing the pair of bits splits its vertices into two nonempty paths. Grow these disjoint connected vertex sets to cover the wired graph, adding one adjacent unused vertex at a time. The resulting cut is a bond containing both bits. Alternatively one may use the common-circuit property of the binary matroid of dual supports.

Write \(c(x,y)=D(x,y)+1\) for the least bond size through the pair and fix \(t\). Set \(w(x)=c(x,t)-2\) for \(x\ne t\). The link test compares \(c(x,y)-2\) with \(w(x)+w(y)\). Call the connected parts of its links *components* in the proof. At a star of size \(d\), every pair satisfies \(c(x,y)-2\le d-2\). Thus if its members \(x,y\ne t\) lie in different components, then

\[
w(x)+w(y)\le d-2. \tag{1}
\]

All bonds have at least three bits, as also follows from the short-cut calculation below. In particular \(w(x)\ge1\). Every shortest bond through \(t,y\) lies wholly in the component of \(y\) after dropping \(t\). For any other \(z\) in this bond, \(c(y,z)-2\le w(y)<w(y)+w(z)\), giving a link.

## Which short cuts can reach a high-degree vertex

If \(U\) has one vertex its cut is a star. For two vertices the cut has size at least six when they are nonadjacent. For adjacent vertices the size is the sum of their degrees minus two. It equals four only for two adjacent trivalent vertices (degree three each). If \(|U|\ge3\), its last three vertices together introduce at least six outputs. At most two are consumed inside \(U\), since their later holders in \(U\) must be among the same three and there is no ordinary triangle. Thus at least four outputs leave \(U\). If exactly four outputs leave, all four come from these last three, each of which has two fresh outputs. If the entire cut has size four, it has no incoming bit. These observations apply to arbitrary cuts and hence to bonds. In particular a three-bit bond is always one trivalent ordinary star.

At a vertex \(v\) of degree at least five, no cut of size at most four contains both an input and an output of \(v\). A singleton of that size meets the star of \(v\) in at most one bit. Two adjacent trivalent vertices cannot both be ordinary neighbors of \(v\), by bipartiteness. For three or more selected vertices, all four boundary bits point out of the selected set. Two consecutive directed bits cannot both do so.

Suppose \(v\) has three fresh outputs and \(t\) is outside its star. **At most two outputs of \(v\) have \(c(t,y)\le4\).** A cut with three or more selected vertices and size at most four has every boundary bit born at a two-fresh vertex. For the smaller selections charge a qualifying output \(y\) to the selected ordinary endpoint \(u\) of \(t\). There are at most two possible \(u\). A singleton endpoint can cover only its one bit shared with \(v\). For a two-vertex selection both vertices are trivalent and adjacent. If \(y\) ends at \(u\), then \(v\) is the unique parent of \(u\). Otherwise \(y\) must end at the second trivalent vertex \(h\). Its unique parent is \(v\), so its meeting with \(u\) has direction \(h\to u\). Now \(h\) is the unique parent of \(u\). These cases allow at most one qualifying output per endpoint because its parent is either \(v\) or one particular child of \(v\). The seed has no parent and gives no extra case. A loose output of \(v\) cannot belong to either small selection unless \(v\) is selected. A degree-at-least-five vertex cannot occur in such a cut. This argument is used for stars of degree five or six.

## The only local form a false separation could take

Say a star is mixed if its bits other than \(t\) meet different components. If \(t\) lies in a star of degree at least four, none of its other bits has weight one because its pair with \(t\) could belong to no trivalent star. For degree four or five, inequality (1) then precludes mixing. For degree six, any bit opposite \(t\) in direction has weight at least three by the input/output result above, so it links every other surviving bit. At degree three, wherever \(t\) lies, every available pair links by (1). This settles all stars containing \(t\).

Now take \(t\) outside the star of \(v\). There is at most one bit of weight one on this star. It would have to meet a trivalent endpoint of \(t\), and \(v\) cannot be adjacent to both ordinary endpoints of \(t\) since those endpoints are adjacent to one another. Degree four follows from (1) because a bit of weight at least two links every other bit. At degree five all bits of weight at least two link each other. If any bit has weight at least three, it links the possible weight-one bit as well. A five-bit mixed star would consequently have a single exceptional bit of weight one, with all four others of weight two and in a common different component. It must have **three parents and two fresh outputs**, since three fresh outputs would include one of weight at least three by the preceding bound. A six-bit star always has three parents and three fresh outputs, so it too has a bit of weight at least three. This links every bit of weight at least two. If the star is mixed, its single exceptional bit again has weight one, and (1) bounds the other five weights by three.

Thus every possible mixed star has three parents, a sole exceptional bit \(x\) going to a trivalent ordinary endpoint \(u\) of \(t\), and all the remaining bits in a common different component. On the other bits \(y\), \(c(t,y)\le4\) for a five-bit star and \(c(t,y)\le5\) for a six-bit star. Each shortest bond through \(t,y\) omits \(x\), by the shortest-bond observation. We will exclude first the case where \(v\) is a parent of \(u\), then the case where \(u\) is a parent of \(v\).

## The exceptional bit cannot go from the mixed vertex to the trivalent one

Suppose \(x=v\to u\). The trivalent vertex \(u\) has \(x\) as its only input, so \(t\) is one of its outputs. Choose an input \(y=p\to v\), and choose a shortest bond \(\partial U\) through \(t,y\). It omits \(x\), putting \(u,v\) on the same side of the cut.

If \(v\in U\), then \(y\) enters \(U\). A singleton or adjacent pair containing \(v\) has cut size at least its degree, and a nonadjacent pair has still larger cut. For \(|U|\ge3\), four outgoing bits in addition to \(y\) already rule out the five-bit star, whose required bond size is at most four. For a six-bit star the required size is at most five. This would give exactly one incoming bit \(y\) and exactly four outgoing bits from the last three two-fresh vertices of \(U\). The three-fresh vertex \(v\) is earlier than those vertices. None of its three outputs can leave \(U\), so it has three distinct later children in \(U\). The parent \(p\) of \(y\) is outside \(U\). The other two parents of \(v\) are inside because \(y\) is the only incoming bit. Choose one of the latter and an earlier common ordinary neighbor of it and \(p\). This two-bit path crosses the cut before the birth of \(v\). Its crossing is different from \(y\) and cannot be one of the outgoing bits from the last three vertices of \(U\). This contradicts the assumed size.

If \(v,u\notin U\), then \(p\in U\), and \(t\) enters \(U\) from \(u\) to an ordinary vertex later than \(v\). A loose \(t\) is already impossible here. At the birth of \(v\) there are three pairwise edge-disjoint ordinary paths from \(p\) to \(v\), comprising the direct bit \(y\) and a three-bit path via each of its other two parents and the corresponding common neighbor with \(p\). These two witnesses must differ, since a single one would be common to all three parents. Bipartiteness and the distinct parents ensure the three paths share no bit. Each path has a crossing no later than \(v\). In addition \(t\) crosses, and the last vertex of \(U\), later than \(v\), contributes at least two outgoing bits. These six distinct bits rule out both required sizes. This excludes \(v\to u\) for every mixed star.

## The exceptional bit cannot go from the trivalent vertex to the mixed one

Suppose \(x=u\to v\). The two other parents of \(v\) each need a different earlier common neighbor with \(u\). Those witnesses must be the two other neighbors of \(u\), so \(t\) has an ordinary other end \(q\), and the remaining bit of \(u\) leads to an ordinary vertex \(r\). Both \(q\) and \(r\) are older than \(v\). Let \(z\) be the other parent witnessed by \(r\). It cannot also be adjacent to \(q\), since \(q\) already witnesses the pair involving the remaining parent and would witness all three. These sites predate \(v\), so their ordinary adjacencies were already present when \(v\) joined. It is in the same bipartition class as \(u\), so it cannot be adjacent to \(u\) either. Thus the star at \(z\) has only one component because any exceptional bit there would have to meet an endpoint of \(t\).

The star of \(r\) is not mixed either. The only possible exceptional bit there is its bit with \(u\), since \(r,q\) are in the same ordinary bipartition class. The direction \(r\to u\) has just been excluded for a mixed star at \(r\). In the direction \(u\to r\), a mixed star at \(r\) would require three parents including \(u\), and hence two distinct earlier witnesses among the other neighbors of \(u\). One of those would have to be \(v\), still unborn at \(r\). This rules out the second direction.

On the ordinary four-cycle \(u,r,z,v,u\), the bits \(uv\) and \(ur\) are in the same component by the trivalent star at \(u\). The stars at \(r\) and \(z\) carry this same component on through \(rz\) to \(zv\). Consequently \(uv\) lies with another bit of \(v\), contradicting its sole exceptional status. This excludes the last kind of mixed star.

## From stars to the physical partition and legal triples

Every surviving bit at any one junction is now in one scalar component. An ordinary incidence path avoiding \(t\) therefore stays in one scalar component. In the other direction, if removing \(t\) separates the ordinary incidence, its two sides are parity presentations meeting only at \(t\). Each side has no bond smaller than three, since any side-supported word is also a full dual word. Across the sides the one-distinction gluing formula gives exactly \(D(x,y)=D(x,t)+D(t,y)-1\). No scalar link can cross the physical cut. This proves equality of the partitions. [One-distinction formula](../../VARIABLE-OUTPUT-READER-20261118-001C/VARIABLE-OUTPUT-READER-20261118-001C-P001_triple_inverse/proof.md#how-one-attachment-changes-a-bond-distance)

An eligible triple consists of loose bits at three different parents. Its three different pair witnesses put those parents on one ordinary six-cycle. Deleting a single shared bit leaves them connected even if that bit belonged to the cycle, and a loose marked bit cannot be a physical separator. Hence every eligible triple survives pruning and has a well-defined retained part. This statement concerns the rejection and restriction steps. Distinguishing all surviving marked triples by their retained data is a further inverse problem.

## Checks and scope of the theorem

Two reachable ten-junction examples place a trivalent vertex among the three parents of a junction, then give that junction a trivalent child. At five and six bits respectively the examples have widths 22 and 23. For each one a direct cut enumeration checked all 1,024 dual supports, every removed distinction and the short-output bounds. For each of the three inputs of the three-parent junction, and each output of its trivalent child (one shared and one loose), a cut through that pair avoiding their shared bit has at least six members in the examples. [Direct cut enumeration](check_separator_lemmas.py)

General cut codes can hide an ordinary separation from the entire table. Take two full binary trees of depth two, join their roots by the tested bit \(t\), and join each of the eight bottom vertices twice to one outside vertex. The ordinary tree has fourteen vertices, each with wired degree three. For any pair of bits, the shortest ordinary vertex path between an ordinary holder of one and an ordinary holder of the other has at most six vertices, with both bits in its boundary. Such a path of \(k\) vertices has boundary size \(k+2\). Its complement is connected through the outside, since every remaining tree component contains a bottom vertex. Thus every pair belongs to a bond of size at most eight.

Split the outside vertex in two, giving each bottom vertex one bit to each copy. Designate one copy the outside. Every old cut is still available by placing the copies together. Any newly available cut separates the copies and has at least eight bits, one on each of the eight disjoint two-bit paths between the copies. Consequently each old bond of size at most eight remains minimal and no pair minimum decreases. All 406 entries of the 29-distinction table agree. In the first presentation deleting \(t\) splits the ordinary tree into two parts. In the second the new ordinary vertex joins both parts. The checker enumerates the 699 and 7,166 bonds and obtains two scalar parts, each with fourteen distinctions, on the identical tables. The added ordinary vertex has degree eight, beyond the maximum allowed by the growth rule. [Full-table comparison](check_separator_lemmas.py)
